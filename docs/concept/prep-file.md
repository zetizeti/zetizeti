# The prep sheet

*What to bring when you want to be questioned into an area you do not work in yet. What the file has to contain, and what the person who prepares it decides. 28 August 2026.*

Attach a prep sheet on the **begin an enquiry** screen, under the box where you would normally name your edge, and the conversation opens differently. Instead of asking about the edge you named, the stone walks six lines through the document you brought — what the field calls things, where it came from, what you need before you can start, what is hard here, what is already to hand, and what the field says about itself. Then it stops, and the enquiry is yours again, in an area you now have words for.

It still never tells you anything. You brought the document, and the document is what does the telling. Every prep question points at a passage of your own file and asks what you already make of it. A question that glossed a term for you is withheld before you read it, by the same guard that withholds an answer anywhere else in this tool.

---

## Three parts, three sittings

The prep runs in **three parts of two lines each**, and it is meant to be spread over three sittings.

**Or in one, and the sheet's name decides which.** 🔴 **A sheet whose filename contains *mentor* is walked straight through, in one sitting; every other sheet is walked in three parts.** You are not asked, because it is a fact about who the sheet is for rather than a preference. The parts exist to hold the gaps and the gaps exist to hold the things to go and do — somebody preparing themselves at one desk takes no gaps, and the two closing and two resuming turns would be a quarter of the sitting with neither able to say anything true: one asks what you will go and do, the other what happened while you were away.

⚠️ **So the name is doing work.** Renaming a file changes how it is walked. That is legible rather than clever — whoever writes a sheet also names it — but it is worth knowing before wondering why an arc came out shorter than expected. The name travels with the turn and is stored nowhere.

| | Lines of questioning | What happens after |
|---|---|---|
| **Part one** | what the field calls things · where it came from | a pause, and anything to try, then you leave |
| **Part two** | what you need first · what is hard here | a pause, and anything to try, then you leave |
| **Part three** | what is already to hand · what the field says about itself | prep ends; your own enquiry begins |

Each part ends by putting the task for the gap in front of you and asking one question about how *you* will meet it. Each later part opens by asking what actually happened: what you did, or what you did instead when you could not.

**Nothing is stored between sittings, so you carry the thread.** At the end of a part, save your transcript with the link that appears. When you come back, begin a new enquiry, attach the same sheet, and bring the saved `.md` back in. The conversation resumes where it stopped, because the transcript is what says how far it got. There is no account, no saved plan and no resume code. There is not going to be one either: this tool holds nothing, and three sittings is not a good enough reason to start.

If you keep going without leaving, nothing stops you. The next question asks what you did, and *nothing yet* is a perfectly good answer.

---

## Two documents

**The prep sheet** is the material: what the field is, in sentences that can be pointed at.

**The things to go and do**, if there are any, are a section of the sheet itself. There is no second upload: the separate slot was removed on 30 August 2026, because tasks and material are prepared by the same person for the same group and a second file to remember is a step to forget.

**They are pre-set, and the stone never writes one.** The closing turn points at what your sheet already said and asks one question about how you will meet it. Nothing here decides what is worth your week. Whoever prepared your material decided that.

If the sheet names nothing to try, the closing turn asks you to name something small yourself. That is thinner material and, arguably, a stronger commitment. **And in a one-sitting walk there are no closing turns at all**, so nothing is asked about a gap you are not taking.

---

## What the files have to be

A **PDF with a real text layer**, a **`.md`**, or a **`.txt`**. **25,000 characters** is the hard ceiling, roughly ten pages, and a file past it is refused; the length that actually works well is a good deal shorter, and is given at the end. All three are read in your browser and never uploaded. Only the extracted text travels, in the turn, just as a paste always has.

A **scan is refused**, and you are told so plainly. A PDF of photographed pages has no text in it. There would be nothing to question, and the conversation would go on asking well-formed questions about nothing at all.

**Write in sentences.** The sheet is split on sentence punctuation, and each piece is what a question can point at. A slide deck exported to PDF has bullet fragments and no full stops, so it arrives as one or two enormous pieces, all six lines point at the same place, and the prep goes flat. If your material is currently bullets, put full stops on them. That alone is usually enough.

---

## What the prep sheet should say

Six sections, one per line of questioning. Each is recognised by a different kind of sentence.

| Section | What makes it land |
|---|---|
| **What the field calls things** | Term-and-meaning lines: `Affordance — what a surface offers before you think about it`, `Latency: the delay between an action and its visible result`, `A heuristic is a rule that usually works`, or an acronym gloss like `large language model (LLM)`. Ten to thirty terms is plenty. |
| **Where it came from** | Dates, and the words that carry a history: *originally*, *grew out of*, *traditionally*, *emerged*, *inherited*, *since the*. |
| **What you need first** | Dependency words: *requires*, *assumes*, *builds on*, *depends on*, *a working knowledge of*, *before you can*. |
| **What is hard here** | *Unsolved*, *an open problem*, *years away*, *expensive*, *no one has yet*, *remains*, *unclear*. |
| **What is already to hand** | *Standard*, *off-the-shelf*, *widely available*, *mature*, *well understood*, *cheap*, *routine*. |
| **What the field says about itself** | The field's own promotional language, quoted as claims: *revolutionary*, *the future of*, *poised to*, *everyone is*, *a breakthrough*, *set to transform*. |

You do not need those exact words and you are not filling in a form. They are what sentences of each kind tend to contain, and they are what each line of questioning looks for.

## If you want things to go and do

**Put them in the sheet, as a short section of their own.** They are for the gaps between parts, so they only apply to a three-sitting walk; part three ends the prep and hands into your own enquiry, and an instruction issued on the way out of a conversation that is no longer about the document would be one too many.

```markdown
## Things you could try
Cut three test pieces in different materials and measure the kerf on each.
Find one person who already does this and ask what they wasted a week on.
```

Small enough to actually happen in the gap. A task nobody does makes the next part open on an apology.

⚠️ **This is the one section that fires by vocabulary rather than by heading**, so sentences elsewhere in the sheet carrying *make*, *build*, *builds on* or *experiment* are read as things to try too. Any sheet about making things has some. It costs the closing turn a little precision and issues nothing as an instruction.

---

## Two things that spoil a sheet

**Do not settle the last section for the reader.** If the sheet says *this is mostly overhyped*, the stone points at that sentence and the student reads your verdict off the page instead of forming one. Write the claims the field makes about itself, as claims, in the field's own voice — *proponents say it is poised to transform diagnosis* — and leave the weighing to the person being prepared. Whether something is worth doing is the judgement the prep exists to provoke. Neither the sheet nor the tool should turn up already holding it.

**A quiz is welcome; an answer key is not.** A list of things worth being able to answer works well. The stone can ask them back, and finding out you cannot answer one is the useful part. Include the answers and they enter the conversation's own material, so the tool is now working against a document that keeps handing it the thing it is forbidden to say. The rules still hold and the questions still get withheld, but every turn costs a wasted regeneration, and the sheet is fighting the tool.

---

## About asking another model

You can put any of these questions into another window and get a fluent answer back. Most people will try it once. There is no rule against it and this is not a warning, so here is what happens.

The questions are not about the field. They are about where *you* stand in it: what you already took a word to mean before you read this sheet, what you can already do and what you would have to go and learn, what is on your desk this week, what you personally would want to see before believing a claim, and what actually happened when you went and did the task. A model does not have that information and cannot get it. It will produce something plausible about somebody who does not exist, and the next question will be about the position that answer just claimed. Nothing is marked or scored, and nobody sees any of it, so there is nothing to be gained either.

Nobody is checking, and nobody has to: this is a property of the questions rather than a policy. A question with no *you* in it is withheld before it reaches you, because a question any model can answer is not what this tool is for.

## If a section is missing

Nothing fails. The format is advisory. A line of questioning that finds no passage of its own asks against the sheet's framing sentences and your own words instead, and the prep still runs to its end.

But you are **told at upload** which of the six the file did not reach. A thin sheet is therefore visible before the conversation starts. If the note says four of six, the conversation will be noticeably vaguer on the two it could not find, and adding a paragraph is usually a two-minute fix.

If it reports very few sentences, two or three for a whole file, that is almost never a thin field. It is the bullet-points problem above.

## A skeleton

```markdown
# Computational fabrication — a prep sheet

## What the field calls things
Toolpath — the route a cutting head takes through material, generated from a model.
Kerf: the width of material a cutting tool removes as it passes.
A jig is a fixture that holds work in a repeatable position.
Subtractive manufacturing means removing material from a solid block.
Computer numerical control (CNC) covers machines driven from coordinate files.

## Where it came from
The field grew out of numerically controlled machining developed for aerospace in the 1950s.
Desktop fabrication emerged much later, from the RepRap project after 2005.
It inherited a vocabulary from industrial machining that often sits badly in a design studio.

## What you need first
This assumes a working knowledge of 3D modelling and of how materials behave under load.
Most of it builds on ordinary workshop practice — you need to be able to measure accurately.
Before you can cut anything you need to be able to read a technical drawing.

## What is hard here
Tolerance stacking across an assembly remains difficult to predict without testing.
Cutting reliably in reclaimed material is an open problem; nobody has solved it generally.
Multi-material joining is years away from being routine, and is expensive when attempted.

## What is already to hand
Laser cutting flat sheet is standard, cheap, and widely available in shared workshops.
Off-the-shelf parametric tools are mature enough for most studio work.
Basic sheet joinery is well understood and freely documented.

## What the field says about itself
"Desktop fabrication is poised to transform how objects are made and distributed."
Everyone is talking about local, on-demand production replacing supply chains.
It is often called a breakthrough that will disrupt manufacturing entirely.
```

Around 800 to 3,000 words is comfortable, well under the ceiling. Below roughly a dozen sentences there is not enough for six lines of questioning to find anything distinct.

## If you are asking an AI assistant to write one

This section is the brief. Hand it over whole.

**You are writing a prep sheet for zetizeti's enquiry mode.** It is raw material for a questioning tool, not a tutorial, a summary or a briefing document. The tool points at individual sentences and asks a student where *they* stand on them. Judge the sheet by whether its sentences can be pointed at, not by whether it explains the field well.

**Produce the six sections above, in that order, with those headings.** The order is a dependency, and the tool walks it from the top: terms before history, history before prerequisites, and all of those before anything is weighed. It is also split into three sittings of two sections each, so a section that is much thinner than its partner makes a lopsided sitting. Do not reorder, do not merge, do not add a seventh.

**Write declarative sentences, one claim each, ending in a full stop.** Not bullet fragments. Every sentence is a potential anchor, and a fragment without punctuation joins itself to its neighbours and stops being one.

**In each section, use that section's characteristic vocabulary at least three or four times across different sentences.** That happens on its own when somebody writes properly about origins, or prerequisites, or what is unsolved. It is not a keyword quota. If a section contains none of its own kind of word, the tool will not find it and will fall back to generic framing.

**Do not evaluate.** No section may tell the reader what is good, worth doing, overhyped, or a waste of time, least of all the last one. Its job is to reproduce the claims the field makes about itself, without comment, so that a student can be asked what they think. A sheet that judges has taken the student's turn for them, and the tool cannot give it back.

**Do not include answers.** If you write questions, leave them unanswered.

**Write the sheet so it supplies material, never conclusions.** The tool will not ask the student what any of this means. It will ask what *they* make of it, what they can already do, what they would try first, what they would want to see. Your sentences are the things those questions point at. This is also why a sheet that argues a case is worse than one that states what the field holds: the argued sheet hands the student a position, and the student repeats it.

**Say only what is true.** These sheets go to students entering an area they cannot yet check, so an invented date, a fabricated open problem, or a plausible-sounding term nobody uses will be believed and repeated. Where you are not sure, write less. A short accurate sheet prepares somebody; a long confident one misleads them, and they will not find out for months.

**If you are asked for things to go and do, put them in the sheet as their own short section**, two of them, one for each gap, each doable in the days between sittings with what a student is likely to have. They are the only place in this whole arrangement where anything gets *told* to the student, so write them as plain instructions and keep them small. Do not write a third, and do not write any unless you were asked.

**Do not name zetizeti, the stone, or this document inside either file.** They are material about a field, not about a tool.
