# name.md — on "zetizeti"

A note on where the name comes from, and on a resemblance it was not built to have but turns
out to carry.

## The root that was chosen

**zeti** is clipped from *zetetic*, an English adjective meaning "proceeding by inquiry,
investigating." It descends from the Greek **ζητέω** (*zēteō*) — to seek, to search for, to
inquire, to ask after — and its derivative **ζητητικός** (*zētētikos*), "inclined to search."
The word names not a body of answers but an *activity*: the seeking itself.

There is a quieter inheritance in it. The ancient Sceptics — the Pyrrhonists — were sometimes
called the *Zetetics*, the seekers, because (as Sextus Empiricus records) they kept inquiring
where the dogmatists had stopped to assert. To be zetetic, in that lineage, is to go on asking
and to withhold the conclusion. The word carries, from its origin, a refusal to hand over the
answer. For a tool whose whole promise is *it won't answer; it sharpens*, this is not
decoration; it is the meaning sitting inside the root.

## The doubling

The name does not use *zeti* once but twice. The reasons given at the time were practical: the
doubling made the word catchy, globally sayable (ZEH-tee-ZEH-tee), and — not least — left the
`.com` free when the single word would not have been. It joins a familiar family of reduplicated
names: *bonbon*, *tuk-tuk*, *Chupa Chups*. The meaning, by design, lives more in the sound than
on the surface; one is not meant to decode *zetizeti* so much as to say it.

But reduplication is rarely only ornamental. Across languages it tends to mark intensity,
repetition, or a thing done again and again — which is exactly what inquiry is. To question is
not a single act but a returning. The doubled name enacts, in its shape, the recursion of asking.

## The resemblance it was not built to have

Said aloud, *zetizeti* falls very close to **neti neti** (Sanskrit नेति नेति) — *na iti na iti*,
"not this, not this." This was not a source of the name. It is a cousin discovered after the
fact. But it is an apt one, and worth keeping in view.

*Neti neti* is a mode of inquiry from the Upanishads — it appears in the mouth of Yājñavalkya in
the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad* — and it became central to Advaita Vedānta. It is the *via negativa*
of that tradition: confronted with the question of what the Self, or Brahman, ultimately *is*,
the inquirer does not supply a positive description. Every attribute that can be named is set
aside — *not this, not this* — until what cannot be negated is what remains. It arrives at
truth not by stating it but by clearing away everything it is not.

So two reduplicated names, from two traditions that never met, both name a discipline of
inquiry, and both share a single stubborn refusal: **neither will tell you the answer.** The
zetetic keeps questioning past the point where assertion is comfortable; the *neti neti* seeker
negates every answer offered until only the unnegatable is left standing. Each leaves the
arrival to the one who inquires.

## A difference worth holding, not dissolving

It would be easy, and slightly too neat, to fold the two into one. They are not the same motion.

The zetetic moves *toward*. Its characteristic gesture is the question — *what is it? what do you
mean? what follows from that?* — Socratic, interrogative, forward-leaning, seeking.

*Neti neti* moves *away*. Its gesture is negation — *not that, not that* — apophatic, subtractive,
a stripping-back. One asks its way in; the other clears its way out.

That they converge at all is the interesting thing. A questioning that presses forward and a
negation that strips back arrive at the same austere place: a refusal to deposit a conclusion in
the seeker's hands. The name *zetizeti*, reached through the Greek for the sound of it, finds an
unbidden double in the Sanskrit for the *sense* of it — East and West landing, independently, on
a reduplicated word for an inquiry that withholds its end.

The name need not resolve the difference between seeking and negating. It is perhaps enough that
it holds both, and that a design student saying it aloud might hear, faintly, two traditions
agreeing on one thing: the answer is not the gift. The asking is.
