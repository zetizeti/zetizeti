## entry: money-and-the-work
**discipline:** money-career
**vocabulary:** money, salary, pay, paid, lucrative, career, job, security, rich, wealth, freelance, rate, corporate, prestige, status, ambition, sellout, selling-out, meaning, purpose, vocation
**felt as:** it pays really well; i need the money; it's a stable job; the big firms pay more; i'd be selling out; i want a serious career; just chase the salary; is it worth doing only for the money; i can't afford to do meaningful work; the prestige matters.
**the_tension:** Two honest claims pull against each other. The first: money, security and a serious career are legitimate — designers must live, fair pay is not shameful, financial stability can be exactly what lets someone take creative risks, and the research that revisited the long-held "pay kills creativity" belief finds the strong version of it weakly supported (Gerhart & Fang), with recognition and fair, relational rewards sometimes *strengthening* the link between motivation and creative work (Fischer and colleagues). The second: orienting a career primarily around money and status can crowd out the intrinsic motivation that the most original work runs on — financial rewards relate negatively to intrinsic task motivation in new-product teams (Malek, Sarin & Haon), the crowding-out tends to be sharpest among the most expert and creative, and meaningful, autonomous work often outlasts pay as an engine of sustained motivation. The student's move is not to decide that money is good or bad, but to ask what the money and security are *for* in this choice — whether they serve work the student cares about, are themselves a thing worth choosing (stability, family, health, freedom), or have quietly become the only measure — and what choosing for the pay, or against it, would each cost.
**failure_modes:** choosing every step for the salary or the status until the work you actually wanted to do has quietly disappeared; mistaking a prestigious employer's name for meaningful work; telling yourself "the money will buy freedom later" when later keeps receding; romanticising precarity — refusing paid or commercial work as if being broke were a virtue, which is itself usually a privilege; treating the refusal of well-paid work as a badge of seriousness, then resenting the precarity it brings; assuming meaningful work cannot also pay, so never asking it to.
**questions_it_invites:** What is the money and security here *for* — and is the work you care about the measure of this choice, or is the security itself a thing worth choosing? · If the pay were ordinary, would you still want this — and if the work were dull but the pay set you free, would you take it?
**sources:** Gerhart & Fang, "Pay, Intrinsic Motivation, Extrinsic Motivation, Performance, and Creativity in the Workplace: Revisiting Long-Held Beliefs," *Annual Review of Organizational Psychology and Organizational Behavior* 2 (2015), pp. 489–521; Malek, Sarin & Haon, "Extrinsic Rewards, Intrinsic Motivation, and New Product Development Performance," *Journal of Product Innovation Management* 37(6) (2020), pp. 528–551; Fischer, Malycha & Schafmann, "The Influence of Intrinsic Motivation and Synergistic Extrinsic Motivators on Creativity and Innovation," *Frontiers in Psychology* 10 (2019), art. 137.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the motivation findings are accurately attributed and genuinely two-sided (Gerhart & Fang revisiting the "pay harms creativity" belief; Malek et al.'s negative finding on financial rewards and intrinsic motivation; Fischer et al. on synergistic rewards). Citations Pass-4 verified (24 May 2026). ⚠ **Pass-3 watch:** the project's own non-profit / anti-extractive orientation could tilt this toward an anti-money verdict — the money/security pole must keep real weight (it does: Gerhart & Fang, and the "romanticising precarity is a privilege" failure mode). No verdict intended; the student decides. **Three-pass (24 May 2026): Pass 2 AND Pass 3 both flagged a real anti-money tilt — the enabling-vs-replacing framing defined money as never "the work itself", the stripping question pointed only at money, and failure modes ran 3:1 against the money pole. Fixed: questions reframed so security is a legitimate end with its own answer-space and the purity-test made symmetric; meaning-side failure modes added to bring the ratio to parity.**

## entry: first-things-first
**discipline:** money-career
**vocabulary:** career, commercial, advertising, branding, consumer, client, corporate, responsibility, society, useful, talent, skills, citizen, ethics, default, market, sell, path
**felt as:** it's just a job; everyone goes into advertising or branding; that's where the work is; what is my talent even for; i should do something useful; but the useful work doesn't pay; it's the obvious career path; am i just serving consumption; i'll do good work later.
**the_tension:** Ken Garland's "First Things First" (1964, revived as "First Things First 2000" with Rick Poynor) charged that a disproportionate share of design talent is absorbed by the most lucrative and least necessary work — what the manifesto names the manufacture of want: advertising, branding, consumer persuasion — and called for turning more of that skill toward more useful ends; Heller and Vienne's *Citizen Designer* presses the same question of design responsibility inside a corporate, over-branded consumer culture. The counter is honest and must be held — Michael Bierut's "Ten Footnotes to a Manifesto" presses it directly against First Things First: commercial work is legitimate, it pays, it builds craft and reaches real scale; virtue does not adhere to the manifesto's "cultural-good versus commercial-suspect" split (there is thoughtful commercial work and exploitative cultural work); a designer who simply vacates advertising only cedes it to less reflective hands; and one who can freely refuse such work is usually already privileged. The manifesto, too, can moralise — casting one set of choices as virtuous and another as betrayal. The student's move is to ask what their skill is being claimed *for* here, whether that is a choice they have made or a default they have drifted into, and whether "it's just a job" is an honest account or a way of not looking at what the work serves.
**failure_modes:** drifting into the most lucrative or default path without ever asking what the skill is for; treating "First Things First" as a purity test that shames anyone doing commercial work; assuming useful work and paid work are simply opposites; performing social responsibility as a brand while the actual work serves the opposite.
**questions_it_invites:** What is your skill being claimed for here — and is that a choice you made, or a default of the field you are accepting? · Is "it's just a job" how you honestly hold this work — or, if you would refuse it, is that refusal a considered position or a borrowed virtue?
**sources:** Garland, "First Things First" (manifesto, 1964; reprinted in *The Guardian*, 24 January 1964, per Poynor); "First Things First 2000" with Poynor, "First Things First Revisited," *Emigre* 51 (1999); Heller & Vienne (eds.), *Citizen Designer: Perspectives on Design Responsibility* (Allworth Press, 2003; 2nd ed. 2018); and the counter-pole: Bierut, "Ten Footnotes to a Manifesto," in *Seventy-nine Short Essays on Design* (Princeton Architectural Press, 2007).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — First Things First (Garland 1964; FTF2000 / Poynor, *Emigre* 51, 1999) and *Citizen Designer* (Heller & Vienne, 2003/2018) are correctly attributed (Pass-4 verified, 24 May 2026). ⚠ **Pass-3 watch:** First Things First is an explicitly value-laden manifesto and this entry sits close to the project's own values — keep the commercial-work pole genuinely weighted (it is: the legitimacy of commercial work, refusal-as-privilege, the manifesto's own moralising risk). No verdict intended. **Three-pass (24 May 2026): Pass 2/3 flagged leading questions and a source asymmetry (the manifesto pole has three citations; the commercial-legitimacy pole has none). Fixed the questions — de-pejorated "drift", made the refusal itself examinable, and attributed "the manufacture of want" to the manifesto rather than the lens voice. Source asymmetry CLOSED (24 May 2026): Bierut's "Ten Footnotes to a Manifesto" — Pass-4 verified (real; *Seventy-nine Short Essays on Design*, Princeton Architectural Press, 2007; confirmed as a critical pushback on First Things First that defends commercial-design legitimacy) — is now cited in the_tension and sources, giving the commercial pole a named source against the manifesto's three.**

## entry: passion-or-paycheck
**discipline:** money-career
**vocabulary:** passion, love, dream, vocation, fulfilling, meaningful, calling, paycheck, just a job, stable, sacrifice, underpaid, devotion, follow
**felt as:** just follow your passion; do what you love and the money follows; find your dream job; i want work that's meaningful, not just a paycheck; i'd take less to do what i love; chase the calling; it's only a job if you don't love it; i should be willing to sacrifice for the work.
**the_tension:** "Do what you love" is the advice everyone gives — find the work you're passionate about and the rest follows. There is real truth in it: research on work passion finds it associated with lower burnout, stronger commitment and better wellbeing, and passionate workers are even treated more favourably and given more opportunity by others (the "Passionate Pygmalion" effect). But Tokumitsu and others argue the same ideology has a darker function — that "do what you love" can legitimise low pay, unpaid overtime and precarity, on the unspoken logic that a passionate worker would do it anyway — and Kim and colleagues show experimentally that people *do* judge exploiting "passionate" workers as more acceptable (*JPSP*, 2020); the passion schema also tends to reward those already privileged enough to chase it. The student's move is not to renounce passion or worship the paycheck, but to ask what the passion is being asked to pay for here — whether it is the reason the work is worth doing, or the reason they are being asked to accept less than the work is worth. (Both poles hold: passion genuinely sustains the best work and a life spent only chasing pay can go hollow; equally, "follow your passion" can be a story told to underpay you, and a stable wage is not a betrayal of anything.)
**questions_it_invites:** What is your passion being asked to pay for here — the meaning of the work, or a discount on what you're owed for it? · If this work paid properly and demanded less devotion, would it be less worth doing — and if "you love it" is the reason to accept less, whose interest does that serve?
**failure_modes:** accepting underpayment, overwork or precarity because you love the work, mistaking exploitation for vocation; treating any concern for pay as selling out, until you can no longer afford to keep doing the work at all; or the reverse — dismissing meaning entirely and chasing only the wage until the work goes dead.
**sources:** Tokumitsu, "In the Name of Love" (*Jacobin*, 2014) and *Do What You Love: And Other Lies About Success and Happiness* (2015); Kim, Campbell, Shepherd & Kay, "Understanding contemporary forms of exploitation: Attributions of passion serve to legitimize the poor treatment of workers" (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2020).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (work passion's real benefits — lower burnout, the Passionate Pygmalion effect — against passion-exploitation, Kim et al. 2020). See verification-consensus-backing.md. ⚠ Pass-3 watch: keep BOTH poles weighted — the project's anti-extractive bent must not make "follow your passion" the virtuous default (it is the thing critiqued here), nor cast the paycheck as the villain. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: specialist-or-generalist
**discipline:** money-career
**vocabulary:** specialise, specialist, niche, expert, focus, generalist, breadth, range, t-shaped, master, narrow, broad, depth, versatile, pigeonholed
**felt as:** i should specialise and pick a niche; become the expert in one thing; focus, don't spread yourself thin; or — be a generalist, learn a bit of everything; i don't want to be pigeonholed; jack of all trades, master of none; depth or breadth; what should i be known for.
**the_tension:** The career advice to specialise is strong: pick a niche, go deep, become the expert — and the deliberate-practice research lends it weight, since reaching the top of a field demands years of focused, effortful practice (Ericsson and colleagues, 1993). But the breadth case is just as evidenced. Güllich and colleagues found that world-class athletes — and Nobel laureates — typically had *more* multidisciplinary experience and specialised *later* than their merely national-level peers (2021); Teodoridis and colleagues found generalists outperform in slower-paced fields while specialists win where the frontier moves fast (2018); and the strong "just specialise and grind the hours" claim is itself overstated — deliberate practice leaves most of the variance in performance unexplained (Macnamara, Hambrick and colleagues). The student's move is to ask what *this* path actually rewards: whether the field is one where deep, narrow mastery compounds, or one where range and the ability to connect distant things matter more — and whether "specialise" is a considered fit or a fear of looking unfocused. (Both poles hold: depth is what makes someone genuinely good at something and breadth without any depth is thin; equally, early over-specialisation can trap a person in a niche the world moves past, and range is a real strength, not a failure to commit.)
**questions_it_invites:** Does this field reward deep narrow mastery, or the range to connect distant things — and which does the work you want actually call for? · Is "specialise" a considered fit for where you're headed, or a fear of looking unfocused — and is "stay broad" curiosity, or avoidance of commitment?
**failure_modes:** specialising early and narrowly into a niche the field later moves past, with no range to move; staying broad and shallow forever, never going deep enough to be genuinely good at anything; mistaking either depth or breadth for a virtue regardless of what the work needs.
**sources:** Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, "The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance" (*Psychological Review*, 1993); Güllich, Macnamara & Hambrick, "What Makes a Champion? Early Multidisciplinary Practice, Not Early Specialization, Predicts World-Class Performance" (*Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2021); Teodoridis, Bikard & Vakili, "Creativity at the Knowledge Frontier: The Impact of Specialization in Fast- and Slow-paced Domains" (*Administrative Science Quarterly*, 2018); the popular synthesis: Epstein, *Range: Why Generalists Triumph in a Specialized World* (2019).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (deliberate practice/specialisation vs multidisciplinary range, contingent on domain pace; the strong deliberate-practice claim empirically overstated — Macnamara/Hambrick). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: freelance-or-employed
**discipline:** money-career
**vocabulary:** freelance, freelancer, self-employed, gig, independent, contract, employed, job, salary, stable, security, autonomy, flexibility, benefits, precarious
**felt as:** i should go freelance for the freedom; be my own boss; freelancing means flexibility; or — i need a stable salaried job; the security of being employed; benefits and a steady paycheck; can i handle the uncertainty; employed feels safe but trapped; on my own feels free but scary.
**the_tension:** Going freelance promises autonomy — your own hours, your own clients, no boss — and the research bears out part of the dream: freelancers and the self-employed consistently report higher *job* satisfaction than the employed, value their flexibility, and are often as satisfied with life overall despite earning less (van der Zwan and colleagues; Binder and Coad). But the same literature names the cost plainly: gig and self-employed workers report higher financial precarity, loneliness, anxiety and health problems, and a striking "autonomy paradox" in which the freedom to set your own hours becomes the freedom to overwork and erode your wellbeing (Shevchuk and colleagues; Wood and colleagues on algorithmic control; Wang and colleagues). Several studies find the sharpest pattern is a split — higher life satisfaction *and* higher anxiety at once, evaluative wellbeing rising while emotional wellbeing falls — and that a *mix* of salaried and independent work can be the most satisfying of all. The student's move is to ask what they are actually trading: security for autonomy, a steady floor for a higher ceiling, emotional calm for evaluative meaning — and whether they are choosing freelance for the freedom or fleeing employment out of a restlessness a different job would settle. (Both poles hold: a salaried job is not a cage, and security genuinely frees some people to do their best work; equally, the autonomy of working for yourself is real and worth wanting, not mere instability dressed up.)
**questions_it_invites:** What are you actually trading here — security for autonomy, a steady floor for a higher ceiling, calm for meaning — and is that a trade you want? · Are you choosing freelance for the freedom, or leaving employment out of a restlessness a different job might settle?
**failure_modes:** going freelance for an idea of freedom that turns out to be precarity, isolation and the autonomy-paradox of never stopping; clinging to a salaried job for a security that has quietly become a trap; assuming one form is simply braver or safer than the other, regardless of the person.
**sources:** Standing, *The Precariat: The New Dangerous Class* (Bloomsbury, 2011); van der Zwan, Hessels & Burger, "Happy free willies? Investigating the relationship between freelancing and subjective well-being" (*Small Business Economics*, 2019); Berger, Frey, Levin & Danda, "Uber happy? Work and well-being in the 'Gig Economy'" (*Economic Policy*, 2019); Shevchuk, Strebkov & Davis, "The Autonomy Paradox: How Night Work Undermines Subjective Well-Being of Internet-Based Freelancers" (*ILR Review*, 2019).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (autonomy/job-satisfaction gains vs precarity/loneliness/anxiety; the evaluative-vs-emotional wellbeing split; mixed work often best). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-portfolio-and-the-personal-brand
**discipline:** money-career
**vocabulary:** portfolio, brand, personal, profile, presence, followers, self-promotion, visibility, reputation, showcase, market, audience, network, instagram
**felt as:** i need to build my personal brand; curate a portfolio that stands out; grow my following; get my work seen; market myself; build an online presence; it's all about visibility; i hate self-promotion but i have to; reputation is everything now.
**the_tension:** Building a portfolio and a personal brand is treated as simply necessary now — your work has to be seen, so you curate, post and present a coherent professional self. Some of that is plainly true: a portfolio communicates what you can do, visibility opens real doors, and refusing to be seen at all can quietly cost a career. But Marwick and Hearn argue that self-branding is not neutral — that turning oneself into a marketable, consistent "brand" is a kind of unpaid labour that can blur the line between the person and the product, reward performance over substance, and pull the maker toward whatever the audience rewards rather than the work they care about (Hearn, "Meat, Mask, Burden", 2008; Marwick, *Status Update*, 2013). The student's move is to ask what the brand is *for* here — whether it is a way to let real work find the people who need it, or a performance that has begun to shape the work itself — and whether the self being presented is a clarification of who they are or a flattening of it. (Both poles hold: a portfolio and a reachable professional presence are genuine, fair tools for getting work, not vanity; equally, a self managed as a brand can quietly start authoring the work, and "build your brand" can mean perform until the audience, not the work, is in charge.)
**questions_it_invites:** What is the brand for here — letting real work reach the people who need it, or a performance that's started to shape the work itself? · Is the self you present a clarification of who you are, or a flattening of it into what the audience rewards?
**failure_modes:** refusing all visibility until good work never reaches anyone who could use it; managing yourself as a brand until the audience's approval, not the work, decides what you make; mistaking a following for a body of work, or self-promotion for the thing itself.
**sources:** Hearn, "'Meat, Mask, Burden': Probing the contours of the branded self" (*Journal of Consumer Culture*, 2008); Marwick, *Status Update: Celebrity, Publicity, and Branding in the Social Media Age* (Yale University Press, 2013).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the self-branding critique (Hearn, Marwick) is a well-known position, citations Perplexity-verified. Position-in-named-works (not a contested empirical claim): citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.
