## entry: knowing-by-making
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** research, theory, practice, make, knowledge, study, evidence, method, academic, through, about, prototype, knowing, rigour
**felt as:** do i research first or just make; is making it research; where's the theory; i learn by doing it; how is this knowledge and not just a project; do i need to read first or build first.
**the_tension:** Christopher Frayling (*Research in Art and Design*, 1993) distinguishes three relationships between research and design: research *into* art and design (studying it, historically or critically); research *through* design (knowledge generated in and by the act of making — the prototype, the experiment, the materials teaching you something); and research *for* design (gathering reference to feed a piece). His pivotal claim is that *through* — knowing by making — is a legitimate form of knowledge production, not a lesser cousin of written theory. The counter is that "the making is the research" can become an alibi for work that produces artefacts but no transferable knowledge — nothing learned that anyone else could use. The student's move is to ask which kind of research this is, and — if claiming to know *through* making — what exactly the making taught that could not have been reasoned out in advance.
**failure_modes:** calling any project "research through design" without saying what was actually learned; assuming knowledge only counts when it is written theory, dismissing what the making revealed; gathering reference (research *for*) and mistaking it for inquiry.
**questions_it_invites:** Are you researching *into* this, *for* it, or *through* the making of it — and which is this? · If the making is your research, what did it teach you that you could not have worked out before you made it?
**sources:** Frayling, "Research in Art and Design," *Royal College of Art Research Papers*, vol. 1, no. 1 (1993).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the into / through / for distinction is Frayling's central, widely-cited contribution. Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers; "research through design" is an established, formalised mode of inquiry — Zimmerman et al. 2010 — and the counter is live in the literature too: its rigour and its claim to be a distinct "designerly way of knowing" are openly contested — Herriott 2019 — which is exactly this entry's caution about making-as-alibi — see verification-consensus-backing.md).

## entry: design-thinking-and-its-critics
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** design thinking, process, method, ideate, empathise, prototype, innovation, workshop, sticky notes, framework, problem solving, human-centred, toolkit
**felt as:** just run a design thinking process; empathise, ideate, prototype, test; use the toolkit; it's the innovation method; do a workshop with sticky notes; design thinking will solve it; is this just a formula; does the method actually work.
**the_tension:** Design thinking — popularised by Tim Brown of IDEO (*Change by Design*, 2009) — packaged design as a transferable, human-centred process (empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test) that anyone, in any field, could use to innovate. Its promise is real: it gives non-designers a way to take users seriously and make ideas tangible early. But it has drawn substantial criticism. Natasha Iskander argues design thinking is "fundamentally conservative", smoothing problems into a tidy procedure that preserves the status quo and the designer's authority (*HBR*, 2018); Lucy Kimbell argues the popular accounts oversimplify what design actually is, reducing situated, embodied practice to a generic cognitive recipe ("Rethinking Design Thinking", 2011). The tension: a portable method democratises design and can genuinely help, but a method abstracted from craft and context can become ritual — sticky notes and empathy theatre that produce the feeling of innovation without its substance. The student's move is to ask whether the process they are running is doing real work on this problem, or performing a procedure — and what the method's tidy steps might be flattening or leaving out.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the process you're running doing real work on this problem, or performing the steps of a method — and how would you tell the difference? · What does the tidy sequence leave out here — whose problem it is, what can't be prototyped in an afternoon, what the method's neatness hides?
**failure_modes:** running design-thinking ritual (the workshop, the sticky notes) and mistaking the feeling of innovation for the substance; dismissing the method wholesale when its discipline of taking users seriously genuinely helps; applying a generic process to a problem that needed deep domain knowledge it skipped.
**sources:** Brown, *Change by Design* (2009) and "Design Thinking" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2008); the critiques — Iskander, "Design Thinking Is Fundamentally Conservative and Preserves the Status Quo" (*HBR*, 2018); Kimbell, "Rethinking Design Thinking" (*Design and Culture*, 2011).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Brown's popularisation and the Iskander/Kimbell critiques are correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified; Brown popularised rather than invented the term). Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: who-gets-to-design
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** participation, participatory, co-design, users, stakeholders, involve, community, expert, designer, collaborate, voice, tokenism, engage, decide
**felt as:** just design it for them; the designer's the expert; involving users slows it down; or — co-design it with the community; nothing about them without them; let users decide; is participation real or just tokenism; who should actually make the call.
**the_tension:** Who has the right to design something — the trained designer, or the people who will live with it? The participatory-design tradition (Pelle Ehn, *Work-Oriented Design of Computer Artifacts*, 1988; Sanders and Stappers, "Co-creation and the new landscapes of design", 2008) argues that those affected should be active participants, not just subjects of research, and the evidence supports real gains: co-designed concepts can score higher in user benefit and novelty, and involving users early improves fit, adoption and satisfaction. But participation is not automatically virtuous. Studies find co-designed concepts can score *lower* in feasibility, that *partial* involvement (users frame the problem, professionals develop the solution) can outperform full co-design, and that participation easily becomes tokenistic — consultation theatre on Arnstein's lower rungs (1969) — when power is not genuinely shared. There is also the expertise question: design knowledge is real, and "let the users decide everything" can abdicate the designer's responsibility. The student's move is to ask who genuinely needs a hand in this decision and at what stage — and whether the participation they are planning shares real power or only performs it.
**questions_it_invites:** Who genuinely needs a hand in this decision, and at what stage — framing the problem, judging options, or making the final call? · Is the participation you're planning sharing real power, or staging consultation while the decisions stay where they were?
**failure_modes:** designing *for* people who should have had a hand in it, missing what only they know; staging participation that consults without ceding any real power (tokenism); abdicating design judgement entirely to "what users said", or letting participation stall a decision that needed making.
**sources:** Ehn, *Work-Oriented Design of Computer Artifacts* (1988); Sanders & Stappers, "Co-creation and the new landscapes of design," *CoDesign* 4, no. 1 (2008); Arnstein, "A Ladder of Citizen Participation," *JAIP* (1969).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (co-design's gains in user benefit/novelty/adoption — Trischler, Slattery — against lower feasibility, the "partial beats full" finding — Mannak — and tokenism — Arnstein). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: thinking-in-systems-or-the-thing
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** system, systemic, holistic, root cause, complexity, leverage, intervention, whole, parts, wicked, interconnected, scope, artefact
**felt as:** you have to think systemically; it's all interconnected; address the root cause; zoom out to the whole system; or — just design the actual thing in front of me; the system is too big to tackle; where do i even intervene; am i overcomplicating this.
**the_tension:** Many problems a designer meets are not really about the object in front of them but about the system it sits in — supply chains, incentives, behaviours, policies. Donella Meadows's *Thinking in Systems* (2008) makes the case that durable change comes from understanding the whole — its feedback loops and leverage points — rather than tinkering with a part, and that intervening at the wrong place (or treating a systemic problem as an artefact problem) produces effort that the system quietly absorbs or defeats. The counter is that systems thinking has its own failure mode: zoomed out far enough, everything connects to everything, and the call to "address the whole system" can become a route to paralysis, grandiosity, or endless mapping that never ships an intervention — while a concrete, well-made artefact can itself be a leverage point that shifts the system more than another diagram would. The student's move is to ask whether this problem genuinely lives in the system or in the thing — and, if in the system, what the smallest real intervention is, rather than using "it's systemic" to avoid making anything.
**questions_it_invites:** Does this problem actually live in the system, or in the thing in front of you — and what's the smallest real intervention if it's the system? · Is "we have to think systemically" opening up the right scope here, or becoming a reason not to make the concrete thing that would move it?
**failure_modes:** treating a systemic problem as an artefact problem, so the well-made object is absorbed by the system it ignored; expanding scope to "the whole system" until nothing concrete ever gets made; mistaking a systems map for an intervention.
**sources:** Meadows, *Thinking in Systems: A Primer* (Chelsea Green, 2008).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Meadows's systems-thinking / leverage-points framework is a well-known, correctly-attributed position (Perplexity-verified). Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: crossing-disciplines-or-going-deep
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** discipline, transdisciplinary, interdisciplinary, cross, integrate, depth, breadth, expertise, silo, generalist, collaborate, field, boundary, knowledge
**felt as:** this problem crosses disciplines; break the silos; integrate everything; bring all the fields together; or — i'm spreading too thin; do i have the depth; should i go deep in one field; is transdisciplinary work just shallow.
**the_tension:** Some problems plainly do not belong to a single discipline — they sit across design, engineering, social science, policy — and Gibbons and colleagues' account of "Mode 2" knowledge (*The New Production of Knowledge*, 1994) describes exactly this shift: knowledge produced around real-world problems, in context, across disciplinary boundaries, rather than within the silos of "Mode 1" academic disciplines; Nicolescu's *Manifesto of Transdisciplinarity* (2002) presses the integrative ideal further. The promise is that crossing boundaries lets you address problems no single field can hold. The counter, which the transdisciplinary student feels acutely, is that depth is real and not interchangeable with breadth: integration without genuine command of the fields being integrated can produce confident superficiality — dilettantism that borrows the vocabulary of many disciplines and the rigour of none — and some problems are best served by someone who has gone genuinely deep. The student's move is to ask whether this problem truly requires crossing fields or whether that is an evasion of the harder depth — and, if it does cross, where the actual expertise to integrate is going to come from.
**questions_it_invites:** Does this problem genuinely require crossing disciplines, or is "transdisciplinary" a way around the harder work of depth in one? · If it does cross fields, where is the real command of each coming from — yours, or collaborators' — so the integration isn't borrowing a rigour it doesn't have?
**failure_modes:** borrowing the vocabulary of many disciplines and the rigour of none, calling superficiality "integration"; staying siloed in one field when the problem genuinely needed others; treating either crossing or depth as the braver choice regardless of what the problem requires.
**sources:** Gibbons, Limoges, Nowotny, Schwartzman, Scott & Trow, *The New Production of Knowledge* (Sage, 1994, Mode 1 / Mode 2); Nicolescu, *Manifesto of Transdisciplinarity* (SUNY Press, 2002).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Mode 1/Mode 2 (Gibbons et al.) and Nicolescu's transdisciplinarity manifesto are well-known, correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified). Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off. (Relates to money-career's specialist-or-generalist but is about knowledge production across fields, not career structure.)

## entry: the-wicked-label-or-the-tractable-problem
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** wicked problem, complex, messy, intractable, systemic, tame, solvable, no solution, manageable, governance, accountability, scope, label, define, too big
**felt as:** this is a wicked problem; it's too complex to solve; there's no right answer so don't expect one; everything's connected so nothing's fixable; or — calling it wicked is a cop-out; isn't this just hard, not wicked; am i using "complex" to dodge being held to anything; could a chunk of this actually be solved.
**the_tension:** Rittel and Webber's "wicked problems" (*Policy Sciences*, 1973) named a real class of planning problem — ill-defined, with no stopping rule, where every intervention reshapes the problem and there is no true-or-false solution, only better-or-worse — and the concept rightly warns against treating such problems as if a tidy technical fix existed. But Brian Head and John Alford ("Wicked Problems: Implications for Public Management?", *Administration & Society*, 2015) push back on how the label gets *used*: they argue wickedness is a matter of degree, not an all-or-nothing verdict, that many problems called "wicked" are partly tractable, and — pointedly — that the label can become an excuse, a way to lower expectations, diffuse accountability, and justify inaction ("it's wicked, so nothing we tried could have worked"). The tension is that naming a problem wicked can be honest realism about its structure, or a rhetorical move that protects the namer from being held to an outcome. The student's move is to ask whether "wicked" is describing this problem's actual structure or excusing them from a tractable part of it — and which sub-parts could, in fact, be made better.
**questions_it_invites:** When you call this wicked, are you describing the problem's structure or lowering what anyone can expect of you — and which part of it could still be made better? · If you split this into pieces, which pieces are genuinely intractable and which are just hard — and who is accountable for the hard ones?
**failure_modes:** using "wicked" or "systemic" to pre-excuse failure and dodge accountability for the tractable parts; treating a genuinely wicked problem as a tame one with a single technical fix, so the fix rebounds; flattening a matter of degree into an all-or-nothing verdict either way.
**sources:** Rittel & Webber, "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning," *Policy Sciences* 4, no. 2 (1973): 155–169; Head & Alford, "Wicked Problems: Implications for Public Management?", *Administration & Society* 47, no. 6 (2015): 711–733.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Rittel & Webber's concept and Head & Alford's reassessment (degree-not-kind; the label as accountability-dodge) are correctly-attributed positions, both live-verified (Exa/Perplexity, 3 June 2026). Distinct from `interaction-design.md`'s `reflection-in-action-and-wicked-problems`: that entry is about the *method* a wicked problem calls for (tame-vs-wicked, reflection-in-action / Schön); this one is about the *validity and use of the label itself* (concept-validity / governance / accountability — Head & Alford). Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: solutionism-or-staying-with-the-trouble
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** solution, solutionism, fix, problem, solve, app, intervention, optimise, eliminate, messy, trouble, stay with, response, ongoing, repair, resolve
**felt as:** let's just solve it; there's an app for that; design a fix and ship it; problems are there to be eliminated; or — some things can't be "solved"; am i forcing a fix onto something that needs tending not solving; what if the mess is the point; is reaching for a clean solution making it worse.
**the_tension:** The reflex of design is to find the problem and solve it. Evgeny Morozov calls the inflated version of this *solutionism* (*To Save Everything, Click Here: The Folly of Technological Solutionism*, 2013): the urge to recast every messy human situation as a neat problem with a technological fix, which mis-describes the situation, ignores who it serves, and often produces new harms while claiming to dissolve old ones. Against the solve-and-move-on posture, Donna Haraway argues for *staying with the trouble* (*Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene*, 2016) — remaining present and responsive in difficult, entangled situations rather than reaching for either techno-fix optimism or game-over despair; and Ezio Manzini's design for social innovation (*Design, When Everybody Designs*, 2015) similarly treats many social problems as ongoing collaborative processes to be cultivated, not one-shot solutions to be delivered. The counter holds, and genuinely: many problems *do* have solutions, "staying with the trouble" can curdle into endless deliberation that helps no one, and a refusal to act in the name of complexity is its own failure. The student's move is to ask whether this situation wants solving or tending — and whether a clean fix would describe it truthfully or flatter the designer's wish to be done.
**questions_it_invites:** Does this situation want solving, or staying-with and tending — and how would you tell which? · If you ship a clean fix here, what about the situation does that fix have to ignore to stay clean?
**failure_modes:** forcing a tidy technological fix onto a situation that needed ongoing care, so the fix mis-describes it and creates new harm (solutionism); using "you can't just solve it" to justify endless deliberation while a solvable problem goes unaddressed; mistaking the wish to be finished for evidence that the problem is finishable.
**sources:** Morozov, *To Save Everything, Click Here: The Folly of Technological Solutionism* (PublicAffairs, 2013); Haraway, *Staying with the Trouble: Making Kin in the Chthulucene* (Duke University Press, 2016); Manzini, *Design, When Everybody Designs: An Introduction to Design for Social Innovation* (MIT Press, 2015).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Morozov's "solutionism" and Haraway's "staying with the trouble" are correctly-attributed, well-known positions (live-verified, Exa/Perplexity, 3 June 2026; Manzini's diffuse social-innovation framing confirmed). A theory/position tension. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: participation-that-shifts-power-or-performs-it
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** participation, consultation, engage, stakeholders, community, power, decide, voice, tokenism, tyranny, co-design, workshop, listen, control, who decides
**felt as:** we ran a consultation; we did a community workshop; we listened to stakeholders; we engaged everyone; or — did any of that actually change who decided; is this real power-sharing or just optics; the community spoke but we'd already decided; who actually held the decision in the end.
**the_tension:** It is one thing to involve people; it is another to change who decides. Sherry Arnstein's "A Ladder of Citizen Participation" (*Journal of the American Institute of Planners*, 1969) ranks participation precisely on this axis — from manipulation and "therapy" at the bottom, through tokenistic consultation and "placation" in the middle, up to genuine partnership and citizen control at the top — and her point is that most "participation" lives on the lower rungs: it stages voice while the decision stays exactly where it was. Bill Cooke and Uma Kothari's *Participation: The New Tyranny?* (Zed Books, 2001) sharpens the warning: participation can be not merely empty but actively harmful — a "tyranny" that launders existing power, manufactures consent, co-opts dissent, and lends legitimacy to decisions communities never really shaped. The counter is real and must be weighed: full citizen control is not always feasible or even wanted, expertise and accountability sometimes rightly sit with the designer or commissioner, and consultation that informs a decision-maker is not worthless just because it is not control. The student's move is to ask, of the participation they are running, what it actually changes about who decides — and whether it shares power or performs the appearance of sharing it.
**questions_it_invites:** What does the participation you're running actually change about who makes the final decision — and what would be different if it changed nothing? · Where on the rung from "informing a decision already made" to "the community deciding" does this sit, and is that where you told people it sits?
**failure_modes:** staging consultation or a workshop while the decision stays untouched, then citing "we engaged the community" as cover (tokenism / Arnstein's lower rungs); participation that launders power and manufactures consent for a predetermined plan (Cooke & Kothari's "tyranny"); over-correcting into demanding full citizen control where expertise or accountability rightly sat elsewhere, stalling a decision that needed making.
**sources:** Arnstein, "A Ladder of Citizen Participation," *Journal of the American Institute of Planners* 35, no. 4 (1969): 216–224; Cooke & Kothari (eds.), *Participation: The New Tyranny?* (Zed Books, 2001).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Arnstein's ladder and Cooke & Kothari's "tyranny" critique are correctly-attributed, well-known positions (live-verified, Exa/Perplexity, 3 June 2026). Distinct from `who-gets-to-design` (which is about *who is in the room* — the right to participate at all); this entry is about whether participation *changes who decides* — the power axis, not the access axis. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-designer-as-facilitator-or-as-author
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** facilitator, neutral, enable, empower, everybody, diffuse, expert, author, voice, decisions, steer, designer, behind, agenda, invisible
**felt as:** i'm just facilitating; i'm enabling the community to design; i'm a neutral enabler; design is by everybody now; or — am i really neutral; my framing already steered it; whose vision is this actually; should i own the choices i'm making instead of hiding behind "facilitation".
**the_tension:** When design moves from making objects to enabling social processes, the designer's role seems to dissolve into facilitation — Ezio Manzini (*Design, When Everybody Designs*, 2015) describes a world of *diffuse design*, where everybody designs, and the professional becomes an enabler of others' creativity rather than the author of a finished thing. That shift is real and valuable: it takes seriously that people can design their own lives and that the expert's job is often to host, prompt, and support. But Manzini himself insists the *expert designer* does not thereby disappear — expert design still contributes specific cultural and technical competence, makes consequential framing choices, and brings an agenda — and the danger is the pretence of neutrality: a "neutral facilitator" who quietly authors the outcome through how they frame the question, who is in the room, and which options get drawn, while disclaiming responsibility for the result. The tension is between genuinely enabling others and using "I only facilitated" to hide authorship the designer is in fact exercising. The student's move is to ask where, in this process, they are actually making the choices — and whether calling themselves a facilitator names their role honestly or launders an authorship they would rather not own.
**questions_it_invites:** Where in this process are you actually making the choices — in the framing, the room, the options drawn — even while calling yourself a facilitator? · If you owned the parts you authored rather than calling the whole thing "facilitation", what would you have to take responsibility for?
**failure_modes:** claiming neutral facilitation while steering the outcome through framing and selection, then disclaiming responsibility for it; over-correcting into authorial control that crowds out the people the process was meant to enable; treating "everybody designs" as a reason to abandon the expert's specific competence and judgement where it was needed.
**sources:** Manzini, *Design, When Everybody Designs: An Introduction to Design for Social Innovation* (MIT Press, 2015) — the diffuse-design / expert-design distinction.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the diffuse-vs-expert-design distinction (and the "expert design does not disappear" claim) is Manzini's, correctly attributed and live-verified (Exa/Perplexity, 3 June 2026). The "neutral facilitator is a myth" counter is the entry's framing of a tension internal to Manzini's own account, not a separate cited author. Distinct from `who-gets-to-design` and `participation-that-shifts-power-or-performs-it` (those concern the participants; this concerns the *designer's* role and honesty about authorship). Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: measuring-impact-or-protecting-the-unmeasurable
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** impact, measure, metrics, SROI, social value, evidence, prove, outcomes, accountability, quantify, target, audit, unmeasurable, distort, funders
**felt as:** prove the impact; show me the numbers; measure the social value; funders need metrics; quantify the outcome; or — the thing that mattered won't fit a number; chasing the metric is bending the work; what gets measured gets gamed; some of this can't be counted and shouldn't be.
**the_tension:** Social and civic design is increasingly asked to *prove* its impact, and there are good reasons to: methods like Social Return on Investment (the standard *A Guide to Social Return on Investment*, Cabinet Office / SROI Network, 2009) let organisations articulate and account for the social and environmental value they create, move resources toward what works, and answer funders and communities with more than anecdote. Measuring impact can protect good work and expose empty work. But Marilyn Strathern's anthropology of audit ("'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system", *European Review*, 1997) names the cost: audit "does more than monitor — it has a life of its own that jeopardizes the life it audits", and her widely-cited rendering of Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — captures how quantification can distort the very practice it was meant to account for, crowding out the unmeasurable goods (trust, dignity, slow relationship-building) that don't fit the indicator. The tension is that what cannot be measured tends to be neglected, yet what is measured tends to be gamed and to displace what mattered. The student's move is to ask what this measurement would make visible and accountable — and what it would quietly push out of the work because it cannot be counted.
**questions_it_invites:** What would this measurement make visible and hold to account — and what part of the work would it quietly push out because it can't be counted? · If the metric became the target here, how would the work bend to hit it, and would that bending still be the work you meant to do?
**failure_modes:** letting the measure become the target, so the work bends to hit the indicator and the unmeasurable goods that mattered are crowded out (Strathern / Goodhart); refusing all measurement in the name of the "unmeasurable", leaving empty work unaccountable and indistinguishable from real work; treating a number as the whole of the value rather than a partial, gameable proxy for it.
**sources:** *A Guide to Social Return on Investment* (Cabinet Office / Office of the Third Sector & The SROI Network, 2009; authors Jeremy Nicholls, Eilis Lawlor, Eva Neitzert, Tim Goodspeed); Strathern, "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system," *European Review* 5, no. 3 (1997): 305–321.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both poles live-verified (Exa, 3 June 2026): the SROI guide is the standard UK reference document (named authors confirmed on the title page), and Strathern's audit paper is the source of the "measure becomes a target" / "audit jeopardizes the life it audits" formulation. An empirical/position tension; not yet a dedicated Consensus pass (the two-sidedness is established in the named works themselves). Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-process-model-or-the-reframe
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** process, double diamond, discover, define, develop, deliver, stages, model, framework, reframe, frame, problem, abduction, brief, diverge, converge
**felt as:** just follow the double diamond; discover, define, develop, deliver; run the stages; we have a process; or — the real move was changing the question, not running the steps; the brief's problem was the wrong problem; the model assumes the problem's already framed; where in the process does the actual reframing happen.
**the_tension:** Design processes are often taught as a model with stages — most famously the Design Council's *Double Diamond* (2005): discover, define, develop, deliver, diverging then converging twice. The model is genuinely useful: it gives a shared language, makes the work legible to clients and teams, and reminds people to explore before they commit. But Kees Dorst (*Frame Innovation: Create New Thinking by Design*, MIT Press, 2015) argues that the heart of expert design is not running stages but *frame creation* — the abductive move of reframing the problem itself, proposing a new way of seeing the situation so that a different and better space of solutions opens up. On this view, a stage model can mislead: it tends to assume the problem is already correctly framed and just needs solving, so following the diamond faithfully can deliver a polished answer to the wrong question, while the decisive creative act — changing the question — has no clear home in the boxes. The counter holds: process models coordinate real work and prevent teams from skipping discovery, and "it's all reframing" can become an excuse for an undisciplined process. The student's move is to ask whether the problem they are running through the stages is the right problem to begin with — and where, if anywhere, this process lets them stop and reframe it.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the problem you're running through the stages the right problem — and where in this process could you stop and change the question itself? · What does the neat sequence of stages assume is already settled here that might actually be the thing to reopen?
**failure_modes:** following the process model faithfully to a polished solution for the wrong problem, because the model assumed the frame was already right; treating "it's really about reframing" as licence for an undisciplined process that never converges or delivers; mistaking moving through the stages for having done the hard work of framing.
**sources:** Design Council, the *Double Diamond* design process model (2005); Dorst, *Frame Innovation: Create New Thinking by Design* (MIT Press, 2015).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the Double Diamond (Design Council, 2005) and Dorst's frame-creation/abduction (2015) are correctly-attributed, well-known positions (live-verified, Exa/Perplexity, 3 June 2026). ## entry: scaling-up-or-the-network-of-locals
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** scale, scaling up, grow, replicate, spread, impact, reach, expand, roll out, local, small, distributed, network, connected, diffusion
**felt as:** how does this scale; we need to grow it; roll it out everywhere; replicate the model; reach more people; or — does it have to grow to matter; scaling it would kill what made it work; what if it spreads as many small connected versions instead of one big one; bigger isn't the only way to matter.
**the_tension:** A social innovation that works in one place faces the question of how it grows. The conventional answer is *scaling up* — Frances Westley and Nino Antadze ("Making a Difference: Strategies for Scaling Social Innovation for Greater Impact," *The Innovation Journal*, 2010) describe broadening impact by replicating, adapting, and changing the institutions and resource flows of the wider system so a solution reaches higher and wider; on this view, impact means reach, and reach means growth or institutional adoption. Ezio Manzini offers a different logic of scale in his *SLOC* scenario — small, local, open, connected ("Small, Local, Open and Connected", *Journal of Design Strategies*, 2010): many local, human-scale solutions that stay small but, because they are open and networked, become "a network of locals" whose connection — not whose individual size — is the scale. Here a thing can matter at large scale without any single instance growing, and forcing a place-based, relationship-dependent initiative to "scale up" can destroy the very locality and trust that made it work. The counter holds both ways: some innovations genuinely need institutional scale to reach the people who need them, and a romance of "small and local" can leave good work stranded and self-limiting. The student's move is to ask whether this thing should grow, replicate, or connect — and what its actual unit of impact is.
**questions_it_invites:** Should this grow bigger, be replicated, or stay small and connect to others — and what is its real unit of impact? · What about this works *because* it's small and local, and what would scaling it up have to break to make it bigger?
**failure_modes:** forcing a place-based, relationship-dependent initiative to scale up, destroying the locality and trust that made it work; staying small as a matter of romance when reaching the people who need it genuinely required institutional scale; assuming reach must mean growth of one entity rather than connection among many.
**sources:** Westley & Antadze, "Making a Difference: Strategies for Scaling Social Innovation for Greater Impact," *The Innovation Journal: The Public Sector Innovation Journal* 15, no. 2 (2010), article 2; Manzini, "Small, Local, Open and Connected: Design Research Topics in the Age of Networks and Sustainability," *Journal of Design Strategies* 4, no. 1 (2010).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Westley & Antadze's scaling-up account and Manzini's SLOC ("network of locals") scenario are correctly-attributed, live-verified positions (Exa, 3 June 2026; both primary texts read). Reuses Manzini on a genuinely different axis from `the-designer-as-facilitator-or-as-author` (there: the designer's role; here: the mechanism of scale). ## entry: the-designerly-leap-or-the-evidence
**discipline:** transdisciplinary-design
**vocabulary:** intuition, judgement, designerly, knowing, leap, gut, evidence, evidence-based, research, proof, studies, data, hunch, justify, abductive
**felt as:** i just know this is right; trust the design judgement; it's a designerly leap; you can't prove it before you make it; or — where's the evidence for that; show me a study; is this a hunch dressed as expertise; should this decision be grounded in research rather than instinct.
**the_tension:** Designers routinely make confident moves they cannot fully justify in advance — and Nigel Cross ("Designerly ways of knowing," *Design Studies*, 1982) argues this is not a defect but a distinct, legitimate form of knowledge: designers reason abductively, proposing a solution and a frame together, working through the artefact rather than from prior proof, in ways that are not reducible to the methods of the sciences or the humanities. The designerly leap is real knowing. But against the leap stands the case for grounding decisions in evidence: Roger Ulrich's landmark study showing surgical patients with a window view of trees recovered faster, on less pain medication, than those facing a brick wall ("View Through a Window May Influence Recovery from Surgery," *Science*, 1984) became foundational to *evidence-based design* — the position that consequential design decisions (especially where wellbeing or safety is at stake) should be tested against data, not left to a designer's intuition. The tension is that intuition is where genuine design insight often originates and cannot always wait for proof, yet "trust my judgement" can be a hunch or a bias dressed as expertise, and in high-stakes domains the evidence may flatly contradict the confident leap. The student's move is to ask whether this particular decision is one their designerly judgement can rightly carry — or one that should be grounded in evidence before they commit.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this decision one your design judgement can rightly carry, or one whose stakes mean it should be grounded in evidence before you commit? · When you say you "just know" this is right, what is that knowing built on — and what would it take for evidence to change your mind?
**failure_modes:** dressing a hunch or a bias as "designerly judgement" and refusing evidence that could check it, especially where wellbeing or safety is at stake; demanding proof before any move in a situation that genuinely required an abductive leap, paralysing the design; treating intuition and evidence as enemies rather than asking which the specific decision calls for.
**sources:** Cross, "Designerly ways of knowing," *Design Studies* 3, no. 4 (1982): 221–227; Ulrich, "View Through a Window May Influence Recovery from Surgery," *Science* 224, no. 4647 (1984): 420–421 (a foundational evidence-based-design study).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Cross's "designerly ways of knowing" (abductive, designerly judgement) and Ulrich's window-view study (foundational to evidence-based design) are correctly-attributed, live-verified works (Exa, 3 June 2026; both confirmed against repository/journal pages). A methodology tension on *how a design decision is warranted*; Ulrich's healthcare example keeps it distinct from space-design's building-performance entries (the axis here is intuition-vs-evidence as warrant, not building science).
