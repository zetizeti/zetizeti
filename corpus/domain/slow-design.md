# Domain corpus: The Slow-Design Lens

> **Part B — cross-cutting lens.** Synthetic, retrieved per turn via FTS5. Where the interaction-design entries describe the discipline's tensions neutrally, these entries are a *lens* aimed at the learner's **conventional** assumptions — speed, efficiency, frictionlessness, engagement, cleanliness, "more". Their vocabulary is deliberately keyed to those conventional words, so the lens activates exactly when a student reaches for them.

## entry: restraint-is-not-neutral
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** minimal, clean, neutral, simple, flat, plain, modern, default, grid, restraint, less, uncluttered, timeless
**felt as:** let's just keep it clean and minimal; i want it simple and modern; make it neutral so it doesn't get in the way; strip it back, less is more; just use a plain grid; keep it timeless and uncluttered.
**the_tension:** "Clean", "minimal", "neutral" can feel like the *absence* of an aesthetic. Design history reads restraint differently — as a particular style with a lineage (Bauhaus, Swiss, modernist) that some content can't enter. Is your "neutral" choice serving clarity here, or carrying an aesthetic position you haven't named — and who or what does it leave out?
The modernist grid was offered as rational, clear, transparent — order rather than style. Later reading treats it as culturally specific, not timeless: a grid is a container that some kinds of content cannot enter. The same goes for "invisible" typography, long held as the ideal of a craft that should not draw attention to itself. On this reading, minimalism is a position — with a history and an exclusion — rather than the ground zero from which other choices are deviations. (The counter holds, and genuinely: shared conventions like the grid aid clarity and legibility, often serve the reader well, and treating every restraint as ideology can paralyse — sometimes the clean choice is simply the right one.)
**failure_modes:** mistaking one's own aesthetic training for neutrality; a grid so assumed that content which won't conform is judged as bad rather than as evidence the grid won't bend.
**questions_it_invites:** Does this "neutral" choice leave anything out — and if so, what? · Whose idea of clarity is this, and is it the right one here?
**sources:** Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1981); Warde, "The Crystal Goblet, or Printing Should Be Invisible" (essay 1955; from the lecture "Printing Should Be Invisible", 1930).

## entry: who-slowness-excludes
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** slow, friction, reflective, considered, patient, effort, attention, time, deliberate, demanding, deep, mindful
**felt as:** i want people to slow down and reflect; make them sit with it; add some friction so it's more considered; it should be deliberate and patient; give it depth and make them take their time.
**the_tension:** Slow, effortful, reflective design assumes a user who *has* time, attention, and the cultural fluency to recognise what is being offered. Designing for slowness can be a gift — or a quiet act of exclusion. Who can't afford the slowness you're designing, and is that exclusion a failure, or is it constitutive of what you're making?
Slow design positions reflection and deceleration as virtues against a culture of speed. But the slow-design invitation to "take your time, sit with this" can presume leisure and prior fluency that many users don't have; friction-as-virtue can read as a private joke for those already initiated. The lens makes the student hold both: the value of resisting the cult of speed, and the privilege baked into that resistance. (The counter holds: relentless frictionlessness has its own harms — addiction, thoughtlessness — and *some* exclusivity is honest, not every artefact must serve everyone.)
**failure_modes:** designing slowness for a user who looks like the designer and calling it universal; treating a user's lack of time or fluency as their failing rather than a designed-in barrier.
**questions_it_invites:** Who is this slowness for, and who is shut out by it? · Is the friction protecting the user, or flattering the people who already get it?
**sources:** Strauss & Fuad-Luke, "The Slow Design Principles" (2008); Honoré, *In Praise of Slowness: Challenging the Cult of Speed* (2004).

## entry: the-shortcut-skips-the-journey
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** search, shortcut, fast, quick, efficient, skip, instant, find, jump, save time, streamline, seamless
**felt as:** just add a search so they can jump straight to it; let them skip ahead; make it quick and instant; streamline it, save them time; cut straight to what they want; make it seamless.
**the_tension:** Search — and every shortcut — is the anti-slow gesture: it knows what it wants and skips the journey. Sometimes the journey *was* the value; sometimes it was just friction in the way of a person in a hurry. When a user reaches for the shortcut, what are they confessing — and what is lost, or rightly saved, by giving it to them instantly?
A shortcut treats the path as cost. But in reflective, associative, or learning contexts, the path is where the value lived — the wandering, the things found on the way to the thing. The lens asks the student to notice when efficiency quietly deletes the experience it was meant to deliver. (The counter holds: for a user with a known goal and no time, the journey is genuinely just friction, and refusing them the shortcut is precious, not principled.)
**questions_it_invites:** When the user skips straight to it, what do they never encounter that they might have needed? · Was the journey the value, or the obstacle — and for whom?
**failure_modes:** optimising away a path that was the actual point; romanticising the journey for a user who only wanted the destination.
**sources:** Hallnäs & Redström, "Slow Technology – Designing for Reflection" (2001).

## entry: wandering-or-abandonment
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** explore, discover, browse, open-ended, freedom, wander, rabbit hole, serendipity, navigation, orient, lost, get lost
**felt as:** let users explore and discover on their own; let them get lost and wander; keep it open-ended and free; no fixed path, let them roam; trust them to find their own way; lean into serendipity and rabbit holes.
**the_tension:** "Let the user explore and get lost" is celebrated as associative discovery — but the labyrinth only works if the wanderer trusts there's something worth finding. At what point does productive wandering become disorientation, and disorientation become abandonment? From inside the experience, what tells the difference between a design that invites you to get lost and one that has simply failed to orient you?
Open-ended, exploratory design resists the funnel and the optimised path; it trusts the user to find their own way. But the same lack of orientation reads as freedom to a confident user and as being-lost to an anxious one. The lens asks the student to locate the line — and to notice that "we let users explore" can be a virtue or an alibi for not having designed orientation at all. (The counter holds, and genuinely: some discoveries are only reachable by getting lost — over-orientation can sand away the very serendipity that was the point, and a confident wanderer rarely thanks you for a handrail they did not need.)
**questions_it_invites:** What tells your user there is something worth finding, before they find it? · When does the freedom to wander here become being-lost — and which is this design closer to?
**failure_modes:** mistaking absence of guidance for freedom; a wanderer who leaves because nothing assured them the wandering had a point.
**sources:** Hallnäs & Redström, "Slow Technology – Designing for Reflection" (2001); Thackara, *In the Bubble: Designing in a Complex World* (2005).

## entry: visibility-and-the-unrewarded
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** likes, engagement, gamify, reward, badge, points, streak, social, feedback, motivate, contribute, retention, score
**felt as:** add likes and points to drive engagement; gamify it with badges and streaks; reward people so they keep coming back; show counts to motivate contributions; or — we stripped out all the metrics so we're not manipulative; how do we get anyone to take part.
**the_tension:** Removing likes, counts and badges is framed as a refusal to gamify — a moral upgrade. But people contribute to things partly *because they are seen* contributing. If you strip out social feedback, what replaces it — and if the honest answer is "nothing," who is willing to act for nothing, and is that small group enough to sustain what you're building?
The anti-gamification stance resists manufactured engagement and the dignity-cost of being farmed for metrics. Yet visibility is also genuine fuel; a refusal to gamify can quietly become a refusal to motivate anyone but the already-devoted. The lens makes the student hold both the integrity of refusing the slot-machine *and* the survival question it leaves open. (Note: this lens challenges the conventional gamify-everything reflex *and* the equally conventional we-removed-all-metrics-so-we're-virtuous reflex.)
**questions_it_invites:** If you remove all the counts, what is left to make someone act — and who still will? · Is "we don't gamify" a principle here, or a reason no one stays?
**failure_modes:** gamifying by reflex until the activity is only about the points; removing all feedback and assuming intrinsic motivation will simply appear.
**sources:** Strauss & Fuad-Luke, "The Slow Design Principles" (2008).

## entry: made-to-be-lived-with
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** efficient, productivity, optimise, fast, engagement, retention, usage, performance, speed, time-on-task, frictionless, convenient
**felt as:** i want to boost engagement and time-on-app; optimise it, make it faster and more efficient; improve retention and usage; get the task done quicker; make it convenient and frictionless; measure success by how much it gets used.
**the_tension:** Most design is measured by speed and usage — task done faster, more time-on-app, higher engagement. Slow technology proposes a different aim: artefacts made to be *lived with* and reflected on, present over years rather than consumed in seconds. Is what you're making meant to be used quickly and forgotten, or to be dwelt with — and are you measuring it by the right clock?
Efficiency metrics answer "did they do it faster / more often"; they cannot see whether a thing deepened, lasted, or was worth returning to. Slow technology designs for reflection and long presence, not throughput. The lens asks the student which kind of thing this is — and notes that optimising for speed and engagement can crowd out the qualities (depth, longevity, meaning) a reflective design might have been *for*. (The counter holds: for genuinely instrumental tools, fast-and-forgotten is the correct goal, and "live with it" would be precious.)
**questions_it_invites:** Is this made to be used and forgotten, or lived with? · What clock are you measuring success on — and is it the right one for this?
**failure_modes:** optimising engagement for something that should have got out of the user's way; measuring a reflective artefact by time-on-task.
**sources:** Hallnäs & Redström, "Slow Technology – Designing for Reflection" (2001); Thackara, *In the Bubble: Designing in a Complex World* (2005).

## entry: slow-on-the-phone
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** mobile, phone, app, responsive, on the go, quick, notification, scroll, fragmented, attention, swipe, feed
**felt as:** it needs to work on the phone, on the go; make it responsive and quick to glance at; add notifications to pull them back; design for short sessions and scrolling; put it in a feed; it has to be mobile-first.
**the_tension:** The phone is the device of fragmented attention — built for the quick, interruptible, in-between moment. Slowness asks for the opposite. Is "slow mobile" a contradiction in terms, or is the phone's temporal grammar exactly what most needs a slow counter-design? When you move something onto the phone, are you serving it, or surrendering it to the feed?
Designing for mobile usually means accommodating distraction: short sessions, glanceability, notifications that pull. A reflective, slow experience seems to fight the device itself. The lens asks the student whether porting to mobile extends the work or dissolves it into the scroll — and whether a genuinely slow mobile interface is possible or a category error. (The counter holds: refusing mobile can be its own elitism, abandoning the only device many people have.)
**questions_it_invites:** Does this survive the phone, or get absorbed by it? · What would slowness even look like on the device of interruption?
**failure_modes:** porting a reflective experience to mobile by making it glanceable until nothing reflective remains; refusing mobile and quietly excluding everyone who only has a phone.
**sources:** Hallnäs & Redström, "Slow Technology – Designing for Reflection" (2001); Honoré, *In Praise of Slowness: Challenging the Cult of Speed* (2004).

## entry: name-the-tension-first
**discipline:** slow-design
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** improve, better, feature, redesign, add, fix, wishlist, requirements, brief, goal, scope, idea, solution
**felt as:** how could this be better; let's add features and fix things; here's my wishlist; what should it do; we just need to improve it; add this and this and this; let's redesign the whole thing.
**the_tension:** "How could this be better?" tends to generate wishlists — a pile of features answering no clear question. A slower move asks what tension a design would resolve, and names that strain first. But problems and solutions also *co-evolve* — sometimes you only find the real tension by making something. So: is this a moment to articulate the tension before building, or a moment to build in order to discover it?
A design follows from a tension; if the tension is unclear, the design will be cosmetic — rearranging the surface while the real strain goes untouched. This is the slowest gesture of all: refusing to design until the question is articulated. The lens turns the student's own brief back on them — not "what should it do?" but "what is the unresolved strain this is for?" (The counter holds: over-deliberation is real too; at some point you must build to learn, and a tension can be discovered *through* making, not only before it.)
**questions_it_invites:** What tension is this design for — and have you named it, or are you still finding it? · Would articulating the strain now sharpen this, or would making something reveal it faster?
**failure_modes:** producing a feature wishlist with no underlying tension; endless tension-articulation that never lets making begin.
**sources:** Strauss & Fuad-Luke, "The Slow Design Principles: A New Interrogative and Reflexive Tool for Design Research and Practice" (2008).

## entry: maintenance-over-innovation
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** innovate, new, redesign, disrupt, latest, cutting-edge, fresh, reinvent, next, novel, revolutionary, overhaul, version
**felt as:** let's build something new; time for a redesign; we need to innovate; ship the next big thing; start fresh; disrupt the space; what's the latest approach; scrap it and reinvent.
**the_tension:** "Innovate", "redesign", "ship the new thing" is the default ambition; the work of keeping the existing thing running rarely earns the same status. There is a question hiding in the reach for the new: does this situation actually call for a new artefact, or for the unglamorous upkeep of the one already there — and which of those is harder to do well?
Russell and Vinsel argue that culture overvalues innovation and undervalues maintenance — that the people and labour who keep infrastructure, code and objects working are made invisible by an obsession with the new (*The Innovation Delusion*, 2020; "Hail the Maintainers", 2016). Jackson's "broken-world thinking" makes the same move for design: repair and sustaining-work, not invention, are where much of the real value and knowledge live ("Rethinking Repair", 2014). The lens asks the student to weigh maintenance as a design act in its own right. (The counter holds, and genuinely: some situations are not maintainable — the thing is broken at the root, or the world it served has gone — and clinging to upkeep can be its own avoidance; sometimes the new thing is exactly what's needed.)
**questions_it_invites:** Does this call for a new thing, or for keeping the existing one alive — and which is harder here? · What would maintaining this well look like, and is that the work being avoided?
**failure_modes:** reaching for a redesign because the new is more exciting than the upkeep the situation actually needs; clinging to the maintenance of something whose moment has genuinely passed.
**sources:** Russell & Vinsel, "Hail the Maintainers" (*Aeon*, 2016) and *The Innovation Delusion* (Currency, 2020); Jackson, "Rethinking Repair" (in *Media Technologies*, MIT Press, 2014).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the maintenance-vs-innovation critique is Russell & Vinsel's central, well-known thesis (citations Perplexity-verified). Position held in named works (not a contested empirical claim), so treated like first-things-first: citation-verified, framing PENDING the three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: patina-and-the-pristine
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** pristine, polished, clean, flawless, like-new, mint, perfect, fix, scratch, wear, finish, smooth, immaculate
**felt as:** keep it pristine and polished; make it look flawless; fix every scratch; it should feel brand new; smooth out the imperfections; nothing worn or marked; immaculate finish.
**the_tension:** The instinct is to keep a thing looking new — to design out wear, hide the marks of use, treat every scratch as a defect. But some objects deepen as they age, and a surface that can never show use can also never show care. Is the pristineness you're protecting serving the thing, or refusing it a life?
Chapman argues that much waste comes from objects that cannot age gracefully — that designing for *emotional durability* means letting things wear, scar and accrue a history, so attachment can grow rather than curdle into the itch to replace (*Emotionally Durable Design*, 2005/2015). The wabi-sabi tradition Koren describes treats impermanence, imperfection and the patina of use as where beauty lives, not as failure (*Wabi-Sabi*, 1994). The lens asks whether "flawless" is the right aim here. (The counter holds, and genuinely: pristineness is not vanity everywhere — in medical, food, safety or professional contexts visible wear reads as neglect or risk, and "make it look cared-for" is exactly right; not every object is meant to be lived in like a leather chair.)
**questions_it_invites:** Is this a thing that should age, or one that should stay new — and who decides? · What does a mark of use signal here: neglect, or care?
**failure_modes:** designing out the wear that would have let attachment grow, so the object stays new right up until it is discarded; letting things show wear where wear actually reads as neglect or hazard.
**sources:** Chapman, *Emotionally Durable Design: Objects, Experiences and Empathy* (Earthscan/Routledge, 2005; 2nd ed. 2015); Koren, *Wabi-Sabi: for Artists, Designers, Poets & Philosophers* (Stone Bridge Press, 1994).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — emotional durability (Chapman) and wabi-sabi (Koren) are well-known positions, citations Perplexity-verified; emotional-durability empirics are independently Consensus-backed under made-to-be-lived-with. Position-in-named-works otherwise: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-tyranny-of-convenience
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** convenient, automatic, effortless, easy, one-tap, frictionless, hassle-free, simple, instant, do it for them, save effort, streamline
**felt as:** make it convenient and effortless; one tap and it's done; remove all the hassle; let the app do it for them; make it the easy choice; zero effort; just automate the boring part.
**the_tension:** "Make it convenient" sounds like pure service to the user. But convenience is never only a gift — it quietly decides which activities are worth a person's effort and which are handed off, and the things we no longer do, we slowly can no longer do. When you make something effortless, what capability or attention are you also retiring, and is that a fair trade here?
Wu argues that convenience has become an unexamined value that flattens individuality — that we choose the convenient option not because we want the outcome but because the effort of the alternative has been engineered away ("The Tyranny of Convenience", 2018). Borgmann's *device paradigm* names the same pattern: devices deliver a commodity (warmth, music, a meal) while hiding the engaged practice that once produced it, so the practice and its skills recede (*Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life*, 1984). The lens asks what a given convenience hands off. (The counter holds, and genuinely: convenience is also liberation — it frees finite time and attention for what actually matters, and for disabled or overloaded users the "effortful" version is not virtue but a barrier; not every offloaded chore is a loss.)
**questions_it_invites:** What does this convenience hand off — and is that something worth keeping in the user's own hands? · Whose effort are you removing, and would they thank you for it or miss it?
**failure_modes:** engineering away an effort that was the practice's whole point, until the capability quietly atrophies; treating every convenience as a moral loss and burdening users with effort that served no one.
**sources:** Wu, "The Tyranny of Convenience" (*The New York Times*, 16 February 2018); Borgmann, *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life* (University of Chicago Press, 1984).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Wu's essay and Borgmann's device paradigm are well-known positions, citations Perplexity-verified. Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off. (Adjacent empirical backing on automation-offloading lives under automate-or-by-hand.)

## entry: personalise-or-narrow
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** personalise, recommend, tailor, for you, curated, suggestions, relevant, algorithm, feed, customise, smart, predict
**felt as:** personalise it for each user; recommend what they'll like; tailor the feed; show them relevant stuff; make a "for you" view; let the algorithm surface the best; predict what they want next.
**the_tension:** Personalisation promises relevance — show each person what fits them, spare them the irrelevant. But a system that learns what you like is also one that learns what to stop showing you, and "relevant to who you already are" and "narrowing to who you already are" can be the same gesture seen from two sides. When you tailor what someone sees, what do they stop encountering — and does that matter for what you're building?
Pariser's *filter bubble* argues that personalisation algorithms enclose users in a self-reinforcing loop of the already-familiar, thinning the range of views they meet (*The Filter Bubble*, 2011). The empirical picture is genuinely split: individual-level studies do find recommenders narrowing what a person sees over time, yet large-scale work finds the societal "bubble" weak or absent — users often meet a *more* diverse diet than non-users, and effects differ sharply by platform; Bruns argues the bubble has been "severely overstated" (*Are Filter Bubbles Real?*, 2019). The lens asks the student which claim they're leaning on. (The counter holds, and genuinely: personalisation reduces overload and surfaces the buried-but-wanted, and a feed that showed everyone everything would drown most users; relevance is a real service, not only a cage.)
**questions_it_invites:** When you tailor what this user sees, what do they stop encountering — and does that loss matter here? · Is the narrowing you fear a property of the system, or of the person — and which can you design for?
**failure_modes:** tuning for relevance until the user only ever meets a reflection of who they already are; refusing personalisation and leaving users to drown in undifferentiated volume they cannot navigate.
**sources:** Pariser, *The Filter Bubble: What the Internet Is Hiding from You* (Penguin, 2011); Bruns, *Are Filter Bubbles Real?* (Polity, 2019).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (individual narrowing found; societal bubble contested/overstated). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: calm-or-attention-grabbing
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** notify, push, alert, re-engage, badge, streak, reminder, ping, hook, retention, pull back, nudge, attention
**felt as:** send a notification to pull them back; add push alerts and badges; re-engage the user; ping them so they don't forget; nudge them daily; hook them with reminders; keep them coming back.
**the_tension:** The reflex when usage dips is to reach out — a notification, a badge, a reminder to pull the person back. But every signal you send spends something of theirs: a sliver of attention, a small interruption of whatever they were doing. When you decide to notify, are you serving the user's purpose or your retention number — and can you tell the difference from where you sit?
Weiser and Brown's *calm technology* argues that good technology should mostly stay at the periphery and come to the centre only when it genuinely needs to — informing without demanding ("The Coming Age of Calm Technology", 1996; Case, *Calm Technology*, 2015). The interruption literature gives this empirical teeth: notifications impose measurable attentional and emotional costs, yet the same studies show the answer is rarely "send none" — well-timed, batched or boundary-deferred signals keep most of the value while shedding the harm, and removing notifications entirely can raise anxiety and fear-of-missing-out rather than calm. The lens asks where on that spectrum a given signal sits. (The counter holds, and genuinely: a timely reminder, a safety alert, a message a person actually wants can be a real service — silence is not automatically respect, and "we don't notify" can mean a user misses the thing that mattered.)
**questions_it_invites:** Does this signal serve what the user came for, or what you need from them — and would they thank you for it? · If you sent nothing, what would the user lose — and is that loss yours or theirs?
**failure_modes:** sending signals to defend a retention metric until the product becomes one more thing buzzing for attention; stripping out every notification and letting users miss what they genuinely needed to know.
**sources:** Weiser & Brown, "The Coming Age of Calm Technology" (1996); Case, *Calm Technology: Principles and Patterns for Non-Intrusive Design* (O'Reilly, 2015).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; two-sided (interruption costs are real, but well-timed/batched signals retain value and blunt removal can backfire). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: measure-it-or-feel-it
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** measure, track, metric, data, dashboard, kpi, quantify, number, analytics, optimise, target, score, benchmark
**felt as:** let's measure it and track the numbers; make it data-driven; put it on a dashboard; what's the KPI; quantify success; optimise the metric; we need a target to hit; if we can't measure it we can't manage it.
**the_tension:** Reaching for a metric feels like rigour — name a number, track it, optimise it, and you have replaced opinion with evidence. But the moment a measure becomes the target, people and systems start serving the number rather than the thing it stood for, and the unmeasured slips quietly out of view. What is this metric a proxy for — and what happens to everything it cannot see?
What Goodhart observed — and Strathern later phrased as "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (1997) — is borne out across organisations: optimising a single proxy reliably produces gaming, selective attention and distortion of the very thing it was meant to track. Muller calls the broader reflex *metric fixation* — substituting standardised measurement for judgement (*The Tyranny of Metrics*, 2018). Yet the literature does not conclude "stop measuring": the proposed answer is a slate of measures, qualitative accounts alongside the numbers, and metrics used to *check* gaming rather than drive it. The lens asks the student which they are doing. (The counter holds, and genuinely: measurement also disciplines wishful thinking, surfaces what intuition misses, and guards against bias — abandoning metrics can be worse than misusing them, and "we just know it's working" is its own failure.)
**questions_it_invites:** What is this number standing in for — and what does it stop you seeing? · Is the metric here informing your judgement, or replacing it?
**failure_modes:** optimising a single proxy until people serve the number and the real aim erodes; refusing to measure at all and mistaking a hunch for evidence.
**sources:** Muller, *The Tyranny of Metrics* (Princeton University Press, 2018); Goodhart's law (orig. C. Goodhart; the popular wording is M. Strathern, 1997).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; two-sided (proxy-optimisation distorts and gets gamed, but a slate of measures + judgement beats abandoning measurement). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: subtract-or-add
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** add, feature, more, option, configurable, powerful, extra, include, build, expand, capability, another, plus
**felt as:** let's add a feature for that; give them more options; make it configurable and powerful; just add a setting; we could include this too; build in another mode; more is more; what else can we add.
**the_tension:** When something needs improving, the mind reaches first for what to add — a feature, a setting, a mode. Taking something away is rarely the first idea, even when it would help more. Before you add to this, have you actually considered whether removing something would serve it better — or did subtraction never come up?
Adams and colleagues showed experimentally that people systematically default to additive changes and overlook subtractive ones, even when subtraction is the better solution and even at the cost of overburdened systems (*Nature*, 2021; Klotz, *Subtract*, 2021); the bias is implicit and persists unless something cues subtraction. The lens borrows that cue: it asks the student to consider removal as seriously as addition. (The counter holds, and genuinely: subtraction is not automatically virtuous — removing a feature can strip a capability a real user depended on, and "subtraction" can be a way of deflecting work or cost onto someone else rather than improving the thing; less is sometimes just less.)
**questions_it_invites:** Before adding to this, what would removing something do — and did that option even come up? · Who relies on the thing you would take away, and is its removal a gift or a loss to them?
**failure_modes:** piling on features and options because adding is the default move, until the thing collapses under its own weight; removing something that was quietly load-bearing for a user you didn't picture.
**sources:** Adams, Converse, Hales & Klotz, "People systematically overlook subtractive changes" (*Nature*, 2021); Klotz, *Subtract: The Untapped Science of Less* (Flatiron, 2021).
**provenance:** pending

## entry: automate-or-by-hand
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** automate, generate, ai, auto, one-click, script, let the tool, no manual, do it for me, batch, hands-off, effortless
**felt as:** just automate it; let the AI generate it; one click and it's done; script the whole thing; the tool can do it for me; no manual work; make it hands-off; why do it by hand.
**the_tension:** When a task is tedious, automating it looks like obvious progress — the machine does it faster and you move on. But some of what feels like drudgery is also where skill and understanding are built, and a capability you stop exercising is one you slowly lose. Is the work you're about to automate pure friction, or is it where the knowing actually happens — and for whom?
Studies of automation and AI assistance find a recurring pattern: relying on the tool can erode the user's own skill and the tacit knowledge that only forms through doing, often without the person noticing the loss; in design and other fields this "deskilling" is well documented, and some of it accumulates across generations as the entry-level work that taught newcomers gets automated away. Crawford and Sennett make the parallel case for the hand — that manual, skilled work is itself a form of thinking and a source of meaning, not just output (*Shop Class as Soulcraft*, 2009; *The Craftsman*, 2008). (The counter holds, and genuinely: automation also frees people from real drudgery and democratises what once needed an expert — not every skill is worth keeping in the hand, and romanticising "by hand" can gatekeep or simply waste a person's finite time; some tasks should be handed to the machine.)
**questions_it_invites:** Is the work you want to automate pure friction, or where the skill is actually formed — and which is it here? · If the tool does this, what does the person no longer learn — and does that matter for what they're trying to become?
**failure_modes:** automating the very practice through which competence was built, so the skill quietly atrophies; insisting on doing by hand what the machine should carry, burning a person's time on drudgery that taught them nothing.
**sources:** Crawford, *Shop Class as Soulcraft: An Inquiry into the Value of Work* (Penguin, 2009); Sennett, *The Craftsman* (Yale University Press, 2008).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; deskilling-through-reliance confirmed across fields, and the counter (automation frees/democratises; obsolete skills fine to shed) holds. ## entry: the-feed-or-the-archive
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** feed, stream, latest, timeline, new, recent, scroll, real-time, fresh, updates, what's new, infinite, chronological
**felt as:** put it in a feed; show the latest first; make it a stream they can scroll; surface what's new; keep it fresh with updates; real-time timeline; newest at the top; infinite scroll.
**the_tension:** The feed is the default container for content now — newest first, always something more, the old sinking out of sight. It answers "what's new?" superbly and "what lasts?" not at all. Is what you're building meant to be a stream that flows past, or a place things accumulate and can be returned to — and have you chosen, or just reached for the feed?
The *Slow Media Manifesto* argues for media that are timeless rather than merely timely — made to be kept, re-read and built into over years, not consumed once and scrolled past (Köhler, David & Blumtritt, 2010). On this reading, the feed's recency bias quietly devalues everything that isn't recent, and an archive — where material is arranged to be found again, not flushed — is a different kind of design with a different relationship to time. The lens asks which the student is actually making. (The counter holds, and genuinely: for news, conversation, markets or anything where currency *is* the value, the feed is exactly right, and forcing an archive's permanence onto live information would be precious and unhelpful.)
**questions_it_invites:** Is this a stream meant to flow past, or a place things should accumulate and be found again — and which did you choose? · What in here deserves to outlast the moment it appeared, and does the feed let it?
**failure_modes:** pouring lasting material into a feed that buries it the moment it stops being new; freezing genuinely live, time-sensitive information into an archive no one needs to revisit.
**sources:** Köhler, David & Blumtritt, "The Slow Media Manifesto" (2010).

## entry: seamful-or-seamless
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** seamless, invisible, smooth, magic, just works, hide, frictionless, effortless, transparent, behind the scenes, abstract away, polished
**felt as:** make it seamless and invisible; it should just work like magic; hide all the complexity; smooth over the joins; abstract it away; the user shouldn't see how it works; keep it effortless behind the scenes.
**the_tension:** "Seamless" is held up as the highest praise — the user never sees the machinery, it just works. But a system whose workings are completely hidden is also one the user cannot understand, question or repair when it behaves strangely. When you smooth over every join, are you sparing the user complexity they don't need, or hiding the very seams that would let them grasp what's happening?
Chalmers and Galani argue for *seamful* design — deliberately revealing the boundaries, limits and heterogeneity of a system so people can understand and work with them — as a counter to the seamless, "invisible" ideal that Weiser's ubiquitous-computing vision made dominant ("Seamful interweaving", 2004). On this reading, some seams are worth showing: they are where legibility, trust and the ability to recover from failure live. The lens asks which seams a design is hiding and at what cost. (The counter holds, and genuinely: most users most of the time do not want to see the plumbing — seamlessness genuinely reduces cognitive load, and exposing every internal seam can overwhelm rather than empower; hiding complexity is often exactly the service.)
**questions_it_invites:** Which seams are you hiding here — and would showing them help the user understand, or just burden them? · When this behaves unexpectedly, can the user see enough to make sense of it, or only that it "broke"?
**failure_modes:** hiding the workings so completely that users cannot understand or recover when something goes wrong; exposing internal seams that carry no meaning for the user and only add noise.
**sources:** Chalmers & Galani, "Seamful interweaving: heterogeneity in the theory and design of interactive systems" (DIS, 2004); engaging Weiser's calm/ubiquitous-computing ideal.

## entry: finished-or-open-ended
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** finished, complete, polished, resolved, done, final, locked, definitive, ship, fixed, settled, closed
**felt as:** get it finished and polished; ship it when it's complete; lock it down; make it final and resolved; it should feel done; no loose ends; settle the design; close it off.
**the_tension:** A finished, resolved artefact is the usual goal — every decision made, nothing left dangling, shipped as complete. But a thing that is fully closed is also a thing users can only accept or reject, never make their own. Is this something that should arrive finished, or something whose value depends on people adapting it after you've gone — and which are you building?
Dix argues for *designing for appropriation* — deliberately leaving room, ambiguity and "loose fit" so users can bend a tool to purposes the designer never foresaw ("Designing for Appropriation", 2007); Dourish's study of how people repurpose technologies makes the same case empirically (2003). On this reading, over-finishing can foreclose the very adaptation that would have made a thing useful and lived-in. The lens asks whether completeness here is a virtue or a closing-off. (The counter holds, and genuinely: open-endedness can also be an abdication — users often want a clear, resolved thing that simply works, and "we left it open for the community" can be a cover for decisions the designer should have made; sometimes finished is exactly the gift.)
**questions_it_invites:** Should this arrive finished, or stay open enough for people to make it their own — and which does it actually need? · Is the openness here an invitation to appropriate, or a decision you've avoided making?
**failure_modes:** polishing a thing shut so users can only take it or leave it, foreclosing the adaptation that would have made it theirs; leaving open what the designer should have resolved, and calling indecision "flexibility".
**sources:** Dix, "Designing for Appropriation" (*Proc. BCS HCI 2007 / People and Computers XXI*); Dourish, "The Appropriation of Interactive Technologies: Some Lessons from Placeless Documents" (*CSCW*, 2003).

## entry: the-new-model-or-the-lasting-thing
**discipline:** slow-design
**vocabulary:** upgrade, new model, latest, version, replace, disposable, turnover, cheap, next, refresh, retire, obsolete, swap
**felt as:** ship the next model; time to upgrade; release a new version; make it cheap and replaceable; encourage them to swap up; retire the old one; refresh the line; the latest is better.
**the_tension:** The product rhythm of a newer model every cycle feels like progress — and it drives sales. But each upgrade that retires a still-working thing produces waste, and "obsolete" is often a perception you designed rather than a fact about the object. When you push the new version, is the old one genuinely spent, or have you decided for the user that it should feel that way?
Slade and Packard documented *planned obsolescence* — designing the upgrade cycle to limit a product's useful life and keep replacement flowing (*Made to Break*, 2006; *The Waste Makers*, 1960); Chapman extends the critique to the psychological obsolescence by which still-working things come to feel like junk (*Meaningful Stuff*, 2021). The empirical picture, though, is not one-sided: studies find that extending lifespan is usually the greener choice and that much "obsolescence" is perceived rather than functional — yet for energy-using goods, efficiency gains can genuinely favour replacement, refurbished devices carry real reliability limits, and the obsolescence *narrative* itself can backfire by making people trust working devices less. The lens asks the student to separate functional from manufactured obsolescence here. (The counter holds: sometimes the new model is genuinely better, safer or more efficient, and clinging to the old thing is nostalgia rather than care.)
**questions_it_invites:** Is the old version genuinely spent, or have you designed it to *feel* spent — and can you tell the difference? · If this lasted twice as long, who would lose and who would gain — and is that trade one you can defend?
**failure_modes:** driving an upgrade cycle that retires still-working things and calls manufactured obsolescence progress; keeping an old design alive out of nostalgia when a newer one would genuinely serve the user better.
**sources:** Slade, *Made to Break: Technology and Obsolescence in America* (Harvard University Press, 2006); Packard, *The Waste Makers* (1960); Chapman, *Meaningful Stuff* (MIT Press, 2021).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; two-sided (designed/psychological obsolescence drives waste; yet replacement is sometimes greener/safer, and the obsolescence narrative can backfire). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.
