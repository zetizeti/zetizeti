## entry: pricing-creative-work
**discipline:** entrepreneurship
**vocabulary:** price, pricing, rate, hourly, day rate, quote, estimate, budget, cost, fee, charge, worth, value, billable, scope, undercharge
**felt as:** what should i charge; how do i price this; i charge by the hour; just quote a day rate; i don't know what it's worth; i undercharged again; how do you put a number on design.
**the_tension:** Two ways to put a price on design. One bills inputs — hours, days, cost-plus-margin — and feels safe and fair. The other prices the *value* created for the client — the outcome, the risk shifted, the position won — and decouples the fee from the time it took. Blair Enns (*Pricing Creativity*, 2018) argues creative firms should leave the billable hour behind and price on value, because billing time caps the reward at your speed and quietly tells the client the work is a commodity measured in hours. Mike Monteiro (*Design Is a Job*, 2012) adds that designers usually know what they should charge and are simply afraid to ask. The counter is real: value pricing demands you can name and defend the value, the client must believe it, and for routine, low-stakes or commodity work, time-and-materials is honest and simple — not every job is a value conversation. The student's move is to ask which kind of work this is, and whether the hourly habit is protecting the client or sparing them a harder conversation.
**failure_modes:** pricing every job by the hour because it is safe, capping the reward at your speed; quoting a value price you cannot actually justify, so the number feels arbitrary; dressing a routine, low-stakes job in value-talk to inflate the fee or dodge giving a simple number; undercharging out of fear and calling it "being competitive."
**questions_it_invites:** Are you billing the time this took, or the value it creates — and which does this job actually call for? · If you couldn't mention hours at all, how would you explain what this is worth — and where is the hours figure simply the honest answer?
**sources:** Enns, *Pricing Creativity: A Guide to Profit Beyond the Billable Hour* (2018); Monteiro, *Design Is a Job* (A Book Apart, 2012).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — value-vs-time pricing (Enns) and "charge what you're worth / fear of asking" (Monteiro) are the central, Perplexity-confirmed theses of both works. Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers; value-based pricing is empirically linked to firm performance — Liozu et al. 2013, the first quantitative demonstration — yet the counter holds: >80% of firms still price on cost or time, adoption is low with a high failure rate, and fixed-fee or time-based schemes perform near-optimally in many service settings — see verification-consensus-backing.md).

## entry: free-pitching-vs-paid-expertise
**discipline:** entrepreneurship
**vocabulary:** pitch, pitching, spec, speculative, free, competition, RFP, proposal, win the work, prove it, concepts, mockups, audition, unpaid
**felt as:** they want to see ideas before they'll pay; should i pitch for free to win it; everyone's pitching for this; just throw together some concepts; i'll do it on spec; it's a competition; how else do i get the work.
**the_tension:** To win the work, should a studio give away its thinking up front — speculative concepts, free pitches, competition entries — or refuse, and sell its expertise from a position of authority? Blair Enns (*The Win Without Pitching Manifesto*, 2010) argues that free pitching commoditises creative work and inverts the expert relationship — the doctor does not audition by prescribing for free — and calls for replacing the unpaid pitch with paid diagnosis: being paid to think before anyone commits to a solution. Mike Monteiro (*Design Is a Job*, 2012) reinforces the designer's right to refuse terms that devalue the work. The counter is the pipeline reality: for an unknown or junior studio, refusing every pitch can mean refusing to eat, and some pitches are genuine shop windows. The student's question is whether this pitch is building their authority or spending it — and who the free labour actually serves.
**failure_modes:** giving away the thinking that *is* the product, then being hired merely to execute it; entering a race to the bottom where whoever works most for free wins; refusing all pitches so absolutely that an unknown studio is never seen.
**questions_it_invites:** When you pitch for free, what are you giving away — and is it the thing they should be paying for, or a worthwhile way to be seen? · For a studio no one knows yet, what does refusing every pitch cost — and how would you tell a worthwhile shop-window from spent expertise?
**sources:** Enns, *The Win Without Pitching Manifesto* (2010); Monteiro, *Design Is a Job* (A Book Apart, 2012).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — anti-speculative-pitching / paid-expertise is Enns's central, Perplexity-confirmed thesis. Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers; the anti-spec movement within design is empirically documented — Kennedy 2013 — unpaid/spec work displaces others in the sector — Siebert & Wilson 2013 — and unpaid placements are found *not* to be stepping stones — Hunt et al. 2023; yet the counter has real weight: design competitions can spur more original work up to a point — Gross 2018, on commercial logo contests — see verification-consensus-backing.md).

## entry: scale-vs-craft
**discipline:** entrepreneurship
**vocabulary:** scale, grow, growth, bigger, hire, team, agency, studio, expand, more clients, headcount, efficiency, systematise, boutique, manage
**felt as:** i want to grow the studio; should i hire and scale up; take on more clients; we need to systematise; bigger means more impact; i don't want to just manage; will it still feel like my work.
**the_tension:** Should a studio grow — more people, more clients, more revenue — or stay small to protect the craft and the hand in the work? E. F. Schumacher (*Small Is Beautiful*, 1973) challenges the reflex that bigger is always better, arguing for *appropriate scale* — the size that fits the work and the people, not the maximum the market allows. Adrian Shaughnessy (*How to Be a Graphic Designer, Without Losing Your Soul*, 2005) traces how studio size and a growing client roster pull a designer from making toward managing, and warns against the designer dwindling into a voiceless executor of other people's briefs. Roger Martin's reliability-versus-validity distinction (*The Design of Business*, 2009) sharpens why: scale rewards reliability — repeatable, efficient, standardised process — which quietly crowds out the exploratory, validity-seeking work design lives on. The counter is honest too, and more than intuition: large firms are found to be a major engine of innovation, spending more and using it more productively (Knott & Vieregger), and creative studios often grow *because of*, not despite, their quality focus (Loots and colleagues) — scale buys reach, resilience, and work a soloist could never take on, and "staying small to stay pure" can be a comfortable excuse. The student's move is to ask what growth is *for*, and what it would cost the work.
**failure_modes:** scaling for its own sake until the founder only manages and never makes; staying small out of fear — turning down work you could do well, leaving the studio fragile and its reach narrow; growing the client list faster than the studio can hold its standards.
**questions_it_invites:** What is growing the studio *for* — and what would it cost, or gain, for the part of the work you most care about? · At what size would this stop being the work you wanted — and is staying small protecting the work, or protecting you from the risk of growing?
**sources:** Schumacher, *Small Is Beautiful: A Study of Economics as if People Mattered* (1973); Shaughnessy, *How to Be a Graphic Designer, Without Losing Your Soul* (2005); Martin, *The Design of Business* (Harvard Business Press, 2009).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high (citations Perplexity-confirmed); three-pass-revised 24 May to rebalance toward the growth pole. ⚠ sign-off watch: this tension sits close to the project's own density-not-mass view and the passes flagged it tilting "stay small" — keep the growth pole genuinely weighted. Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers) and the growth pole is now empirically anchored: large firms are found to be the major engine of innovation/growth (Knott & Vieregger 2020), and creative-industry firms grow *because of*, not despite, their quality focus (Loots et al. 2022); the craft pole holds too (diseconomies of scale and bureaucratic drag — Link et al. 1990; more innovation per R&D dollar in small firms — Plehn-Dujowich 2009). The size–innovation relationship is genuinely contested in the literature, which is the honest shape for this tension — see verification-consensus-backing.md.

## entry: author-vs-service-provider
**discipline:** entrepreneurship
**vocabulary:** author, voice, style, signature, service, client, brief, solve, mediate, hired, point of view, identity, originality, execute
**felt as:** is this my work or theirs; i'm just executing their brief; where's my voice in this; i'm a hired hand; should i have a signature style; do i solve their problem or make my statement; i lost myself in client work.
**the_tension:** Is the designer an *author* — a maker with a voice, a point of view, work that is recognisably theirs — or a *service professional* whose job is to solve the client's problem and mediate users' and stakeholders' needs, voice mostly set aside? Adrian Shaughnessy (*How to Be a Graphic Designer, Without Losing Your Soul*, 2005) holds that a designer can keep a distinctive voice and ethical stance inside client work, and warns against dissolving into the brief until nothing of you remains. Mike Monteiro (*Design Is a Job*, 2012) presses the other way: design is a service, and the professional's task is to solve the problem and justify the decisions to the client — not to perform mystique or treat the brief as a canvas for self-expression. The discriminating question is which register this project is in, and whether "expressing my voice" is serving the work or hijacking it — and whether "just serving the brief" is professionalism or self-erasure.
**failure_modes:** treating a client's problem as a canvas for personal style the brief never asked for; dissolving so completely into service work that no point of view is left to bring; mistaking a signature style for having something to say.
**questions_it_invites:** In this project, is your voice serving the problem or competing with it? · If none of this carried your signature, would that be a loss — or simply the job done well?
**sources:** Shaughnessy, *How to Be a Graphic Designer, Without Losing Your Soul* (2005); Monteiro, *Design Is a Job* (A Book Apart, 2012).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both theses Perplexity-confirmed (Shaughnessy: voice/soul within service; Monteiro: design-as-service). Consensus-checked (20 papers; the designer-as-author debate is documented in design scholarship — McCarthy 2011 traces its formal emergence, Sadokierski 2006 its two definitions — and the service pole gets an empirical anchor: professional designers are found to regard their clients as co-authors of the final work — Falcão & Nicolau 2017. Note: these are design-studies sources documenting the debate, not empirical-science findings, with the one noted exception — see verification-consensus-backing.md).

## entry: specialist-or-full-service
**discipline:** entrepreneurship
**vocabulary:** specialist, generalist, niche, positioning, full-service, focus, expert, broad, narrow, vertical, differentiate, range, services
**felt as:** should i specialise or offer everything; pick a niche or stay full-service; i don't want to turn work away; the specialist charges more; being a generalist is safer; what should my studio be known for; if i narrow down i'll lose clients.
**the_tension:** A new studio's instinct is to stay broad — offer every service, take every client, turn nothing away. David C. Baker (*The Business of Expertise*, 2017) argues the opposite builds a stronger practice: positioning as a narrow specialist concentrates pattern-recognition, builds authority, and commands premium fees, because a focused expert is rare while a generalist is a substitute for many others. The evidence is real but conditional — specialists do command premiums and outperform in many professional-service settings, *and* generalists win where networks are rich in brokerage opportunities, where a field changes slowly, or where clients hold strong bargaining power; the worst place to sit is "stuck in the middle", neither focused nor broad. The student's move is to ask what positioning this practice can actually own, and whether narrowing would concentrate their authority or simply shrink their market in a place that rewards range. (Both poles hold: a specialist commands more and is remembered for one thing; a generalist is resilient, flexible, and can serve a market too thin to sustain a niche — and narrowing too early, before you know what you're good at, is its own trap.)
**questions_it_invites:** What could this practice genuinely own as a specialist — and would narrowing concentrate your authority, or just shrink a market that rewards range? · Where is "full-service" real resilience and flexibility, and where is it a refusal to decide what you're for?
**failure_modes:** staying broad and undifferentiated until the studio is a substitute for many others and competes only on price; narrowing into a niche too early or too tightly, before knowing what you're good at or whether the market sustains it; sitting "stuck in the middle", neither focused enough to command a premium nor broad enough to be resilient.
**sources:** Baker, *The Business of Expertise: How Entrepreneurial Experts Convert Insight to Impact + Wealth* (2017).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided and contingent (specialist premium/outperformance — Spaenjers, Castaldi, Casterella — vs generalist advantage under brokerage / slow change / client bargaining power, and the "stuck in the middle" risk — Shipilov, Teodoridis, Adner). See verification-consensus-backing.md. (Relates to money-career's specialist-or-generalist — the individual's skills; here it is studio/practice positioning.) ## entry: own-product-or-client-services
**discipline:** entrepreneurship
**vocabulary:** product, service, client, services, productise, ip, recurring, scale, bootstrap, build, equity, agency, side project
**felt as:** should we build our own product; client work pays but doesn't scale; i want to make our own thing; productise the service; keep doing client work or bet on a product; build ip we own; the side project could be the business.
**the_tension:** A design studio lives on client services — reliable, but it trades time for money and scales only by hiring. The recurring temptation is to build *its own* product: something it owns, that can sell many times without more hours, that builds equity rather than only income. Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson (*Rework*, 2010) make the case for the product path — start something you own, stay lean, let it pay for itself. The counter is the studio's daily reality: client services fund the lights now and carry low risk, while a product demands sustained investment, pulls focus and talent away from paying work, and most fail — and "productising" can quietly hollow out the bespoke craft that made the studio good. The discriminating move is the *dual* one most studios actually run: a stable services practice that funds and de-risks a product built on its side. The student's question is what this product is *for* — owning equity and escaping the time-for-money ceiling, or fleeing client work they have not learned to do well — and whether the services can carry it without being starved.
**questions_it_invites:** Is building your own product a move toward owning what you make, or an escape from client work you haven't yet mastered — and which is it here? · Can the services practice fund and survive the product's long, uncertain build — or would the bet starve the work that pays?
**failure_modes:** betting the studio on a product while the services that fund it are starved of attention and decline; staying purely in time-for-money services, capped at what hours allow, never building anything you own; productising until the bespoke craft that made the studio worth hiring is hollowed out.
**sources:** Fried & Hansson, *Rework* (Crown Business, 2010); and the documented services-to-product / "productised services" debate in creative practice.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the services-vs-product / productisation debate is a recognised position in creative-business discourse (Perplexity-verified; *Rework* the popular anchor), the dual-model reality well documented. Position-in-named-works (strategy discourse, not a contested empirical claim): citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: bootstrap-or-raise
**discipline:** entrepreneurship
**vocabulary:** bootstrap, raise, investment, funding, investor, vc, venture, equity, self-fund, capital, scale, control, growth, runway
**felt as:** should i raise money or bootstrap; take investment to grow faster; i don't want to give up control; we need capital to scale; bootstrapping is too slow; investors would speed it up; can i build this without raising; growth needs funding.
**the_tension:** To grow faster, should a venture raise outside capital — investment that buys speed, scale and expertise — or bootstrap, funding growth from its own revenue to keep control and discipline? Eric Ries's *Lean Startup* logic (2011) and the venture model hold that for a business with real scale potential, capital and investor expertise accelerate growth and reach a size self-funding never could — and the evidence shows venture-backed firms reach far larger scale and lower early-stage failure. Jason Fried and David Heinemeier Hansson (*Rework*, 2010) argue the opposite for most ventures: bootstrapping forces financial discipline, keeps the founder in control, and avoids the growth-at-all-costs pressure investors impose — and the evidence is genuinely equivocal, with bootstrappers showing comparable outcomes and *less* failure variance, while venture-backed firms often show worse profitability and ceded control. The student's move is to ask what this venture actually needs capital *for* — a genuine scale opportunity with a closing window, or a substitute for revenue it has not yet learned to earn — and what raising would cost in control and in the kind of company it becomes. (Both poles hold: for a real scale opportunity, refusing capital can mean losing the race; for most ventures, raising trades away control and discipline for speed they did not need.)
**questions_it_invites:** What does this venture need capital *for* — a real scale opportunity with a closing window, or a substitute for revenue it hasn't yet learned to earn? · What would raising cost in control, discipline and the kind of company this becomes — and is that price worth the speed?
**failure_modes:** raising to chase growth the business didn't need, ceding control and bending to investors' growth-at-all-costs clock; bootstrapping so cautiously that a genuine, time-limited scale opportunity is lost to a funded competitor; treating either raising or bootstrapping as the braver or purer choice regardless of what the venture is.
**sources:** Fried & Hansson, *Rework* (Crown Business, 2010, the bootstrapping case); Ries, *The Lean Startup* (Crown Business, 2011, the build-measure-learn / scaling case).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (VC-backed firms reach larger scale and lower early failure — Puri & Zarutskie, Colombo — while bootstrappers show comparable outcomes, less failure variance, and retain control, and VC-backed often show worse profitability — Rutherford, Bhide, Bronzini). See verification-consensus-backing.md. ⚠ Sign-off watch: the project's own non-profit / anti-venture-scale orientation must NOT tilt this into an anti-raising verdict — both poles are genuinely weighted; the student decides.
