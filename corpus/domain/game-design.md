## entry: meaningful-play
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** meaningful play, choice, decision, outcome, consequence, matters, feedback, integrated, discernable, options, button, does it matter, pointless
**felt as:** the choices don't feel like they matter; you click but nothing really changes; there are loads of options but none of them count; players can't tell what their move did; it's just busywork; does any of this actually affect anything; the decisions feel fake.
**the_tension:** Salen & Zimmerman (*Rules of Play*, 2003) argue a game's play becomes *meaningful* only when a player's action has an outcome that is both **discernable** (they can perceive what it did) and **integrated** (it connects into the larger system of the game's actions and outcomes, including later) — by which measure many "choices" in a design fail one test or the other: visible but inconsequential, or consequential but invisible. The counter-pull is that not every action must carry weight: ambient, low-stakes, even pointless interactions (idle fiddling, cosmetic options, texture) are part of what makes a world feel alive and inhabitable, and a design where *everything* must matter can become airless and exhausting. The student's move is to ask, of a given choice, whether the player can see what it did and whether it will matter later — and whether this particular action needs to be meaningful at all, or is doing some other kind of work.
**failure_modes:** choices that are visible but change nothing (false agency); consequences the player cannot perceive, so the choice feels arbitrary; insisting every interaction carry weight until the game is airless and humourless.
**questions_it_invites:** When the player makes this choice, can they tell what it did — and will it matter later? · Does this action need to be meaningful, or is it doing something else — texture, rhythm, breathing room?
**sources:** Salen & Zimmerman, *Rules of Play: Game Design Fundamentals* (MIT Press, 2003) — the concept of *meaningful play* (discernable + integrated outcomes).
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** high — *meaningful play* (discernable + integrated) is the central organising concept of Salen & Zimmerman (2003); position-in-named-work, citation-verified (MIT Press **2003**, not 2004). Counter-pole (not everything must matter) is a design judgement, argued not smuggled.

## entry: mechanics-dynamics-aesthetics
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** mechanics, dynamics, aesthetics, rules, behaviour, experience, feel, emergent, system, fun, designer intent, MDA, what the player feels
**felt as:** i built the rules but it doesn't feel how i wanted; the system does its own thing once people play; i can't control the experience directly; the mechanics are fine but it's not fun; what i designed and what they feel are different; how do i design a feeling.
**the_tension:** The MDA framework (Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek, 2004) splits a game into **mechanics** (the rules and components the designer actually builds), **dynamics** (the run-time behaviour that emerges when those rules meet players), and **aesthetics** (the felt experience — the "fun"). Its sharp claim is that designer and player traverse these in *opposite* directions: the designer can only build mechanics and must reason *forwards* to a hoped-for experience, while the player meets the aesthetics first and rarely sees the mechanics at all — so you never author a feeling directly, only the rules that might produce it. The counter is that this neat causal chain can mislead: experience also comes from fiction, art, sound, social context and the player's own history, none of which reduce to mechanics, and treating "fun" as the output of a rules-pipeline can make a designer over-engineer systems while neglecting theme, story and the room the game is played in. The student's move is to ask whether they are tuning the *mechanics* when the problem actually lives in the *dynamics* or the *aesthetics* — and what carries the experience here that no rule controls.
**failure_modes:** tuning rules endlessly when the felt problem lives in emergent dynamics or in theme/fiction; assuming a desired aesthetic can be specified directly rather than coaxed out of mechanics; ignoring everything (art, sound, social setting) that shapes experience but is not a mechanic.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the thing you want to change a matter of the rules, of how they behave once people play, or of how it feels — and which of those are you actually working on? · What is carrying the experience here that no rule of yours controls?
**sources:** Hunicke, LeBlanc & Zubek, "MDA: A Formal Approach to Game Design and Game Research", Challenges in Game AI Workshop, 19th National Conference on Artificial Intelligence (AAAI Press, 2004).
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** high — MDA is a well-known, correctly-attributed framework (citation-verifier confirmed authorship + AAAI 2004 venue, eds. Fu & Orkin). The opposite-traversal claim is the paper's own. Counter-pole (experience exceeds mechanics) argued.

## entry: the-magic-circle
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** magic circle, play space, separate, real world, just a game, stakes, real money, identity, safe, bleed, boundary, consequences, pretend, harassment
**felt as:** it's just a game, it shouldn't affect real life; but real money is involved; what happens in the game spills into how people treat each other; is this a safe separate space or not; the stakes feel real even though it's pretend; where does the game end and life begin.
**the_tension:** Huizinga (*Homo Ludens*, 1938) described play as happening inside a bounded space and time — a "magic circle" (the term elaborated by Salen & Zimmerman, 2003) within which special rules hold and ordinary life is suspended; the idea frames the game world as a protected zone where players can experiment, fail and role-play without ordinary consequence — which is part of what makes a game a safe place to try things. Consalvo ("There Is No Magic Circle", 2009) argues the other way: the boundary is porous — money, reputation, emotion, cheating and harassment can cross it — so treating the circle as sealed may let a design overlook the real-world stakes some games carry. The student's move is to ask what their design treats as "inside the game, so it doesn't count" — what that protected space gives the people playing, and where real consequences are crossing the boundary anyway.
**failure_modes:** treating the game as a sealed safe space while real money, harassment or addiction cross the boundary; designing as if "it's just a game" settles the question of real-world consequence; over-correcting until nothing is allowed to be playful, low-stakes or pretend.
**questions_it_invites:** What does treating this as "inside the game, so it doesn't count" give the people playing — what can they do here that they couldn't if it counted? · And where it counts anyway — money, feelings, reputation crossing back out — has the design noticed, or is it leaning on a boundary that isn't holding?
**sources:** Huizinga, *Homo Ludens: A Study of the Play-Element in Culture* (1938; Eng. Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1949); Salen & Zimmerman, *Rules of Play* (2003) — the "magic circle"; Consalvo, "There Is No Magic Circle", *Games and Culture* 4(4) (2009).
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** high — the magic circle (Huizinga / Salen & Zimmerman) and Consalvo's named critique are well-documented and citation-verified (Consalvo DOI 10.1177/1555412009343575 confirmed; Huizinga subtitle is "in **Culture**", not "in Society"). Two-sided debate in the literature; the entry's framing was de-loaded in verification (title trimmed from "-and-its-leakage"; "licenses"→"frames"; questions rebalanced so one articulates what the protected space gives players) — see the sign-off sheet. Neither the sealed nor the porous reading is privileged.

## entry: flow-or-designed-difficulty
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** flow, difficulty, challenge, skill, too hard, too easy, frustration, balance, mastery, give up, in the zone, fair, punishing, rage quit
**felt as:** it's too hard and people give up; it's too easy and they get bored; i want them in the zone; the difficulty curve feels off; should it be frustrating or smooth; the hard part is the whole point; players rage quit; how punishing should it be.
**the_tension:** Csikszentmihalyi's *flow* (1990) gives designers a tempting target: keep challenge and skill in balance and the player enters an absorbed, optimal state — too hard breeds anxiety, too easy breeds boredom, so smooth the curve to hold them in the channel. But the flow model is contested even in psychology (the challenge–skill balance is only partially supported, and "control" states can be more enjoyable than flow itself), and games complicate it further: Juul (*The Art of Failure*, 2013) argues that the *pain* of failing is not a bug to be smoothed away but part of why winning means anything — designed difficulty, even harshness, can produce meaning that frictionless flow cannot. The student's move is to ask whether the difficulty here is serving the player's absorption or their *investment* — and whether smoothing the frustration away would remove something that makes mastering it matter, or an obstacle that was never serving the player at all.
**failure_modes:** smoothing difficulty until nothing is at stake and victory means nothing; punishing difficulty that reads as unfair or inaccessible rather than meaningful; assuming "flow" is the only good experience and treating all frustration as a defect.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this difficulty there to keep the player absorbed, or to make what they achieve mean something — and are those the same here? · If you smoothed this frustration away, what would change in what the player feels when they finally get it — and would something be lost, or nothing?
**sources:** Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow: The Psychology of Optimal Experience* (Harper & Row, 1990); Juul, *The Art of Failure: An Essay on the Pain of Playing Video Games* (MIT Press, 2013).
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** medium — flow and Juul's art-of-failure are correctly-attributed, citation-verified positions. Consensus-checked: the challenge–skill balance is only *partially* supported and the four-channel model is directly challenged (Engeser 2008; Lambert 2013), so the entry deliberately frames flow as a contested target, not settled science. See verification-consensus-backing.md.

## entry: the-unnecessary-obstacle
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** rules, obstacle, inefficient, constraint, shortcut, the long way, voluntary, point of the game, friction, easier way, cheating, why not just
**felt as:** why can't players just take the shortcut; the rules make it harder than it needs to be; there's an obvious easier way to win; the constraint seems pointless; why not let them skip it; the inefficiency is annoying; they keep trying to break the rules to win faster.
**the_tension:** Bernard Suits (*The Grasshopper*, 1978) defined playing a game as the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles — golf is hard *on purpose*; the rules forbid the efficient means (carrying the ball to the hole) precisely so the inefficient means (striking it with clubs) becomes an activity worth doing, and players take up what Suits calls the *lusory attitude*: accepting the rules just so the activity they make possible can exist. The radical consequence for a designer is that **the obstacle is not a problem to be solved but the substance of the game itself** — remove the friction and you remove the game. The counter is that not every inefficiency is a beloved unnecessary obstacle: some friction is just bad design — confusing rules, tedious bookkeeping, fiddly setup — that players endure rather than enjoy, and "it's meant to be hard" can excuse genuine clumsiness. The student's move is to ask whether a given difficulty is the *constitutive* obstacle players came for, or incidental friction they are tolerating — and whether removing it would deepen the game or dissolve it.
**failure_modes:** stripping out the "inefficiency" that *was* the game, leaving a frictionless non-activity; defending tedious or confusing friction as essential "challenge" when it is incidental; reading players' rule-breaking as a verdict on the rules rather than asking which obstacle they actually value.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this difficulty the obstacle players actually came to overcome, or friction they are putting up with to get to the game? · If you let them take the efficient shortcut, would the game get better — or would there be no game left?
**sources:** Suits, *The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia* (University of Toronto Press, 1978) — the lusory attitude; playing a game as the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles.
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** high — Suits's definition is canonical and correctly attributed (citation-verified, University of Toronto Press 1978). Framed as his idea (the short formulation is a genuine Suits phrase). Counter-pole (not all friction is constitutive) argued. Strong board + digital relevance.

## entry: emergence-or-progression
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** emergence, progression, levels, possibility space, replayability, rules, content, systems, linear, sequence, story mode, replay value, depth, roguelike
**felt as:** players finish it once and never come back; do i build content or systems for this; it's the same every time; i keep having to make more levels; should there be a fixed path or should it generate itself; how do i get replay value.
**the_tension:** Juul ("The Open and the Closed", 2002; *Half-Real*, 2005) distinguishes games of **emergence** — a few rules combining into a vast space of situations (chess, Go, poker, *The Sims*, roguelikes) — from games of **progression** — a designed sequence of challenges the player advances through (story-driven levels, adventure games). Emergence buys huge variety and replayability from little authored content, but the designer cannot fully control or foresee what arises; progression buys authorial control over pacing, narrative and the exact experience, but every hour of play must be hand-built and is largely spent once. The student's move is to ask whether the experience they want comes from *combinable rules* or from an *authored sequence* — and whether they are paying the right cost: unpredictability and balancing, or content that is consumed and gone.
**failure_modes:** hand-authoring linear content for a game whose life should come from emergent systems (and exhausting it in one play); leaning on emergence where the experience actually needs authored pacing and story, and getting shapeless mush; refusing to choose, so the game is neither deep as a system nor satisfying as a sequence.
**questions_it_invites:** Does the experience you want come from rules combining in ways you didn't script, or from a sequence you author and control? · What are you willing to pay — the unpredictability of emergence, or content that is largely spent after one play?
**sources:** Juul, "The Open and the Closed: Games of Emergence and Games of Progression", in Mäyrä (ed.), *Computer Games and Digital Cultures Conference Proceedings* (Tampere University Press, 2002), 323–329; Juul, *Half-Real: Video Games between Real Rules and Fictional Worlds* (MIT Press, 2005).
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** high — emergence/progression is Juul's well-known, correctly-attributed distinction (citation-verified: 2002 proceedings pp. 323–329, ed. Mäyrä; 2005 MIT Press). Two-sided by construction (each pole carries a named cost).

## entry: procedural-rhetoric
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** procedural rhetoric, argument, meaning, message, rules, system, persuade, model, represents, politics, theme, point, says something, preachy
**felt as:** i want the game to say something; the message isn't landing; players just optimise and ignore the point; how does a game make an argument; the theme is in the story but not the rules; it's preachy; does the system itself mean anything.
**the_tension:** Ian Bogost (*Persuasive Games*, 2007) argues that games make arguments not through words or images but through their **procedures** — the rules and systems themselves embody a claim about how some part of the world works, a "procedural rhetoric" that persuades by letting the player *operate* a model rather than read a thesis. On Bogost's account this gives a designer a distinctive expressive channel: a game about labour, debt or bureaucracy can argue through what it makes the player do. The counter is that procedural arguments are notoriously slippery — players optimise, exploit, ignore or misread the system, taking away "win faster" where the designer intended "feel the trap" — and meaning in games also lives heavily in fiction, art and writing, so leaning entirely on procedure can produce an argument no one actually receives. The student's move is to ask what their *rules* (not their story) are claiming about the world — and whether a player chasing the win will read that claim or optimise straight past it.
**failure_modes:** assuming the procedural argument lands as intended when players are optimising past it; loading the message into story/text while the rules quietly argue the opposite (ludonarrative dissonance); dismissing fiction and art as mere decoration on top of "real" procedural meaning.
**questions_it_invites:** What is the *system* you've built claiming about the world — separately from anything the story says? · A player trying hard to win — what do they actually take away from the system you built: the claim you meant, or something else?
**sources:** Bogost, *Persuasive Games: The Expressive Power of Videogames* (MIT Press, 2007) — the concept of *procedural rhetoric*.
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** high — procedural rhetoric is Bogost's correctly-attributed coinage (citation-verified, MIT Press 2007). Counter (arguments misread / optimised past; meaning also in fiction) argued, not smuggled.

## entry: story-or-system
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** story, narrative, system, rules, plot, ludology, narratology, cutscene, gameplay, mechanics, fiction, characters, is it a story
**felt as:** is this fundamentally a story or a game; the story and the gameplay fight each other; players skip the cutscenes; should i lead with narrative or mechanics; it's a great story but boring to play; the rules say one thing, the plot says another; do games even need stories.
**the_tension:** An old and productive argument runs through game studies: are games best understood as **stories** (Murray, *Hamlet on the Holodeck*, 1997 — the computer as a narrative medium) or as **systems** (the emphasis of Aarseth, *Cybertext*, 1997, and Juul — games as rule-structures that fiction dresses)? For a designer this is not academic: lead with narrative and you can move players deeply but may build a "story with chores attached" that they click past to reach the next scene; lead with system and you can build something endlessly playable but possibly cold, where the fiction is a thin skin over numbers. The two can also reinforce — when what the player *does* and what the story *says* agree, each deepens the other. The student's move is to ask what is really carrying this game — the unfolding of a story or the working of a system — and whether the other is supporting it or fighting it.
**failure_modes:** bolting story onto a system as cutscenes players skip; building rich systems with fiction so thin the game feels meaningless; letting the narrative and the mechanics argue opposite things (the player rewarded for what the story condemns).
**questions_it_invites:** What does the story do here that the system can't, and what does the system do that the story can't — and which is the player actually here for? · Where the two meet, do they reinforce each other, or is one fighting the other?
**sources:** Murray, *Hamlet on the Holodeck: The Future of Narrative in Cyberspace* (Free Press, 1997); Aarseth, *Cybertext: Perspectives on Ergodic Literature* (Johns Hopkins University Press, 1997); Juul, *Half-Real* (MIT Press, 2005).
**provenance:** verified
**framing_confidence:** high — the ludology/narratology emphases and these named works are well-documented and citation-verified. Framed as a live, two-sided debate (the entry declares no winner) and deliberately avoids the overblown "war" caricature — differing emphases, not enemies.

## entry: agency-and-authored-experience
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** agency, freedom, choice, authored, control, open world, railroad, designer intent, branching, meaningful action, linear, my choices, narrative paradox
**felt as:** i want players to feel free but also tell a story; giving them freedom breaks the experience i designed; open world but the plot falls apart; how much choice should they really have; the branching is unmanageable; players go off and miss the good part; freedom versus the experience i want them to have.
**the_tension:** Janet Murray (*Hamlet on the Holodeck*, 1997) named **agency** — the satisfying feeling of taking meaningful action and seeing it matter — as a core pleasure of digital media, and it pulls designers toward giving players ever more freedom and consequence. But agency sits in direct tension with **authorship**: the more genuinely free the player, the less the designer can shape pacing, surprise, theme or a crafted arc (the "narrative paradox") — total freedom can yield a world where nothing the designer cared about reliably happens, while a tightly authored experience can deliver a masterpiece the player merely walks through, feeling railroaded. The student's move is to ask where the felt power of *acting* matters most in this design, and where the shape of an *authored* experience matters more — and whether the freedom they are offering is real agency or just the appearance of choice.
**failure_modes:** offering so much freedom that the authored experience never reliably happens; railroading so tightly that the player feels like a spectator holding a controller; faking agency with choices that visibly change nothing (which players quickly feel).
**questions_it_invites:** Where in this design does the player's felt power to *act* matter most — and where does the shape of an experience you author matter more? · Is the freedom you're offering real consequence, or the appearance of choice the player will see through?
**sources:** Murray, *Hamlet on the Holodeck: The Future of Narrative in Cyberspace* (Free Press, 1997) — *agency* as a core aesthetic of digital narrative.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — agency is Murray's correctly-attributed concept (citation-verified, Free Press 1997). The agency-vs-authorship ("narrative paradox") tension is standard and genuinely two-sided. Distinct from interaction-design's direct-manipulation-vs-agents (there the axis is manipulation vs delegation) and from this file's story-or-system (there the axis is the game's medium-identity — story vs ruleset); here the axis is the player's freedom vs the designer's authored arc.

## entry: game-feel-and-juiciness
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** game feel, juice, feedback, polish, responsive, tactile, satisfying, screen shake, controls, sensation, snappy, feels good, juicy, mushy
**felt as:** it just doesn't feel good to play; the controls feel mushy; it needs more juice; everything's functional but unsatisfying; how do i make it feel responsive; the feedback is flat; it plays fine but there's no punch; should i polish the feel or add depth.
**the_tension:** Steve Swink (*Game Feel*, 2009) names the real-time, tactile sensation of control — the moment-to-moment responsiveness, the "juice" of feedback (sound, shake, particles, easing) that makes an action feel satisfying in the hand — and argues it is a primary, often-decisive layer of digital play that has little to do with rules or story. Get it right and a mechanically thin game can be a joy; get it wrong and a deep one can feel dead. The counter is that juice can become a substitute for substance: lavish feedback can make a shallow game feel momentarily satisfying — papering over how little there is to do, and flattering thin mechanics. The student's move is to ask whether the problem in front of them is one of *feel* — which polish and responsiveness would fix — or of *depth*, which more juice would only disguise.
**failure_modes:** shipping deep mechanics with feel so flat the game seems broken or lifeless; pouring juice over hollow mechanics so it feels great for ten minutes and empty after; treating feel as mere "polish" to add at the end rather than a core design layer.
**questions_it_invites:** Is what's missing here a matter of how it *feels* to act — responsiveness, feedback, weight — or of what there is to do? · If you added all the juice you could, would the game be better, or just better at hiding what's thin?
**sources:** Swink, *Game Feel: A Game Designer's Guide to Virtual Sensation* (Morgan Kaufmann/Elsevier, 2009).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — game feel is Swink's correctly-attributed subject (citation-verified; keep **2009**, a 2008 catalogue variant exists). Position-in-named-work; counter-pole (juice as substitute for substance) argued. Digital-specific (real-time virtual sensation).

## entry: fun-as-learning
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** fun, learning, boring, mastery, pattern, repetitive, grind, gets old, teaching, skill, novelty, stale, hook, why is it fun
**felt as:** it gets boring once you figure it out; players master it and stop; why is this fun at all; it feels grindy and repetitive; the novelty wears off; how do i keep it interesting; it's fun for an hour then stale; what makes a game keep its hook.
**the_tension:** Raph Koster (*A Theory of Fun*, 2004) argues that fun *is* the brain enjoying itself while learning — a game is fun as long as it is still teaching the player a pattern to master, and it goes boring the moment the pattern is solved and nothing new is being learned. The design consequence is sharp: a game must keep offering fresh, learnable structure or it dies, and "grind" is what is left when the learning has stopped. The counter is that this elegant theory may not be the whole of fun: social pleasure (playing *with* people), narrative, beauty, relaxation, the comfort of a familiar ritual and sheer competition can keep players engaged long after the learning curve flattens — and treating "learnable pattern" as the only source of fun can lead a designer to over-value novelty and under-value the pleasures of mastery, company and return. The student's move is to ask whether the engagement here comes from *still learning something*, or from some other pleasure entirely — and which one this game actually runs on.
**failure_modes:** assuming a solved game is necessarily a dead one, and chasing novelty over the pleasures of mastery, ritual and company; ignoring that some games do go genuinely stale once their pattern is learned; manufacturing endless "content" to learn while neglecting the social or aesthetic reasons people stay.
**questions_it_invites:** When this stops being fun, is it because the player has finished learning it — or for some other reason? · What keeps people here after the learning curve flattens — mastery, each other, the story, the ritual — and is the design feeding that?
**sources:** Koster, *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* (Paraglyph Press, 2004; 2nd ed. O'Reilly Media, 2013).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high (as a position) — fun-as-learning is the correctly-attributed central argument of Koster (2004), citation-verified. Framed explicitly as "Koster argues", **not** as established cognitive science (the verifier flagged it as a popular-press thesis, not an empirical finding). Counter-pole (social/aesthetic/competitive pleasures) argued.

## entry: extrinsic-rewards-and-gamification
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** gamification, points, badges, leaderboard, reward, motivation, intrinsic, extrinsic, streak, engagement, incentive, overjustification, hooked
**felt as:** should i add points and badges; will rewards make it more engaging; gamify it to keep people coming back; add a leaderboard; the streak keeps them hooked; does the reward cheapen it; people only do it for the points now; how do i motivate them.
**the_tension:** Adding game elements — points, badges, levels, leaderboards — to a non-game activity ("gamification", defined by Deterding et al., 2011) is a reliable way to drive behaviour in the short run, and for some contexts that is genuinely useful. But a long line of motivation research warns of the **overjustification effect**: introducing an extrinsic reward for something a person already found intrinsically interesting can *crowd out* that intrinsic motivation, so they do it for the points and stop when the points stop (Deci, 1971; Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, 1973). The evidence is real but conditional — the crowding-out is strongest for tangible, expected rewards and weakest, even reversed, for informational feedback and unexpected praise — so it is a caution, not a law. The student's move is to ask what motivation already exists in the activity, and whether the reward they want to add would *amplify* it or quietly *replace* it — and what happens to the behaviour the day the reward is taken away.
**failure_modes:** bolting points and badges onto an activity people already valued, and watching the intrinsic interest drain out; assuming rewards always backfire and refusing structure that would genuinely help; designing for the engagement metric while the underlying activity becomes something people only do for the prize.
**questions_it_invites:** What already motivates someone to do this — and would the reward you're adding strengthen that motive, or substitute for it? · If you removed the points tomorrow, what would people still do, and what would stop?
**sources:** Deterding, Dixon, Khaled & Nacke, "From Game Design Elements to Gamefulness: Defining 'Gamification'", MindTrek '11 (ACM, 2011); Deci, "Effects of Externally Mediated Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 18(1) (1971); Lepper, Greene & Nisbett, "Undermining Children's Intrinsic Interest with Extrinsic Reward", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 28(1) (1973).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — gamification (Deterding et al.) and the overjustification studies (Deci; Lepper, Greene & Nisbett) are correctly attributed and citation-verified (DOIs confirmed). Consensus-checked: overjustification is empirically supported but *conditional* (near-zero mean in one quantitative review; reward-type dependent), so the entry frames it as a caution, not a law. Both poles weighted — the design value of rewards is granted (NB: zetizeti's own anti-gamification stance, invariant #6, is about the *product*, not a verdict the corpus carries).

## entry: player-types
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** player types, achievers, explorers, socialisers, killers, audience, who is it for, segments, motivation, taxonomy, casual, hardcore, Bartle
**felt as:** who is this game for; different players want different things; some want to win, some to explore, some to chat; should i target one type; how do i please everyone; what kind of player is this; people don't fit neatly into one box.
**the_tension:** Richard Bartle's taxonomy ("Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades", 1996) sorted players into **achievers, explorers, socialisers and killers**, and frameworks like it give designers a useful tool: a way to name who a game is for, to notice whose pleasures the design serves and whose it ignores, and to balance features across different motivations. The counter is that such taxonomies can *flatten* the people they describe — a single player moves between types within one session (exploring, then competing, then chatting); the categories were drawn from one genre (MUDs) and may not transfer; and "designing for a type" can harden into a stereotype that designs the actual, mixed, changeable player out. The student's move is to ask whether naming a player type is helping them see whose experience they are shaping — or substituting a tidy label for the messier person who will actually play.
**failure_modes:** designing for a stereotyped "type" and missing that real players move between motivations; using a taxonomy from one genre as if it were a law of all players; refusing any model of the audience and so designing only for oneself.
**questions_it_invites:** When you picture "the player" of this, whose pleasures are you serving — and whose are you leaving out? · Does naming a player type help you see them more clearly, or let you stop seeing the messier person who'll actually play?
**sources:** Bartle, "Hearts, Clubs, Diamonds, Spades: Players Who Suit MUDs", *Journal of MUD Research* 1(1) (1996).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Bartle's taxonomy is correctly attributed and citation-verified (1996, *Journal of MUD Research*; born-digital, pre-DOI — cite URL). The critique (taxonomies flatten; players move between types; MUD-specific origin) is well established. Two-sided.

## entry: paidia-and-ludus
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** free play, structured, rules, open, sandbox, improvise, toy, goal, win condition, unstructured, make-believe, constraints, aimless, mess around
**felt as:** should there be a goal or just let them play; it's a toy not a game; players just want to mess around; do i need win conditions; it's too open and aimless; it's too rigid and rule-bound; should it be a sandbox or a challenge; how much structure does it need.
**the_tension:** Roger Caillois (*Man, Play and Games*, 1958/1961) placed all play on a spectrum between **paidia** — free, improvised, tumultuous play (a child with blocks, messing about, make-believe) — and **ludus** — play bound by arbitrary, effortful rules and explicit goals (chess, sport, the puzzle). A design can sit anywhere along it, and the choice shapes everything: lean toward paidia and you get a sandbox or toy that invites open, self-directed play but may leave players aimless and unsure what to do; lean toward ludus and you get clear goals and challenge that focus the player but can feel rigid and close down improvisation. The student's move is to ask where on the paidia–ludus spectrum this design wants to sit — whether it is offering a *toy* to play with or a *contest* to win — and whether the structure it has matches the kind of play it is actually inviting.
**failure_modes:** an open sandbox so unstructured that players feel aimless and drift off; rules so rigid they crush the free, improvisational play the design seemed to promise; being unclear whether it is a toy or a game, so players don't know how to engage.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this offering a toy to play *with* or a contest to *win* — and does its structure match that? · Where players feel aimless or boxed in, is it because there is too little structure for the play you want, or too much?
**sources:** Caillois, *Man, Play and Games*, trans. Barash (Free Press, 1961; orig. *Les jeux et les hommes*, Gallimard, 1958) — the paidia/ludus spectrum.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — paidia/ludus is Caillois's correctly-attributed distinction (citation-verified: Barash trans., Free Press 1961; Gallimard 1958). Two-sided (each pole's cost named). Distinct from emergence/progression (that axis = where content comes from; this = the player's mode of play).

## entry: uncertainty-and-chance
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** luck, chance, randomness, dice, uncertainty, fair, skill, random, swingy, outcome, unpredictable, balance, deterministic, RNG
**felt as:** the dice ruin it; too much luck, not enough skill; it feels random; the better player should win; randomness keeps it exciting; it's too predictable; the randomness feels unfair; how much chance should there be; newcomers never stand a chance.
**the_tension:** Greg Costikyan (*Uncertainty in Games*, 2013) argues that uncertainty is what keeps a game alive — if the outcome is known, there is no reason to play — and that it comes from many sources (randomness, hidden information, the opponent's choices, the player's own execution), not chance alone. Randomness specifically does real work: it keeps outcomes open, gives weaker players a fighting chance, and creates dramatic swings and stories. But it also cuts the other way: too much chance severs the link between skill and outcome, so the better player no longer reliably wins and decisions stop feeling consequential — the reason chess and Go use none, while poker and backgammon build much of their depth on it. The student's move is to ask where the uncertainty in this game *comes from*, and whether chance is doing the work they think — opening the game up and welcoming newcomers — or loosening the link between the skill they want to reward and how the game turns out.
**failure_modes:** so much randomness that skill barely affects the outcome and choices feel pointless; so little uncertainty that the outcome is decided early and there is no reason to keep playing; assuming "uncertainty" must mean dice, when hidden information or opponents' choices could supply it instead.
**questions_it_invites:** Where does the uncertainty in this game actually come from — chance, hidden information, the opponent, or the player's own skill? · Is the randomness here opening the game up and keeping it alive, or loosening the link between what a player does and how well they do?
**sources:** Costikyan, *Uncertainty in Games* (MIT Press, 2013).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Costikyan's "uncertainty sustains games" plus the taxonomy of uncertainty sources is the book's correctly-attributed thesis (citation-verified, MIT Press 2013, Playful Thinking series). Two-sided (chance welcomes newcomers vs dilutes skill). Strong board/tabletop relevance.

## entry: balance-and-asymmetry
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** balance, asymmetry, fair, overpowered, dominant strategy, symmetric, factions, broken, viable, options, nerf, matchup, unbalanced
**felt as:** one strategy is clearly the best; it's unbalanced; everyone picks the same thing; the factions feel unfair; how do i make options equally viable; should both sides be the same; one side always wins; there's a dominant strategy.
**the_tension:** A competitive game wants **balance** — no single choice, faction or strategy so dominant that the others become pointless and the real game collapses into one optimal line (the "dominant strategy" problem; Schreiber & Romero, *Game Balance*, 2021). The cleanest route is symmetry: give everyone the same resources, as chess does. But **asymmetry** — different factions, powers, starting positions — is often where a game's richness, replayability and character live, and asymmetry is precisely what is hardest to balance, because "fair" no longer means "identical" but "different yet equally viable". The student's move is to ask whether the variety they want is worth the balancing burden it creates — and whether an imbalance they have spotted is a flaw to fix, or asymmetry that is fair-but-different and part of the game's depth.
**failure_modes:** asymmetry so unbalanced that one option dominates and the rest are dead on arrival; balancing toward such sameness that the factions lose all character and the game goes flat; chasing perfect balance forever when "varied and roughly fair" is what the game actually needs.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the variety you want here worth the balancing it will cost — and can you keep the differences while keeping them viable? · When one option looks "best", is that a flaw to fix, or difference that is fair in a way "identical" wouldn't be?
**sources:** Schreiber & Romero, *Game Balance* (A K Peters/CRC Press, 2021).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium — balance/asymmetry and the dominant-strategy problem are standard game-design concerns; Schreiber & Romero (2021) is a correctly-attributed textbook treatment (citation-verified) rather than the origin of the idea, so cited as the field's reference, not its coinage. Two-sided. Strong board + competitive-digital relevance.

## entry: the-compulsion-loop
**discipline:** interaction-design
**vocabulary:** compulsion loop, engagement, retention, variable reward, addictive, hooked, loot box, daily, grind, exploitative, monetisation, keep playing
**felt as:** how do i keep players coming back; the daily reward keeps them hooked; it's designed to be addictive; loot boxes feel exploitative; engagement is up but is this ok; players can't stop even though they're not enjoying it; the loop is compulsive; is this immersion or manipulation.
**the_tension:** Many games run on a **reward loop** — act, get a variable reward, want to act again — and at its best this is the engine of immersion and flow: the act–reward–act rhythm of a well-tuned roguelike, a sport or a satisfying combat system, where the pull is the play itself and it is the reason a well-paced game is hard to put down. But the same mechanism is, structurally, the one Natasha Dow Schüll (*Addiction by Design*, 2012) traced in machine gambling: variable-ratio reinforcement engineered to maximise *time on device* rather than the player's satisfaction, pulling people into compulsive use they do not endorse — the lineage behind loot boxes, gacha pulls, daily-login streaks and endless ladders. The hard part is that the line between *engaging* and *exploitative* is not in the mechanic itself but in whose interest it serves: a loop that deepens a player's own enjoyment versus one tuned to retention and spend at the player's expense. The student's move is to ask what their reward loop is optimised for — the player's experience, or the player's continued presence and payment — and how they would tell those two apart in their own game.
**failure_modes:** tuning a loop for retention or spend until players are compelled rather than delighted (and resent it on reflection); importing gambling-style variable rewards without owning what they do to people; flinching from all reward loops so the game loses the rhythm and momentum that make play absorbing.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this loop optimised for the player's enjoyment, or for their continued presence and spend — and are those the same here? · Someone deep in this loop, stepping back from it later — what would they say the time gave them?
**sources:** Schüll, *Addiction by Design: Machine Gambling in Las Vegas* (Princeton University Press, 2012); cf. Deterding et al., MindTrek '11 (2011), on game elements outside games.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium — Schüll's machine-gambling analysis is correctly attributed and citation-verified (Princeton 2012); applying it to game compulsion loops / loot boxes is a well-established but interpretive move (framed as "the lineage behind", not a claimed equivalence). Two-sided (immersion vs exploitation; the line is whose-interest, not the mechanic).
