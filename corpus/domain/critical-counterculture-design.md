# Domain corpus: Critical, Counterculture & Attention-Economy Lens

> **Part B — cross-cutting lens.** Synthetic, retrieved per turn via FTS5. Where slow design challenges *speed*, this lens challenges the deeper conventional assumptions: that design should *solve, please, and smooth*; that interfaces are *neutral*; that *engagement* is the goal. Vocabulary is keyed to those conventional words, so the lens activates when a student reaches for "useful / intuitive / seamless / engaging / neutral / scalable."
> **Held two-sided.** Critical design *challenges* conventional design; it does not win by default — user-friendly, problem-solving design genuinely serves people, and critical/contestational design can be self-indulgent or exclusionary. Each entry gives the conventional pole real weight.
> **Intent register (`felt as:`).** Each entry carries a `felt as:` line — the plain, colloquial way a student voices the *conventional* move this lens questions — weighted in retrieval, so the lens activates on intent, not just the precise word (architecture.md §5.1). Exact-word FTS5; no embeddings.

## entry: affirmative-vs-critical-design
**discipline:** critical-design
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** solve, problem, solution, useful, user-friendly, intuitive, please, easy, fix, product, optimise, helpful
**felt as:** i just want to solve the problem and make it useful; make it intuitive and easy; the user should find it effortless; let's fix it and ship a helpful product; user-friendly is the whole goal.
**the_tension:** Most design is *affirmative* — it solves a problem and reinforces how things already are. *Critical* design instead uses the artefact to ask a question, provoke, or critique. Is your design here to solve a problem and please the user, or to make them think — and is "user-friendly" always the right aim?
Dunne (in *Hertzian Tales*) argues design is too preoccupied with making technology "easy, sexy and consumable," and proposes a "post-optimal object" and even an "aesthetics of user-unfriendliness" — design that resists smooth consumption to open reflection. But the counter is real: most people, most of the time, want the thing to work, and provocation-for-its-own-sake can be a designer's self-indulgence dressed as critique. The student's question is which register this project is in — and whether it knows.
**failure_modes:** dressing up an unusable artefact as "critical"; reflexively optimising for ease when the brief actually called for a question.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this design solving a problem, or asking one? · When you make this effortless, what stops getting thought about?
**sources:** Dunne, *Hertzian Tales: Electronic Products, Aesthetic Experience, and Critical Design* (1999); Dunne & Raby, *Design Noir* (2001); Pierce, "Working by Not Quite Working: Resistance as a Technique for Alternative and Oppositional Designs" (PhD dissertation, Carnegie Mellon University, 2015).

## entry: speculative-design-for-debate
**discipline:** critical-design
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** future, predict, trend, roadmap, forecast, what users want, innovation, next, vision, scenario
**felt as:** what's the future of this; let's predict the trend and build the roadmap; design for what users will want next; chase the next big thing; map out where it's heading; forecast the vision.
**the_tension:** Conventional future-facing design *predicts and optimises* — trends, roadmaps, what users will want. Speculative design instead poses "what if" provocations to open debate about the futures people do and don't want. Are you forecasting the likely future, or interrogating which future is desirable — and have you confused the two?
Dunne & Raby's speculative design uses design fictions to make "reality more malleable," widening the space of imaginable futures rather than betting on the probable one. The counter: speculation untethered from delivery can become gallery-bound and evade the question of what to actually build now. The student should know whether the work is meant to *ship* or to *provoke* — both are legitimate, conflating them is not.
**failure_modes:** a "speculative" project that quietly smuggles in a single inevitable future; a roadmap that never asks whether the future it optimises for is one anyone wants.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this the future you predict, or the future you're arguing for? · What future does this design quietly treat as inevitable?
**sources:** Dunne & Raby, *Speculative Everything: Design, Fiction, and Social Dreaming* (2013); Dunne, "Design for Debate" (2008).

## entry: adversarial-design-and-agonism
**discipline:** critical-design
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** consensus, seamless, smooth, agree, frictionless, harmony, resolve, neutral, agreement, friction-free, conflict
**felt as:** make it seamless and smooth; get everyone to agree; resolve the friction; keep it harmonious and frictionless; smooth over the disagreement; make it all just flow.
**the_tension:** Conventional design smooths toward consensus and seamlessness. Adversarial design instead *surfaces* conflict — it treats contestation (agonism) as foundational to democratic life rather than something to design away. Is smoothing the disagreement here a service, or is it erasing a conflict that deserves to be seen?
DiSalvo argues design can "provoke and engage the political," making power and dissent visible rather than resolving them; recent work designs *for* agonism — friction, refusal, disagreement — as a legitimate alternative to consensus-based methods. The counter: not every interaction is a political arena, and manufacturing conflict where people just want to get something done is its own failure. The student should ask whether the smoothness is serving the user or hiding a stake.
**failure_modes:** designing away a real conflict so the interface feels harmonious; staging conflict in a context where the user simply needs the task done.
**questions_it_invites:** What conflict does this smoothness hide? · Whose disagreement disappears when this becomes seamless?
**sources:** DiSalvo, *Adversarial Design* (MIT Press, 2012); Jing et al., "Designing for Agonism: 12 Workers' Perspectives on Contesting Technology Futures" (2024); Koskinen, "Agonistic, Convivial, and Conceptual Aesthetics in New Social Design" (2016).

## entry: defamiliarisation-and-ambiguity
**discipline:** critical-design
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** clear, obvious, intuitive, transparent, unambiguous, simple, easy to understand, legible, self-explanatory, clean
**felt as:** make it crystal clear and obvious; it should be intuitive and self-explanatory; remove any ambiguity; keep it simple and transparent; people shouldn't have to think about it; legible at a glance.
**the_tension:** Usability prizes clarity and transparency — the interface should be obvious. But ambiguity and *defamiliarisation* can be resources: making the familiar strange invites the user to interpret, engage, and reflect rather than consume on autopilot. When is clarity a gift, and when does it foreclose the thinking that a little strangeness would have opened?
Gaver argues ambiguity "encourages close personal engagement," and reflective-design traditions deliberately estrange to prompt reflection. The counter: in most consequential, time-pressured, or accessibility-critical contexts, ambiguity is cruelty — clarity *is* the ethics. The student's discrimination is which this is: a moment for interpretation, or a moment where the user must simply understand.
**failure_modes:** ambiguity deployed where a user urgently needs to understand (errors, consent, safety); flattening everything to "obvious" where interpretation was the point.
**questions_it_invites:** What does the user stop interpreting when this becomes perfectly clear? · Is this a moment for reflection, or for being understood instantly?
**sources:** Gaver, Beaver & Benford, "Ambiguity as a Resource for Design" (*CHI* 2003); Sengers et al., "Reflective Design" (*Critical Computing / Aarhus*, 2005); Bentvelzen et al., "Revisiting Reflection in HCI" (2022).

## entry: whose-values-feminist-hci
**discipline:** critical-design
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** neutral, universal, objective, everyone, the user, best practice, standard, default, unbiased, general
**felt as:** just design it for the user; keep it neutral and objective; follow best practice; make it work for everyone; use the standard default; an unbiased, general solution.
**the_tension:** "Good design" is often presented as neutral, universal, serving "the user." Feminist HCI argues the opposite — that every design embeds values and a model of who the user is, so "neutral" can quietly carry the values of whoever did the designing. Does "the user" here name a real, specific person — and is the convention you're following a fair default, or one that fits some bodies and lives better than others?
Feminist HCI (Bardzell) foregrounds agency, identity, equity and marginalised experience, arguing design is never value-free; care- and justice-oriented approaches extend this. The counter: shared standards and conventions genuinely aid usability and interoperability, and treating every default as oppression can paralyse practice. The student's move is to make the embedded values *visible* and chosen, rather than left implicit and called "neutral" — while granting that a shared default is sometimes exactly right (interoperable, learnable, accessible), and inventing a bespoke alternative would serve no one.
**failure_modes:** "designing for everyone" by designing for a default that looks like the designer; treating universal conventions as inherently illegitimate.
**questions_it_invites:** When you say "the user," who specifically are you picturing — and does anyone you're designing for fall outside that picture? · Is this default neutral here, or does it carry a value worth naming and choosing on purpose?
**sources:** Bardzell, "Feminist HCI: Taking Stock and Outlining an Agenda for Design" (*CHI* 2010); Croon, "Thinking with care in human–computer interaction" (*Feminist Theory* 23(2), 2022).

## entry: pluriversal-and-decolonial-design
**discipline:** critical-design
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** universal, global, scalable, best practice, standard, one design, works everywhere, localise, roll out, mainstream
**felt as:** build one design that works everywhere; make it universal and scalable; roll it out globally; follow the global standard; one solution we can localise later; make it work for the mainstream.
**the_tension:** Conventional design seeks the universal, scalable solution — one design that works everywhere. Decolonial and pluriversal design argue that "universal" design often exports one (frequently Western) world and crowds out others. Is the universality you're reaching for a genuine strength here — shared, accessible, interoperable — or is it carrying one world's assumptions as if they were everyone's?
Escobar argues for a "pluriverse" — *a world where many worlds fit*, in the Zapatista phrase he draws on — against the one-world ontology baked into mainstream design; decolonial HCI critiques the Eurocentric "human" at the centre of human-centred design. The counter: shared standards genuinely enable access, interoperability and scale, and not every local difference must be preserved. The student should ask what a "universal" design assumes and what it overwrites.
**failure_modes:** scaling one cultural model as if it were neutral; fetishising the local to the point of denying people access to shared infrastructure.
**questions_it_invites:** What does this "universal" design assume that isn't actually universal? · Where did this standard come from — and is it serving the people it's applied to, or just familiar to you?
**sources:** Escobar, *Designs for the Pluriverse: Radical Interdependence, Autonomy, and the Making of Worlds* (2018); Raihan, "The Colonial Legacy of the Human in Human-Computer Interaction" (*Aarhus*, 2025).

## entry: convivial-tools-vs-dependence
**discipline:** counterculture
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** automatic, convenient, do it for you, seamless, frictionless, no setup, just works, easy, effortless, managed, hands-off
**felt as:** make it automatic so it just works; do it for the user, no setup; keep it effortless and hands-off; convenient and managed; they shouldn't have to think about it; seamless and frictionless.
**the_tension:** Convenience-driven design makes tools that act *for* the user — automatic, managed, hands-off. The convivial-tools tradition (Illich) prizes the opposite: tools people can understand, shape, and repair, that enlarge rather than replace their capacity. Does this convenience free the user's attention for what matters, or quietly remove a capacity worth keeping — and who can fix the tool when it breaks?
Illich argued for tools that foster autonomy and conviviality over institutions that create dependence; the counterculture-to-cyberculture lineage (Turner) traces how personal computing carried an emancipatory promise that, on this reading, commercial platforms later narrowed. The counter holds, and genuinely: convenience is not mere dependence — managed, hands-off tools widen who can use a thing at all (people without the time, expertise, or wish to maintain it), and the demand that everyone become a tinkerer is itself a kind of exclusion. The student should ask what the convenience frees the user to do — and what capacity, if any, it removes in exchange.
**failure_modes:** "just works" until it doesn't, leaving the user unable to understand or fix it; demanding everyone become a tinkerer for basic use.
**questions_it_invites:** What does this convenience free the user to attend to — and what, if anything, does it stop them being able to do? · When it breaks, can the user understand and adapt it, or only wait for whoever made it?
**sources:** Illich, *Tools for Conviviality* (1973); Turner, *From Counterculture to Cyberculture: Stewart Brand, the Whole Earth Network, and the Rise of Digital Utopianism* (2006).

## entry: attention-as-extracted-resource
**discipline:** attention-economy
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** engagement, attention, retention, focus, interest, hold attention, time on app, capture, hook, eyeballs, stickiness, dwell time, screen time
**felt as:** how do we hold their attention; boost engagement and time on app; make it sticky, hook them; capture eyeballs and increase dwell time; keep them coming back; get more screen time.
**the_tension:** The dominant platform business model treats attention as a resource to capture and sell. The attention-economy critique (Wu; Williams) counters that the attention is the user's own — finite, and not the platform's to enclose. When a design holds attention, is it *earning* it (offering something the person values) or *extracting* it (against the person's own ends) — and how would you tell the difference here?
The attention-economy critique (Wu; Williams) frames attention as a scarce common good being enclosed and over-exploited by platforms; "attention as practice" reframes it as ethically and ecologically embedded, not merely an individual resource to defend. The counter: capturing attention is not intrinsically wrong — a teacher, a novel, a good interface all "hold attention," and the question is to what end and with whose consent. The student should ask whether the attention is being *earned* or *extracted*.
**failure_modes:** optimising a metric ("time on app") that is the user's life being spent; treating any attention-holding as automatically virtuous.
**questions_it_invites:** When this holds someone's attention, whose ends does it serve — theirs, or the platform's? · Could the same design be earning attention or extracting it depending on intent — and which is it here?
**sources:** Wu, *The Attention Merchants* (2016); Williams, *Stand Out of Our Light: Freedom and Resistance in the Attention Economy* (2018); Bombaerts et al., "Attention as Practice" (2023).

## entry: engagement-vs-agency
**discipline:** attention-economy
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** engagement, retention, daily active users, time on app, addictive, hooked, autoplay, infinite scroll, notifications, growth, metrics, conversion
**felt as:** maximise engagement and daily active users; add autoplay and infinite scroll; send notifications to pull them back; make it addictive, get them hooked; grow the metrics, push retention and conversion.
**the_tension:** Platforms are incentivised to maximise engagement — autoplay, infinite scroll, notifications, variable rewards — even when it conflicts with users' own interests. The counter-value is the user's *sense of agency*: their felt control over their own use. Does this design extend the user's agency, or quietly override it to extend their session?
Empirical work finds autoplay and recommendations primarily *undermine* sense of agency while search and playlists *support* it [logged]; structured analysis of major platforms catalogues dozens of engagement-prolonging design patterns its authors group as pressuring, enticing, trapping and lulling users. The counter: engagement is not inherently exploitative — people *want* to be absorbed in things they value, and "agency" can be a cover for friction that helps no one. The discrimination is whether the design serves the user's intention or the platform's metric.
**failure_modes:** measuring success by time-on-app while users report feeling out of control; adding friction labelled "agency" that just obstructs.
**questions_it_invites:** Does this design serve the user's intention, or the platform's metric? · After using this, does the user feel they chose how their time went?
**sources:** Lukoff et al., "How the Design of YouTube Influences User Sense of Agency" (*CHI* 2021); Bhargava & Velasquez, "Ethics of the Attention Economy: The Problem of Social Media Addiction" (*Business Ethics Quarterly*, 2020); Schüll, *Addiction by Design* (2012).

## entry: wellbeing-design-or-willpower
**discipline:** attention-economy
**provenance:** verified
**vocabulary:** self-control, willpower, screen time, digital wellbeing, user responsibility, discipline, distraction, focus, habits, moderation
**felt as:** users just need more self-control; add a screen-time limit and a wellbeing dashboard; it's about their willpower and discipline; help them resist distraction; teach better habits and moderation; it's on the user to manage it.
**the_tension:** "Digital wellbeing" is usually framed as the user's job — exercise willpower, set screen-time limits, resist distraction. But if the environment is engineered to hold attention, critics argue that locating the fix entirely in the user's self-control misreads where the problem lives. Is this mostly a matter of the user's habits and discipline, mostly of how the thing is built, or genuinely shared — and how would you decide?
Critical work argues digital wellbeing is treated as personal responsibility when it is structurally produced, and that "fixing the distractible user" obscures the designer's power and the political conditions involved. The counter: agency and habit are real, individual practices (and traditions of attention as discipline) genuinely matter — locating *everything* in the system denies the user any role. The student should ask where responsibility actually sits, and resist the easy answer.
**failure_modes:** shipping an attention-capturing design plus a "wellbeing dashboard" that blames the user; pretending users have no agency at all.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this a failure of the user's willpower, of how the thing is built, or of both at once? · Where does responsibility actually sit here — user, design, or shared — and what changes if you place it differently?
**sources:** Docherty & Biega, "(Re)Politicizing Digital Well-Being: Beyond User Engagements" (*CHI* 2022); Valasek, "Disciplining the Akratic user: Constructing digital (un)wellness" (*Mobile Media & Communication* 10(2), 2022; online-first 2021); Monge Roffarello et al., "The Digital Attention Heuristics" (*ACM TOCHI*, 2025).
