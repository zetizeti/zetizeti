## entry: real-need-vs-manufactured-desire
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** need, want, desire, useful, market, sell, feature, new, novelty, problem, real, useless, consumer, waste, responsibility, disposable
**felt as:** people will want this; it'll sell; let's add a feature; it's new and exciting; does anyone actually need it; we're solving a real problem; or are we inventing one to sell something.
**the_tension:** Victor Papanek (*Design for the Real World*, 1971) opens with the charge that much of industrial design serves *manufactured desire* rather than real human need — designing novelty, obsolescence and want for the already-comfortable while ignoring the genuine needs of most of the world — and argues the designer carries social and ecological responsibility for what they help bring into existence. The counter is that desire is not a lie: delight, status, pleasure and identity are real human goods, and a purely needs-based asceticism can be joyless and presumptuous about what others "should" want. The student's move is to ask, of the thing they are about to make, whether it answers a need someone actually has or a want the design itself is manufacturing — and whether that distinction changes their responsibility for it.
**failure_modes:** inventing a problem so the product has a reason to exist; designing novelty and disposability and calling it innovation; dismissing all desire as frivolous and designing joyless things nobody wants.
**questions_it_invites:** Does this answer a need someone already has, or a want this design is shaping — and is that want a real human good, or one invented to justify the product? · Who is this for — and is the desire it speaks to (delight, status, identity) worth serving in its own right, or a distraction from a need it leaves unmet?
**sources:** Papanek, *Design for the Real World: Human Ecology and Social Change* (1971).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — real-need-vs-manufactured-want and designer responsibility is Papanek's central, well-known thesis. Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers; the economics of manufactured demand and planned obsolescence is well-established — Bulow's foundational theory shows firms rationally prefer short product lives — yet the counter is also empirical: obsolescence is argued to be a necessary condition for technological progress, and experts genuinely split on trap-versus-development-tool — see verification-consensus-backing.md).

## entry: good-design-or-styling
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** good design, styling, less but better, minimal, restraint, honest, principles, decoration, market, sell, taste, timeless, trend
**felt as:** keep it clean, good design is less but better; strip it to essentials; make it timeless and honest; or — it needs to stand out and sell; style it for the market; people want something exciting to look at; restraint is boring; what makes this good design.
**the_tension:** Dieter Rams's "Ten Principles of Good Design" hold that good design is unobtrusive, honest, long-lasting and as *little* design as possible — "less, but better" — a discipline of restraint in which the object serves quietly and does not shout for attention. Against the idea that good design is a settled set of principles stands the logic of the market, articulated by Raymond Loewy's *MAYA* — "Most Advanced Yet Acceptable" — that products must be styled to be desirable and sellable, advanced enough to excite but familiar enough to be accepted, and that styling is not a betrayal of function but how a thing reaches people at all. The tension is real: Rams's restraint can read as a refusal of the delight, expression and desirability that styling provides, while Loewy's market logic can slide into superficial novelty and planned dissatisfaction. The student's move is to ask whether the form they are giving this product serves the thing and its user honestly, or is styling aimed at the sale — and whether those are actually opposed here, or can be the same move.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the form you're giving this serving the object and its user honestly, or styling it to sell — and are those actually opposed here? · If you stripped it to "as little design as possible", what would be lost — desirability, delight, legibility — and does that loss matter for this product?
**failure_modes:** styling for novelty and the sale until the form lies about the product or dates it deliberately; pursuing restraint until the thing is austere and joyless where delight was part of its job; treating "good design" as a fixed creed rather than a question about this object and its user.
**sources:** Rams, "Ten Principles of Good Design" (Braun, 1970s–80s; "as little design as possible" / "less, but better"); Loewy, *Never Leave Well Enough Alone* (1951), articulating the MAYA — "Most Advanced Yet Acceptable" — principle.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Rams's Ten Principles and Loewy's MAYA are well-known, correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified; MAYA was popularised in Loewy's book rather than a literal title slogan). Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off. (Overlaps slow-design's restraint-is-not-neutral on minimalism, but here the axis is product good-design-vs-market-styling, not aesthetic neutrality.)

## entry: the-average-and-the-edge
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** average, typical, standard, ergonomic, anthropometric, fit, sizing, universal, inclusive, accessible, adjustable, one size, edge case, user
**felt as:** design it for the average user; the typical person; one standard size; we can't design for everyone; the edge cases are rare; just fit the majority; who's the average user anyway; do we need it adjustable.
**the_tension:** Designing for "the average user" feels like common sense — fit the typical person and you serve the most people. But the evidence undercuts the premise: when the US Air Force defined the average pilot across ten dimensions, almost no actual pilot fell within the average on all of them (Daniels, 1952), and Todd Rose's *The End of Average* (2016) generalises the point — the "average human" is a statistical fiction that fits almost no one, which is why adjustable seats and a range of sizes outperform a single mean. Henry Dreyfuss's anthropometric tradition (*Designing for People*, 1955; "Joe and Josephine") already pointed designers toward the spread of real bodies, not a phantom average. The counter is that designers cannot make infinite variants: some standardisation is unavoidable and even good — a sensible set of sizes, a well-chosen adjustable range, shared conventions — and full bespoke personalisation carries real cost, complexity and waste. The student's move is to ask who the "average user" in their head actually excludes, and whether a defined range or adjustability would serve real bodies better — without pretending every product can fit everyone perfectly.
**questions_it_invites:** Who does the "average user" in your head actually leave out — and would a range or an adjustable design fit real people better here? · Where is standardising a sensible necessity, and where has it quietly become a one-size that misfits most?
**failure_modes:** designing to a statistical average that fits almost no real body, then treating the misfit as the user's problem; chasing infinite personalisation at a cost and complexity the product can't bear; mistaking "we picked a standard size" for "we considered the range of users".
**sources:** Daniels, "The 'Average Man'?" (USAF, 1952); Rose, *The End of Average* (2016); Dreyfuss, *Designing for People* (1955), and the "Joe and Josephine" anthropometric figures.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the no-average-fits-anyone finding (Daniels 1952; Rose 2016) and Dreyfuss's anthropometrics are correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified); Consensus-checked on human variability (variation grows from the mean; sizing-into-groups; the male-default critique — see verification-consensus-backing.md). The counter (standardisation's real necessity/cost) is a design-economics argument held in-entry. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-object-and-its-meaning
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** meaning, semantics, symbol, communicate, expression, function, form, language, signal, association, read, identity, signify
**felt as:** just make it functional; it only needs to work; why does it matter what it looks like it does; the form should communicate; it signals quality; what does this shape say; people read meaning into it; function is all that counts.
**the_tension:** A strict functionalism says a product's form should follow its function and nothing more — the rest is decoration. Klaus Krippendorff and Reinhart Butter's *product semantics* (1984; Krippendorff, *The Semantic Turn*, 2006) argues this misses how products actually work for people: form *communicates* — it tells users what a thing is, how to use it, what it means and whom it is for — and people respond to that meaning as much as to mechanical function. On this view, a designer is always shaping meaning whether they attend to it or not, so they had better do it deliberately. The counter is that meaning can be over-read and over-designed: loading an object with semantic intention can produce gimmickry, cultural misreadings, or forms that signal more than they deliver, and sometimes a plain, functional object that makes no statement is exactly right. The student's move is to ask what this object is communicating beyond its function — and whether that meaning is one they have chosen and can stand behind, or noise they have not noticed.
**questions_it_invites:** Beyond what it does, what is this object communicating — and is that meaning one you chose, or one you haven't noticed? · Where would attending to meaning make this clearer or more usable, and where would it tip into a form that says more than the thing delivers?
**failure_modes:** designing for function alone while the form sends signals the designer never examined; over-loading an object with meaning until it becomes gimmick or misreads across cultures; assuming a product is "neutral" when its form is already speaking.
**sources:** Krippendorff & Butter, "Product Semantics: Exploring the Symbolic Qualities of Form," *Innovation* 3, no. 2 (1984); Krippendorff, *The Semantic Turn: A New Foundation for Design* (2006).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — product semantics (Krippendorff & Butter; *The Semantic Turn*) is a well-known, correctly-attributed position (Perplexity-verified, exact title/venue confirmed). Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: emotion-or-the-functional
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** emotion, beautiful, attractive, delight, pleasure, usable, function, work, efficient, ugly, love, attachment, aesthetic, feeling
**felt as:** it just needs to work well; usability is what matters; pretty is secondary; or — make it beautiful, people love beautiful things; attractive things work better; it should delight; does beauty actually matter if it functions; function first, looks later.
**the_tension:** A usability-first view treats beauty as a luxury layered on top of a product that works. Donald Norman's *Emotional Design* (2004) argues the relationship runs deeper: attractive things "really do work better", because positive affect changes how people perceive, tolerate and engage with a product — the aesthetic-usability effect, in which perceived beauty raises perceived usability, has real experimental support (Tractinsky; Sonderegger; Moshagen). On this view, emotion is not decoration but part of how a thing functions for a human being. The counter, equally evidenced, is that the effect is smaller and shakier than the slogan suggests: meta-analysis finds only a small, heterogeneous effect on actual performance, several studies find aesthetics does *not* improve perceived usability (and that usability instead drives perceived beauty — "what is usable is beautiful"), the halo *wanes* with prolonged use, and an attractive interface can even mask or excuse poor usability. The student's move is to ask what beauty is actually doing for this product — genuinely improving the experience and the use, or buying goodwill that covers for a thing that doesn't work.
**questions_it_invites:** What is beauty actually doing for this product — improving the experience and the using of it, or buying goodwill that covers for poor function? · If the appeal wore off after a week of real use, what would be left — and is that what you're designing for?
**failure_modes:** treating beauty as mere decoration and missing how affect shapes whether a thing is usable at all; leaning on attractiveness to mask poor usability the user will eventually hit; assuming the aesthetic-usability halo is large and durable when the evidence says it is small and fades.
**sources:** Norman, *Emotional Design: Why We Love (or Hate) Everyday Things* (Basic Books, 2004); the aesthetic-usability effect — Kurosu & Kashimura (1995); Tractinsky, "What is beautiful is usable" (2000).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (aesthetic-usability effect real — Tractinsky, Sonderegger, Moshagen — but small/heterogeneous, sometimes reversed "usable is beautiful", and waning — Thielsch meta-analysis, Tuch, Hamborg, Sonderegger longitudinal). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Distinct from the IxD experience-beyond-usability entry (this is the object's beauty and the aesthetic-usability halo specifically). Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: modularity-or-integration
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** modular, module, interface, integrated, integration, mix and match, components, standardised, swappable, upgrade, system, architecture, performance, optimise, coupling, interchangeable
**felt as:** make it modular so parts swap out; design clean interfaces between the pieces; let people upgrade and repair the bits; or — integrate it tightly so the whole thing performs; the parts are tuned to each other; a modular version would be slower and bulkier; should this be a system of parts or one optimised whole.
**the_tension:** Carliss Baldwin and Kim Clark's *Design Rules, Volume 1: The Power of Modularity* (MIT Press, 2000) shows the power of splitting a product into modules joined by stable interfaces: modules can be designed, improved, repaired and swapped independently, the system tolerates change, and innovation can happen part-by-part without redesigning the whole. The counter is that integration buys performance modularity cannot: Clayton Christensen's *The Innovator's Dilemma* (Harvard Business School Press, 1997) argues that when a product is not yet good enough on the dimensions customers care about, a tightly *integrated* architecture — parts co-designed and optimised to each other rather than to a clean interface — wins on speed, size, reliability and cost, and modularity becomes the better bet only once the product overshoots what users need. Both are real: modularity gives flexibility, repair and independent innovation but spends performance on the interfaces; integration gives performance but locks the parts together so none can change or be replaced alone. The student's move is to ask whether this product is being asked for flexibility and longevity or for raw performance, and which architecture that actually calls for here.
**questions_it_invites:** What is this product being asked to be best at — flexible and upgradable, or as small/fast/cheap as it can be — and which way of joining the parts serves that? · Where would a clean interface between parts let something improve or be repaired on its own, and where would it cost the performance the thing is for?
**failure_modes:** modularising for its own sake until the interfaces eat the performance the product needed; integrating everything so tightly that nothing can be repaired, upgraded or replaced; treating "modular" or "integrated" as a virtue in the abstract rather than a choice about what this product must do well.
**sources:** Baldwin & Clark, *Design Rules, Volume 1: The Power of Modularity* (MIT Press, 2000); Christensen, *The Innovator's Dilemma: When New Technologies Cause Great Firms to Fail* (Harvard Business School Press, 1997).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Baldwin & Clark on modularity and Christensen's integration-when-not-good-enough argument are well-known, correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified: exact titles, years, publishers confirmed). Theory-position entry; framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-machine-or-the-hand
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** machine, handmade, craft, mass production, factory, industrial, workmanship, mechanised, made by hand, standardised, jig, tool, risk, certainty, fabrication, automation
**felt as:** make it by hand, the hand-made one has soul; the machine flattens everything; people can feel the care; or — the machine makes it good, cheap and the same every time; hand-work is slow and uneven; why romanticise the hand; should this be made by a machine or by a person.
**the_tension:** William Morris's "The Lesser Arts" (1877) and the Arts and Crafts argument hold that mass mechanisation degrades both the object and the maker — that machine production strips work of joy and the object of the human care that gives it worth, and that the handmade keeps a connection between maker, material and thing that the factory severs. Against this, Herbert Read's *Art and Industry: The Principles of Industrial Design* (Faber & Faber, 1934), with the Bauhaus/Gropius machine-aesthetic, argues that the machine is not the enemy of good design but its modern medium — that beauty can be designed *into* a mass-produced, standardised object, and refusing the machine condemns good design to the few who can afford the handmade. David Pye's *The Nature and Art of Workmanship* (1968) reframes the whole quarrel away from a simple hand-vs-machine line: the real distinction is the *workmanship of risk* (where the result is continuously at risk from the maker's judgement and skill) versus the *workmanship of certainty* (where the result is predetermined by the tool or jig before work begins) — and a hand can work with certainty, a machine-tended process can carry risk, so the question is which kind of making this object needs. The student's move is to ask what the hand or the machine actually contributes *here* — care, variation and judgement, or consistency, reach and economy — rather than defaulting to either as virtue.
**questions_it_invites:** What does making this by hand actually give it that a machine wouldn't — and is that the variation and judgement the thing needs, or a feeling about authenticity? · Where does this object need the workmanship of risk (the result alive to the maker's judgement) and where the workmanship of certainty (the result fixed before work begins)?
**failure_modes:** romanticising the hand-made until the object is precious, scarce and worse at its job than a well-designed machine-made one; surrendering to the machine until every object is the same, judgement-free and joyless; arguing hand-versus-machine as a moral position without asking which kind of workmanship this thing actually calls for.
**sources:** Morris, "The Lesser Arts" (lecture, 1877); Read, *Art and Industry: The Principles of Industrial Design* (Faber & Faber, 1934); Pye, *The Nature and Art of Workmanship* (Cambridge University Press, 1968) — the workmanship of risk vs certainty.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Morris's Arts-and-Crafts critique, Read's machine-aesthetic defence of industrial design, and Pye's risk/certainty distinction are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified; Morris 1877 lecture is a lecture not a stand-alone 1877 volume — cited as such). Pye is folded in here (not a separate entry) as the contemporary epistemology-of-making axis that reframes the historical Morris/Read quarrel. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: design-for-everyone-or-for-someone
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** universal, inclusive, accessible, everyone, disability, assistive, mainstream, stigma, one design, special, adaptable, accommodate, segregate, integrate, ability, identity
**felt as:** design it for everyone, one product that works for all abilities; don't single anyone out; make it inclusive; or — this is a thing for disabled people, design it well *for them*; "for everyone" makes it bland and hides who it's really for; should this be one universal design or something made unashamedly for some.
**the_tension:** The Center for Universal Design's *Principles of Universal Design, Version 2.0* (North Carolina State University, 1997), led by Ronald Mace, holds that products should be usable by all people to the greatest extent possible without adaptation or special design — one mainstream object that does not segregate or stigmatise the people who most need it to work. Graham Pullin's *Design Meets Disability* (MIT Press, 2009) presses a real counter: the drive to make one thing for everyone can produce timid, lowest-common-denominator design and can erase the specific person it serves — Pullin shows how eyewear became a fashion object precisely by being designed *as* eyewear, unashamedly and well, and argues that designing boldly *for* disability (as fashion, as identity, as expression) can serve people better than folding them invisibly into a universal mean. Both hold: universalism resists stigma and the segregated "special" product, but can flatten; designing-for-someone can be richer, more expressive and more dignifying, but can re-segregate or stigmatise if done carelessly. The student's move is to ask whether "for everyone" here genuinely serves the person at the edge or quietly designs them out — and whether a thing made boldly *for* them would dignify or isolate.
**questions_it_invites:** Does "designing for everyone" here actually serve the person who most needs it to work, or smooth them into an average that leaves them out? · If you designed this unashamedly *for* the people at the edge — as something to be proud of, not to hide — would that dignify them or set them apart?
**failure_modes:** chasing one-design-for-all until the result is bland and serves the person at the edge worst of all; designing a "special" product that works but stigmatises or segregates its user; treating either universalism or designing-for-someone as automatically the inclusive choice without asking who it actually serves here.
**sources:** Center for Universal Design (Mace et al.), *The Principles of Universal Design, Version 2.0* (North Carolina State University, 1997); Pullin, *Design Meets Disability* (MIT Press, 2009).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the Universal Design principles (Mace / NCSU, 1997) and Pullin's *Design Meets Disability* (MIT, 2009) are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified). Distinct from `the-average-and-the-edge` (that is anthropometric fit / the statistical average; this is the universalism-vs-design-for-disability and stigma axis). Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: whose-design-is-it
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** participation, co-design, codesign, users, stakeholders, involve, democratic, expertise, designer, decide, voice, collaborate, facilitator, judgement, authorship, workshop
**felt as:** bring the users in, co-design it with them; they know their own lives; don't design *at* people; let them decide; or — I'm the designer, that's my judgement to make; users can't always say what they need; too many voices and nothing gets designed; whose call is this in the end.
**the_tension:** The participatory-design tradition — Pelle Ehn's *Work-Oriented Design of Computer Artifacts* (Arbetslivscentrum, Stockholm, 1988) and Elizabeth Sanders and Pieter Jan Stappers' "Co-creation and the new landscapes of design" (*CoDesign* 4, no. 1, 2008) — holds that the people who will live with a design have expertise the designer lacks, that involving them is both more effective and more democratic, and that the designer's job shifts toward facilitating others' creativity rather than authoring alone. The counter, in Erik Stolterman's "The Nature of Design Practice and Implications for Interaction Design Research" (*International Journal of Design* 2, no. 1, 2008), is that design is a distinct form of judgement — the designer brings a trained, holistic, situation-reading expertise that cannot be replaced by aggregating user voices, and that someone must finally make the integrative call. Both are real: participation surfaces knowledge and legitimacy the designer cannot manufacture, but can also diffuse responsibility, stall on consensus, or ask users to do design work they are not equipped for; designerly judgement integrates and decides, but can slide into designing *at* people and mistaking the designer's taste for their need. The student's move is to ask, of each decision, whose knowledge it actually needs and whose call it finally is — without pretending participation removes the need for judgement, or that judgement licenses ignoring the people who'll live with it.
**questions_it_invites:** On this decision, whose knowledge does it actually need — the people who'll live with it, or your trained judgement — and whose call is it in the end? · Where would bringing people in surface something you can't know alone, and where would it ask them to do design work that's really yours to do?
**failure_modes:** involving everyone in everything until responsibility diffuses, consensus stalls, and no one is accountable for the integrative call; treating designerly judgement as licence to design *at* people and call your taste their need; staging "participation" that gathers voices but changes nothing about who decides.
**sources:** Ehn, *Work-Oriented Design of Computer Artifacts* (Arbetslivscentrum, Stockholm, 1988); Sanders & Stappers, "Co-creation and the new landscapes of design," *CoDesign* 4, no. 1 (2008): 5–18; Stolterman, "The Nature of Design Practice and Implications for Interaction Design Research," *International Journal of Design* 2, no. 1 (2008): 55–65.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — participatory/co-design (Ehn 1988; Sanders & Stappers 2008) and Stolterman's design-as-judgement (2008) are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified; Ehn cited 1988/Arbetslivscentrum, the 1989 date is a later-bibliography artefact). Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-problem-or-the-brief
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** problem, brief, define, reframe, requirements, given, accept, question the problem, wicked, well-defined, scope, solve, problem-setting, problem-solving, root, symptom
**felt as:** just solve the brief, the problem's been defined; my job is the solution not the question; the requirements are set; or — the brief is wrong, the real problem is somewhere else; we're solving a symptom; should I take the problem as given or reframe it.
**the_tension:** One view treats design as problem-*solving*: the problem is given (in the brief, the requirements, the spec) and the designer's craft is finding a good solution — Herbert Simon's *The Sciences of the Artificial* (MIT Press, 1969) frames design as devising courses of action to change existing situations into preferred ones, a search toward a goal that can be stated and worked against. Horst Rittel and Melvin Webber's "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning" (*Policy Sciences* 4, no. 2, 1973) argues the opposite for the problems design actually faces: they are *wicked* — there is no definitive formulation, no stopping rule, and how you frame the problem already determines the solution, so problem-*setting* is the design act and accepting the brief as given is itself a (often hidden) decision. Both hold: taking the problem as defined lets work proceed, respects the brief and avoids the designer endlessly relitigating the question; but treating a wicked problem as a tame one means solving the wrong thing well, and the framing the designer accepts may smuggle in someone else's assumptions. The student's move is to ask whether the problem in front of them is genuinely given or whether the framing is itself the thing to design — and what accepting this brief as stated quietly decides.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the problem here actually given, or is the way it's framed already deciding the answer — and is that framing yours to question? · What does accepting this brief as stated quietly decide for you — and would a different framing of the problem change what a good solution even is?
**failure_modes:** taking a wicked, ill-defined problem as if it were tame and solving the wrong thing beautifully; endlessly reframing and re-questioning the brief until nothing is ever designed; not noticing that the given framing has already smuggled in assumptions and constraints the designer never chose.
**sources:** Simon, *The Sciences of the Artificial* (MIT Press, 1969); Rittel & Webber, "Dilemmas in a General Theory of Planning," *Policy Sciences* 4, no. 2 (1973): 155–169.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Simon's design-as-problem-solving and Rittel & Webber's wicked-problems are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified). Distinct from the transdisciplinary `the-wicked-problem-or-a-tame-one-in-disguise` (that turns on the *validity* of the wicked-problem concept itself; this is the product-designer's practical brief-reframing axis — accept the given problem vs reframe it). Both invoke Rittel; the line is held. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: repairable-or-sealed
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** repair, repairable, fixable, serviceable, sealed, glued, screws, parts, spares, open up, take apart, right to repair, disposable, replace, integrated, slim
**felt as:** make it repairable, let people open it and fix it; replaceable parts, standard screws; don't glue it shut; or — sealing it makes it thinner, stronger, water-tight, cheaper to make; nobody repairs things anymore; repairability bloats the design; should this open up or be sealed.
**the_tension:** Vance Packard's *The Waste Makers* (1960) named the throwaway logic of products built to be discarded rather than mended, and the right-to-repair movement (iFixit and allied advocacy) argues that products should be openable, documented and built from replaceable parts — that repairability extends life, cuts waste, and keeps power with the owner rather than the manufacturer. The counter is not mere greed: sealing and tight integration buy real things, and the design-for-manufacture-and-assembly tradition — Geoffrey Boothroyd, Peter Dewhurst and Winston Knight's *Product Design for Manufacture and Assembly* (Marcel Dekker, 1994) — shows that reducing part count, bonding rather than fastening, and integrating components makes products cheaper, lighter, more reliable and easier to assemble, while sealing can deliver water-resistance, slimness and structural strength a serviceable design cannot match. Both are real: repairability serves longevity, autonomy and waste-reduction but spends size, cost, sealing and part-count; sealed integration serves performance, manufacturability and cost but forecloses repair and tends toward disposability. The student's move is to ask what sealing this product actually buys, and at what cost in life and ownership — rather than treating either repairability or integration as the obviously responsible choice.
**questions_it_invites:** What does sealing this actually buy — slimness, strength, water-resistance, cost — and is that worth the repair and the longer life it forecloses? · If the owner couldn't open this when one part failed, what happens to the whole object — and is that the life you're designing for it?
**failure_modes:** sealing and integrating for manufacturability and slimness until a single failed part dooms the whole object to the bin; bloating a product with serviceability it will never use, at a cost in size, sealing and price the user didn't want; treating "repairable" or "sealed" as a virtue in itself rather than weighing what each buys for this product.
**sources:** Packard, *The Waste Makers* (David McKay, 1960); Boothroyd, Dewhurst & Knight, *Product Design for Manufacture and Assembly* (Marcel Dekker, 1994) — DFMA part-count reduction; iFixit / right-to-repair advocacy (repair manuals and policy testimony, 2003–).

## entry: lifetime-as-a-design-decision
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** lifespan, durability, obsolescence, planned obsolescence, last, throwaway, replace, lifetime, longevity, wear out, upgrade, disposable, long-lasting, design life, dated
**felt as:** how long should this last; build it to last forever; or — it only needs to last a few years, things move on; people want the new model; making it too durable is wasteful if it dates; building in a replacement cycle keeps the business alive; what's the right lifespan to design for.
**the_tension:** Every product is designed to last *some* length of time, and that lifespan is a decision, not a given. The planned-obsolescence position — captured in Brooks Stevens's own definition of "instilling in the buyer the desire to own something a little newer, a little better, a little sooner than is necessary" (documented in Glenn Adamson's *Industrial Strength Design: How Brooks Stevens Shaped Your World*, MIT Press, 2003) — treats a managed replacement cycle as legitimate and even necessary: it funds development, keeps products current, and a thing built to outlast its own relevance can be its own kind of waste. The counter, in Tim Cooper's edited *Longer Lasting Products: Alternatives to the Throwaway Society* (Gower, 2010), argues that designing for longevity — durability, repair, upgrade, timeless rather than fashionable form — cuts waste and resource use and is the responsible default, and that obsolescence is too often a designed-in death date serving the maker, not the user. Both have force: a long design life saves resources and serves the owner, but can lock in a product past the point it should be superseded and ignore that some things are rightly short-lived; a managed lifespan funds renewal and keeps products current, but slides easily into manufactured dissatisfaction and waste. The student's move is to ask what lifespan this thing should honestly have — set by its use, its materials and what supersedes it — rather than letting either "build it to last" or "they'll want the new one" decide unexamined.
**questions_it_invites:** How long should this honestly last — set by how it's used and what will supersede it — and who does the lifespan you're designing in actually serve? · If this outlived its own relevance, or died the year the warranty ended, which of those is the waste you'd be designing — and is either the life this thing should have?
**failure_modes:** designing in a death date that serves the maker's replacement cycle and calls it progress; building for a permanence the product's own relevance won't match, so it outlives its usefulness as landfill-in-waiting; treating lifespan as a given rather than a deliberate decision about use, materials and what comes next.
**sources:** Stevens (planned obsolescence), as documented in Adamson, *Industrial Strength Design: How Brooks Stevens Shaped Your World* (MIT Press, 2003); Cooper (ed.), *Longer Lasting Products: Alternatives to the Throwaway Society* (Gower, 2010).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Stevens's obsolescence definition (via Adamson 2003) and Cooper's *Longer Lasting Products* (2010) are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified: titles, years, publishers confirmed). Distinct from `real-need-vs-manufactured-desire` (that is Papanek on need vs manufactured *want* and designer responsibility; this is specifically the *lifespan/durability* decision — how long to design a thing to last). ## entry: waste-as-design-or-doing-less
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** waste, cradle to cradle, circular, recycle, upcycle, nutrient, sufficiency, enough, eco-efficiency, materials, reuse, regenerative, less, restraint, consumption, footprint
**felt as:** design out the waste, make it endlessly recyclable, close the loop; waste is just a design flaw; or — the greenest product is the one not made; recycling won't save us, we need to make and buy less; should I design a better, circular thing, or design *less* thing.
**the_tension:** William McDonough and Michael Braungart's *Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things* (North Point Press, 2002) reframes waste as a design failure rather than an inevitability: design every material as a biological or technical "nutrient" that feeds back into a cycle, and a product can be abundant, even generous, without harm — "waste equals food." The counter, in Thomas Princen's *The Logic of Sufficiency* (MIT Press, 2005), is that no amount of design cleverness escapes the deeper problem of *throughput*: efficiency and circularity can license more consumption (the rebound effect), recycling in practice downcycles and never fully closes, and the real move is *sufficiency* — restraint, "enough", designing and making *less* rather than designing better things to make more of. Both are real: cradle-to-cradle dignifies the material and refuses fatalism about waste, but can become an alibi for business-as-usual abundance; sufficiency confronts the throughput that circular design evades, but a doctrine of "less" can be ascetic, presumptuous about others' needs, and offer designers little to actually make. The student's move is to ask whether the honest response to this product's footprint is to design its waste away or to question whether it should be made at scale at all.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the honest move here to design this thing's waste away, or to ask whether it should be made — and at this scale — at all? · If you closed the loop perfectly on this product, would that reduce what's consumed, or quietly license more of it?
**failure_modes:** treating "circular" or "recyclable" as a clean conscience while the product drives more consumption (the rebound); preaching sufficiency and "less" until the position is ascetic, presumptuous about others' needs, and gives the designer nothing to make; assuming recycling closes the loop when in practice it downcycles and leaks.
**sources:** McDonough & Braungart, *Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things* (North Point Press, 2002); Princen, *The Logic of Sufficiency* (MIT Press, 2005).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Cradle to Cradle (McDonough & Braungart, 2002) and Princen's *The Logic of Sufficiency* (2005) are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified: titles, years, publishers confirmed). The rebound-effect and downcycling counters are well-established critiques held in-entry. Distinct from `real-need-vs-manufactured-desire` (need vs want) and `lifetime-as-a-design-decision` (lifespan) — this is the design-out-waste vs reduce-throughput/sufficiency axis. ## entry: nudging-or-leaving-the-choice
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** nudge, persuasion, behaviour change, default, choice architecture, influence, autonomy, dark pattern, manipulate, steer, friction, opt-out, consent, design ethics, intent, persuasive
**felt as:** design it to change their behaviour, set good defaults, make the better choice the easy one; a little nudge helps people; or — that's manipulating users, who am I to steer them; where's the line between a helpful default and a dark pattern; should this design shape what people do, or leave the choice clean.
**the_tension:** Persuasive and behaviour-change design treats steering the user as a legitimate, even kind, design goal: B.J. Fogg's *Persuasive Technology: Using Computers to Change What We Think and Do* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2003) and Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness* (Yale University Press, 2008) argue that products inevitably shape behaviour through defaults and "choice architecture", so the designer should shape it *toward the user's own good* — the better choice made the easier one. The counter comes from the same mechanism turned against the user: Harry Brignull coined "dark patterns" in 2010 (cataloguing deceptive design at darkpatterns.org, now deceptive.design) to name how the identical tools — defaults, friction, framing — steer people *against* their interest, and the deeper objection is that engineering behaviour, even benevolently, can erode the autonomy and consent of the person being steered. Both are real: because a design always shapes behaviour somehow, "leaving the choice neutral" is often an illusion, and a good nudge can genuinely help; but the line between a helpful default and manipulation is thin, the designer's idea of the user's "own good" may not be the user's, and persuasive power deployed for the maker's ends is exactly the dark pattern. The student's move is to ask what this design is steering the user toward, whose interest that serves, and whether the person would consent to being steered if they could see it.
**questions_it_invites:** What is this design steering the user toward — and whose interest does that serve, theirs or the maker's? · If the person could see the nudge plainly — the default, the friction, the framing — would they consent to being steered this way, or feel handled?
**failure_modes:** steering users "for their own good" by a standard that's really the designer's or the maker's, not theirs; deploying defaults, friction and framing for the maker's ends — the dark pattern — while calling it helpful; pretending a design can be behaviourally "neutral" when defaults and framing always shape what people do.
**sources:** Fogg, *Persuasive Technology: Using Computers to Change What We Think and Do* (Morgan Kaufmann, 2003); Thaler & Sunstein, *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness* (Yale University Press, 2008); Brignull, "dark patterns" (coined 2010; darkpatterns.org / deceptive.design — typology of deceptive design).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Fogg's persuasive technology, Thaler & Sunstein's *Nudge*, and Brignull's "dark patterns" (coined 2010) are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified; Brignull coinage year corrected to 2010 against an initial "mid-2010s" error). ## entry: the-method-or-the-judgement
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** design thinking, method, process, empathise, ideate, prototype, framework, recipe, judgement, expertise, repeatable, workshop, post-its, innovation, conservative, status quo
**felt as:** follow the design-thinking process, empathise-define-ideate-prototype-test; the method gets you there; anyone can run it; or — it's become post-it theatre, a recipe that produces safe nothing; real design takes judgement you can't proceduralise; should I trust the method or my designerly judgement.
**the_tension:** The design-thinking movement — popularised in Tim Brown's "Design Thinking" (*Harvard Business Review*, June 2008) and *Change by Design* (HarperCollins, 2009) — holds that good design can be made a teachable, repeatable *process* (empathise, define, ideate, prototype, test) that lets organisations and non-designers innovate reliably, democratising a once-mysterious craft. The counter, from within design scholarship, is that this proceduralisation hollows the thing out: Lucy Kimbell's "Rethinking Design Thinking: Part I" (*Design and Culture*, 2011) questions its universal, managerial framing and its inattention to the situated reality of design practice, and Natasha Iskander's "Design Thinking Is Fundamentally Conservative and Preserves the Status Quo" (*Harvard Business Review*, 2018) argues the method's empathy-then-converge recipe tends to reproduce existing power and produce safe, incremental results rather than genuine change. Both hold: a shared process makes design legible, collaborative and teachable, and rescues it from being an inscrutable gift of the few; but a method run as a recipe can substitute ritual (the workshop, the post-its) for the trained, integrative *judgement* that design actually requires, and can launder the designer's or client's assumptions as "what users want." The student's move is to ask what the process is actually doing here — structuring real judgement, or standing in for it — and where this work needs a method and where it needs a decision no method can make.
**questions_it_invites:** What is the process doing for this work — giving structure to your judgement, or standing in for a judgement you'd rather not have to make? · Where does this design genuinely need a shared, repeatable method, and where does it need a call that no recipe can make for you?
**failure_modes:** running design thinking as a recipe — the workshop, the post-its, the stages — until ritual replaces the judgement design actually needs; rejecting all method as beneath "real" design and leaving collaborators no shared way in; letting the empathise-converge process launder the designer's or client's assumptions as the users' own needs.
**sources:** Brown, "Design Thinking," *Harvard Business Review* (June 2008), and *Change by Design* (HarperCollins, 2009); Kimbell, "Rethinking Design Thinking: Part I," *Design and Culture* 3, no. 3 (2011); Iskander, "Design Thinking Is Fundamentally Conservative and Preserves the Status Quo," *Harvard Business Review* (2018).

## entry: form-follows-function-or-the-expressive
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** form follows function, functionalism, ornament, decoration, expression, modernist, minimal, postmodern, Memphis, playful, justify, superfluous, meaning, less is more, less is a bore, exuberant
**felt as:** form follows function, strip anything that doesn't earn its place; ornament is dishonest; keep it pure; or — let it be playful, expressive, exuberant even where that serves no function; "less is a bore"; does every part have to justify itself by use; should the form be ruled by function or free to express.
**the_tension:** The modernist creed, crystallised in Louis Sullivan's "form ever follows function" ("The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered," *Lippincott's Magazine* #57, March 1896) and carried by the Bauhaus and Mies van der Rohe's "less is more", holds that a product's form should be determined and justified by its function — ornament and expression that serve no use are superfluous, even dishonest. The postmodern and Radical/Anti-Design reply — Robert Venturi's "less is a bore" (*Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture*, Museum of Modern Art, 1966) and Ettore Sottsass's Memphis Group (Milan, 1981), whose deliberately exuberant, ornamental, often *non*-functional objects rejected the doctrine outright — holds that expression, play, colour and ornament are legitimate ends in their own right, that the demand for every form to earn itself through function is impoverishing, and that delight and cultural meaning need no functional alibi. Both hold: functionalism gives clarity, discipline and honesty and resists arbitrary decoration; but it can become a joyless dogma that treats all expression as waste, while expressive freedom enriches and delights but can tip into arbitrary styling, gimmick, or form that fights the thing's use. The student's move is to ask whether the form they're giving this is being ruled by function, freed for expression, or both — and whether a part that serves no function is superfluous here or doing real work that isn't functional.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the form here being ruled by what the thing does, or freed to express something — and does a part that serves no function feel superfluous, or like it's doing real work that isn't functional? · If you let this be playful or ornamental where it serves no use, what would that add, and where would it start to fight what the thing is for?
**failure_modes:** holding "form follows function" as dogma until expression, delight and cultural meaning are all treated as superfluous waste; indulging expression and ornament until the form is arbitrary, gimmicky, or fighting the thing's use; assuming a part must "justify itself by function" without asking whether non-functional work (delight, meaning, play) is part of this object's job.
**sources:** Sullivan, "The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered," *Lippincott's Magazine* #57 (March 1896): 403–409 ("form ever follows function"); Venturi, *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* (Museum of Modern Art, 1966) ("less is a bore"); Sottsass / the Memphis Group (Milan, founded 1981) — anti-functionalist, expressive design.

**framing_confidence:** high — Sullivan's "form ever follows function" (1896 essay container Exa-verified: Lippincott's #57, March 1896, pp. 403–09; the phrase is canonical), Venturi's "less is a bore" (1966), and the Memphis Group / Sottsass anti-functionalism are correctly-attributed positions (Perplexity-verified). ## entry: truth-to-materials-or-the-surface
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** truth to materials, honest, material, surface, finish, imitation, veneer, fake, simulate, wood grain, plastic, authentic, reveal, disguise, texture, artifice
**felt as:** be honest with the material, let the wood be wood and the metal be metal; don't fake a finish; reveal what it's made of; or — the surface can be whatever it needs to be; plastic moulded to look like wood is fine if it works; why should the material show through; should this be true to what it's made of or free to look like something else.
**the_tension:** The "truth to materials" principle, descending from John Ruskin and the Arts and Crafts movement, holds that honest design reveals rather than disguises a material's real nature — wood should read as wood, a join should be expressed not hidden, and a surface that imitates another material (plastic grained to look like timber, veneer faking solid wood) is a kind of dishonesty that cheapens both object and maker. The counter treats surface and artifice as legitimate, even meaningful, design territory: Roland Barthes' "Plastic" (in *Mythologies*, 1957) reads the very imitability of plastic — its capacity to become any surface — as a genuine modern condition rather than a lie, and design-history work like Penny Sparke's *As Long As It's Pink: The Sexual Politics of Taste* (Thames & Hudson, 1995) and Adrian Forty's *Objects of Desire: Design and Society Since 1750* (Thames & Hudson, 1986) shows that "honesty of materials" is itself a culturally and class-loaded taste rather than a neutral truth, and that surface, imitation and decoration carry real social meaning. Both hold: truth-to-materials gives integrity, lets a material be understood and worked with its grain, and resists the cheap fake; but it can harden into a moralised aesthetic that forbids legitimate surface, simulation and play, and pretends one taste is simply "honest". The student's move is to ask what this surface is doing — revealing the material, or standing in for another — and whether that's a lie, a legitimate choice, or just a taste being dressed up as a truth.
**questions_it_invites:** What is this surface doing — revealing what the thing's made of, or standing in for another material — and does that read as honest, as fake, or as a choice that's neither? · Is "truth to materials" here an actual integrity the object needs, or a taste being treated as a moral truth?
**failure_modes:** moralising "honesty of materials" into a rule that forbids legitimate surface, simulation and decoration, and dresses one taste up as truth; faking surfaces so freely (imitation grain, false joins) that the object misleads about what it is and how it'll behave; not noticing that the surface is making a claim about the material at all.
**sources:** Ruskin / Arts and Crafts "truth to materials" (Ruskin's writings, mid-19th c.); Barthes, "Plastic," in *Mythologies* (1957); Sparke, *As Long As It's Pink: The Sexual Politics of Taste* (Thames & Hudson, 1995); Forty, *Objects of Desire: Design and Society Since 1750* (Thames & Hudson, 1986).

## entry: the-articulated-need-or-the-new-meaning
**discipline:** product-design
**vocabulary:** user needs, user-centred, listen, research, ask users, articulate, latent, incremental, radical, meaning, vision, technology, drive, propose, market research, innovation
**felt as:** ask the users what they need, design from their stated needs; listen, don't impose; or — users can't tell you what they don't yet know they want; following stated needs only gets you small improvements; sometimes you propose a new meaning, not answer a request; should I follow the user's articulated need or lead with a new vision.
**the_tension:** User-centred design treats the user's needs as the proper origin of design: Donald Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things* (originally *The Psychology of Everyday Things*, 1988; revised, Basic Books, 2013) argues that products should fit the user's mental model, that misfit is the designer's fault not the user's, and that careful attention to real needs and use is the discipline's foundation. The counter — argued by Norman himself in "Technology First, Needs Last" (*interactions* 17, no. 2, 2010) and by Roberto Verganti's *Design-Driven Innovation: Changing the Rules of Competition by Radically Innovating What Things Mean* (Harvard Business Press, 2009) — is that following stated user needs yields mainly *incremental* improvement, because users can articulate refinements to what they know but rarely the thing they don't yet know they want; radical innovation more often comes from technology opening a possibility or from the designer *proposing a new meaning* for what a product is, which the market then comes to want. Both hold: starting from articulated need keeps design grounded, humble and usable, and resists the designer imposing a vision nobody asked for; but it tends to optimise the present and miss the larger reframing, while leading with a new meaning or technology can open genuinely new ground — or can be hubris, a solution nobody wanted dressed as vision. The student's move is to ask whether the opening here is an articulated need to serve well or a new meaning to propose — and to be honest about which they're actually doing.
**questions_it_invites:** Is the opening here a need the user can already articulate, or a new meaning you'd be proposing for what this thing is — and which are you actually doing? · If you only served the needs people can state, what would you miss — and if you led with your own vision instead, how would you know it's not just what nobody asked for?
**failure_modes:** following only articulated needs until the work is all incremental and the larger reframing is never seen; leading with a "new meaning" or a technology nobody wanted and calling imposition vision; confusing the two — dressing the designer's untested idea as a discovered user need, or dismissing a real need as too small to bother with.
**sources:** Norman, *The Design of Everyday Things* (Basic Books, rev. ed. 2013; orig. *The Psychology of Everyday Things*, 1988), and "Technology First, Needs Last," *interactions* 17, no. 2 (2010): 38–42; Verganti, *Design-Driven Innovation: Changing the Rules of Competition by Radically Innovating What Things Mean* (Harvard Business Press, 2009).
