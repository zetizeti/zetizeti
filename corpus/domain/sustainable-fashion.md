## entry: the-garment-after-the-shelf
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** sustainable, material, eco, organic, recycled, fast, fashion, season, trend, lifecycle, use, repair, longevity, dispose, waste, ethical
**felt as:** i'll use sustainable materials; it's organic cotton so it's fine; how do i make it eco; just swap the fabric; what happens after they buy it; people wear it twice and bin it; how long will it last.
**the_tension:** Kate Fletcher (*Sustainable Fashion and Textiles: Design Journeys*, 2008) argues that sustainability in fashion is not settled at the point of material choice — a greener fabric inside an unchanged system of speed, volume and disposability changes little. She reframes it as a set of *design journeys* spanning the whole life of a garment: how it is used, kept, repaired, loved and let go, and how the rhythm of the system itself might slow. The counter is that material and supply-chain improvements are real and immediately actionable, and that asking a designer to redesign consumption and the whole system can grow so large it paralyses, excusing inaction. The student's move is to ask whether their "sustainable" choice touches the material only or the whole life the garment will live — and where, across that life, the real harm and the real leverage actually sit.
**failure_modes:** swapping to a "green" material while the speed-and-disposal system stays untouched; letting the sustainability claim stop at the label, never tested against the life the garment actually lives; scoping the problem so wide (the whole system) that nothing gets made.
**questions_it_invites:** Does your sustainable choice sit at the material, or across the whole life the garment will live? · Is the highest-leverage move here a concrete material change you can make now, or a whole-life redesign you might never finish?
**sources:** Fletcher, *Sustainable Fashion and Textiles: Design Journeys* (Earthscan, 2008).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium — Fletcher's "beyond materials, across the garment's whole life / design journeys" framing is accurate but synthesises across the book; confirm against the cited chapters in the verification pass. The core empirical claim (extending use-life reduces impact more reliably than fibre substitution) is now strongly Consensus-backed (20 LCA papers; counter-evidence on rebound effects and ineffective longevity strategies also present — see verification-consensus-backing.md).

## entry: repair-and-the-rafugar
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** repair, mend, darn, rafu, rafugari, rafoogari, patch, fix, broken, torn, hole, worn, restore, replace, discard, invisible, visible, sashiko, boro, longevity, care, craft
**felt as:** it's torn, just bin it; can this even be fixed; should the repair show or stay hidden; mend it so you can't tell; i'll just buy a new one; make the patch a feature; nobody repairs clothes anymore; who even knows how to darn; it's not worth fixing.
**the_tension:** In the South Asian craft of *rafugari* — invisible darning, documented by Priya Ravish Mehra among the hereditary rafugars of Najibabad and Kashmir — the mender's art is to rebuild the weave of a damaged pashmina or kani shawl thread by thread until the wound disappears and the garment is whole again, sometimes keeping an heirloom in use for generations. The repair's dignity lies in its *invisibility*: the object is honoured by being made un-broken. Against this sits a second ethic — the contemporary visible-mending movement and traditions like Japanese sashiko and boro — which holds that the mend should *show*, that the patch and the darn are marks of care and history, and that hiding repair colludes with the fantasy that things never wear. And beneath both sits the harder counter: repair is rare and difficult. Lifecycle research repeatedly finds that extending a garment's active life is consistently among the highest-leverage environmental interventions, yet in practice only a small fraction of garments are ever mended before disposal, blocked by lost skills, cost, and the sense that a visibly worn thing is no longer "mine to wear" — and much of the living repair-craft knowledge that could change this sits in the Global South, largely absent from the circular-economy conversations of the North. But repairability is not always the right value: some things are honestly better made cheap and replaceable, some are hygienic or fast-changing goods where mending makes little sense, and designing every object to be lovingly repaired can be its own imposition — or a way of pushing onto the owner a fix that belonged upstream. The student's move is to ask whether repair is even the right ambition for this thing — and if it is, whether the design imagines that repair and *whose* hands would do it, and whether the mend should vanish or be seen.
**failure_modes:** designing a thing as though it will never tear, leaving no thought to how — or whether — it could be mended; treating repairability as a virtue in itself, even where a cheap, hygienic or genuinely short-lived object is the honest, lower-impact choice; romanticising hand-repair while ignoring that most people lack the skill, time or access and will simply discard; treating the visible-versus-invisible choice as merely a look, when each carries an ethic about how a worn thing deserves to be honoured; reaching for "traditional craft" as ornament while ignoring the people who hold that knowledge.
**questions_it_invites:** When this wears or tears, is repair even the right answer for it — and if it is, does your design imagine that repair, and whose hands would do the mending? · Should a mend disappear (the rafugar's ethic — dignity in wholeness) or be seen (the visible-mender's ethic — the scar is the story), and what does your choice say about how you want a worn thing to be regarded?
**sources:** Mehra, Priya Ravish. "'Rafoogari' of Najibabad." *Textile Society of America 9th Biennial Symposium*, Oakland, California, 2004 (DigitalCommons@University of Nebraska–Lincoln, https://digitalcommons.unl.edu/tsaconf/456/).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium — the rafugari description (invisible darning, hereditary rafugars of Najibabad/Kashmir, keeping shawls in active use) is grounded in the citation-verified Mehra 2004 paper (verified against the UNL DigitalCommons page — note Perplexity initially gave the wrong symposium / year / city, corrected here). The repair-as-sustainability claim and the invisible/visible/discard tension are Consensus-backed (20 peer-reviewed papers; "extending clothing life is the most effective intervention" restated repeatedly, yet only ~3.3% of items repaired before disposal, with skill/cost/identity barriers and the Global-South-knowledge omission documented — see verification-consensus-backing.md). Confirm the framing against the Mehra paper's own text in the next pass.

## entry: the-natural-or-the-synthetic
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** natural, synthetic, cotton, polyester, organic, fibre, material, wool, microplastic, biodegradable, eco, fabric, plastic, recycled
**felt as:** just use natural fibres, they're sustainable; cotton is better than polyester; organic cotton is the eco choice; synthetics are bad; avoid plastic fabrics; natural means biodegradable; which fabric is the green one.
**the_tension:** The intuitive sustainable choice is the natural fibre — cotton, wool, linen — over the synthetic. Lifecycle assessment complicates this badly: there is no single "sustainable" fibre. Cotton carries heavy water, pesticide and land burdens (conventional cotton's footprint is severe, and even organic cotton is water-intensive); synthetics like polyester shed microplastics — estimated at around a third of primary marine microplastics — and depend on fossil resources; and which fibre "wins" flips depending on the impact category you weigh (climate, water, toxicity, microplastics, biodegradability), the energy mix and the country of production. The newer finding sharpens it: natural and semi-synthetic microfibres also pollute, sometimes as harmfully, so even "it biodegrades" is not clean. And research keeps pointing past the fibre: how long the garment is used, how it is washed and how much is produced often matter more than the material swap. The student's move is to ask whether their "sustainable material" choice actually reduces the impact that matters most for this garment — or whether it is a swap that feels green while the real leverage sits in use, longevity and volume. (The counter holds: fibre choice is not nothing — a genuinely lower-impact material in the relevant category is a real, immediately actionable improvement, and refusing to choose because "it's complicated" is its own evasion.)
**questions_it_invites:** For this garment, which impact matters most — water, climate, microplastics, biodegradability — and does your fibre choice actually reduce *that*, or just feel natural? · Is the fibre swap the real leverage here, or is it use, longevity and how much you make — and are you choosing the material to avoid the harder question?
**failure_modes:** swapping to a "natural" or "organic" fibre and declaring the garment sustainable while ignoring the impact category that actually dominates; treating synthetics as simply bad or naturals as simply good when the lifecycle says it depends; using "no fibre is perfect" as an excuse to stop thinking about material at all.
**sources:** Fletcher & Grose, *Fashion & Sustainability: Design for Change* (Laurence King, 2012); and textile lifecycle-assessment literature on fibre impacts (no single superior fibre).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; the "no perfect fibre" finding is strongly supported (cotton's water/pesticide burden; polyester microplastics — Henry et al.; category-dependence; natural microfibres also pollute — Jolly; use/longevity/volume often outweigh fibre — Laitala, Munasinghe). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: recycling-or-making-less
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** recycle, recycled, circular, closed-loop, recyclable, downcycle, blend, waste, reduce, less, reuse, fibre, regenerate, take-back
**felt as:** make it recyclable; design it for the circular economy; use recycled fibres; close the loop; we'll recycle it at end of life; recycling solves the waste; just make it recyclable and it's sustainable; how do we make fashion circular.
**the_tension:** "Make it recyclable / circular" has become the reflex answer to fashion's waste. The lifecycle evidence is sobering: textile-to-textile recycling is still marginal — most garments are blends that current processes cannot cleanly separate, mechanical recycling degrades fibres into lower-value downcycling, and only a small fraction of clothing is recycled at all; one consequential analysis found that scaling textile-to-textile recycling to a tenth of EU consumption would cut climate impact by well under one percent. Across the studies a hierarchy keeps reappearing: *reducing* production and *extending* a garment's life, then reuse, beat recycling — because most of recycling's benefit comes from displacing virgin production, which is weak when the displaced fibre was cheap. The student's move is to ask whether "recyclable" is doing real work here or supplying permission to keep producing — and where, between making less, making it last, and making it recyclable, the real leverage sits. (The counter holds: recycling is not worthless — it diverts waste from landfill and incineration and displaces some virgin material, and recycled fibres generally carry lower impact than virgin — so dismissing it entirely is as lazy as treating it as the whole answer; it is just downstream of the bigger levers.)
**questions_it_invites:** Is "recyclable" doing real work for this garment, or supplying permission to keep producing and discarding? · Between making less, making it last, and making it recyclable, where does the real leverage sit here — and which are you actually choosing?
**failure_modes:** designing "for recycling" while the garment is a blend no process can cleanly recycle, so it downcycles or lands in waste anyway; letting the recyclability claim license continued overproduction (the circular alibi); dismissing recycling entirely when it still beats landfill and displaces some virgin material.
**sources:** Ellen MacArthur Foundation, *A New Textiles Economy: Redesigning Fashion's Future* (2017); and textile-LCA literature on the limits of recycling versus reduction and reuse.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; strongly two-sided (recycling limited by blends/downcycling and small aggregate benefit — Sandin consequential LCA, Silva, Juanga-Labayen; reduction/reuse/life-extension beat recycling — Levänen, Sandin & Peters; yet recycling still beats landfill and recycled < virgin — Karim, Moazzem). See verification-consensus-backing.md. (Relates to manufacturing's the-material-and-its-afterlife and slow-design's the-new-model-or-the-lasting-thing, but is textile-recycling-specific.) Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-second-hand-and-its-afterlife
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**vocabulary:** second-hand, used, donate, donation, charity, thrift, resale, reuse, vintage, export, mitumba, waste, afterlife, give away
**felt as:** just donate it, donating is sustainable; give old clothes to charity; second-hand is the eco choice; resell it; thrifting saves clothes from landfill; donating clears my conscience; someone poorer will wear it; reuse extends its life.
**the_tension:** Donating or reselling used clothes feels like the clearly sustainable, even charitable, end for a garment — it extends the life, and lifecycle studies agree reuse is among the lowest-impact options. But the global trade in that "donated" clothing complicates the virtue. Andrew Brooks (*Clothing Poverty*, 2015) traces how clothing given away in the Global North is baled and exported to the Global South, where — at Kantamanto in Accra, in Mozambique, across East Africa — it both clothes millions affordably and creates livelihoods *and* arrives in volumes that exceed demand, undermining local textile industries and dumping unsellable waste, a pattern critics name "waste colonialism". A further finding cuts at the comfort: people who buy second-hand tend to buy *more* new clothing too, so resale may supplement rather than displace fast-fashion consumption. The student's move is to ask what actually happens to this garment after it is "donated" — whether the gesture extends a real life or exports a disposal problem with a clear conscience attached — and whether reuse here genuinely reduces consumption or merely licenses more. (The counter holds: second-hand clothing is the primary, affordable source of clothes for many of the world's poorest and sustains real livelihoods — dismissing the trade wholesale erases the people it genuinely serves.)
**questions_it_invites:** What actually happens to this garment after it's donated — does the gesture extend a real life, or export a disposal problem with a clear conscience attached? · Does buying or designing for second-hand here genuinely reduce consumption, or quietly license more — and for whom does the trade work?
**failure_modes:** treating donation as guilt-free disposal that "someone poorer will use", while the surplus becomes someone else's waste; dismissing the second-hand trade entirely, erasing the affordable clothing and livelihoods it genuinely provides; assuming second-hand consumption displaces fast fashion when it may sit alongside it.
**sources:** Brooks, *Clothing Poverty: The Hidden World of Fast Fashion and Second-Hand Clothes* (Zed Books, 2015); and the second-hand-trade and reuse literature (livelihoods and affordability vs waste-export and rebound).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Consensus-checked; genuinely two-sided (second-hand clothes the world's poorest affordably and creates livelihoods — Brooks 2025, Baden, Sumo — yet undermines local industry and dumps waste / "waste colonialism" — Manieson/Kantamanto, Hoang, Guo — and may not displace new buying, the rebound finding — Peleg Mizrachi). See verification-consensus-backing.md. Carries the Global-South dimension alongside repair-and-the-rafugar. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: slow-fashion-or-the-livelihood
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**vocabulary:** slow, fast, fashion, deceleration, livelihood, jobs, labour, garment, worker, volume, production, decelerate, less, factory, Bangladesh, RMG, supply, sweatshop
**felt as:** we should just slow fashion down; fast fashion is the problem; make less, that's the answer; reduce production; the industry needs to decelerate; but those factories employ people; what happens to the workers if we make less; slowing down sounds nice for rich people.
**the_tension:** The dominant call is to *slow fashion down* — Kate Fletcher named the term in "Slow Fashion" (*The Ecologist*, 2007), and Niinimäki et al. (*Nature Reviews Earth & Environment*, 2020) make the empirical case for "a deceleration of manufacturing" and decreasing clothing purchases, with Peters et al. proposing fast fashion be eliminated as a business model. The counter is not a defence of waste but of the people inside the system: the same fast-fashion volume that pollutes also employs tens of millions in low- and middle-income countries, and the deceleration that helps the planet can displace exactly those livelihoods — Repp (*Resources, Conservation & Recycling*, 2021) models a circular/deceleration transition *reducing* apparel-production jobs in producing countries while shifting them to reuse/recycling, and Barua's work on the Bangladesh ready-made-garment workforce traces the displacement a slow-fashion shift threatens. So the empirical picture is genuinely two-sided: less volume is lower impact *and* fewer jobs for those who can least absorb the loss. The student's move is to ask whether "slow down" here is a position they can hold from a distance, and what becomes of the hands that currently make the volume — and whether the design imagines a transition that carries those livelihoods or simply subtracts them.
**questions_it_invites:** When you say "make less", whose making is the less — and what happens to the people whose living is in the volume you'd cut? · Is the deceleration you're picturing one that carries the producing communities with it, or one that lands its cost on them?
**failure_modes:** treating "slow fashion" as self-evidently good while ignoring that the volume cut is also a livelihood cut for producing-country workers; defending fast-fashion volume as "jobs" while ignoring the environmental and safety cost those jobs are paid at (Rana Plaza); designing a deceleration that quietly exports its human cost to the Global South.
**sources:** Fletcher, "Slow Fashion" (*The Ecologist*, 2007); Niinimäki et al., "The environmental price of fast fashion" (*Nature Reviews Earth & Environment* 1, 2020); Repp et al., "Circular economy-induced global employment shifts in apparel value chains: Job reduction in apparel production activities, job growth in reuse and recycling activities" (*Resources, Conservation & Recycling*, 2021).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high (empirical) — Consensus-checked and genuinely two-sided: deceleration pole (Niinimäki 2020 explicit "decelerate manufacturing"; Peters 2021; Bick 2018 environmental-justice) vs livelihood pole with hard evidence (Repp 2021 job-reduction modelling — title/venue/year Exa-verified, author Lars Repp et al.; Williams 2022; Barua 2025 Bangladesh RMG displacement; Wan 2021 "progress at high social/environmental cost"). Confirm Repp 2021 pagination/volume and Barua 2025 venue in the verification pass. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-hide-or-the-plastic
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** leather, vegan, hide, animal, plastic, polyurethane, PU, synthetic, faux, pleather, mycelium, by-product, tanning, durability, cruelty-free, biodegradable
**felt as:** use vegan leather, it's cruelty-free; faux leather is the ethical choice; avoid animal products; plastic leather is more sustainable; real leather is cruel; vegan means greener; just use the plant-based one; which leather is the kind one.
**the_tension:** The intuitive ethical choice is the vegan substitute over the animal hide — no animal, no cruelty, so it reads as the sustainable pick. Lifecycle assessment complicates this sharply and does not resolve cleanly to either side: da Silva et al. (2026) find bovine leather the highest-impact and fossil-based PU the lowest in a cradle-to-gate comparison, *but* flag "a significant trade-off between impacts and durability" and exclude the use and disposal phases where a hide's far longer life and a plastic's microplastic-shedding, non-biodegradable end change the picture; Ding et al. (2011) split it cleanly — hide better on human-health and resource categories, PU better on the ecological-environment category; and Hildebrandt et al. (2020) find substitutes win *only* when "coupled with less frequent replacement". A maturing third pole — biofabricated mycelium leather — reports a fraction of bovine's footprint in early studies but is unscaled and durability-unproven. So the hinge is not "vegan is worse" or "leather is worse" but lifespan, whether the hide is counted as a meat-industry by-product, and what happens at end-of-life. The student's move is to ask which impact and which life-stage their "ethical" material claim actually rests on — and whether the vegan choice trades animal harm for a fossil-plastic that outlives the garment as waste.
**questions_it_invites:** When you call the substitute "the ethical choice", which harm are you weighing — the animal, the fossil resource, the microplastic, the lifespan — and does your material actually win on *that* one? · If the plastic alternative wears out and discards faster than the hide, is it the lower-impact choice over the whole life, or only at the moment of buying?
**failure_modes:** treating "vegan" as automatically lower-impact while the fossil-PU substitute sheds microplastics and outlives the garment as non-biodegradable waste; defending animal leather without counting the tanning chemistry and whether it is genuinely a by-product; reaching for unscaled mycelium leather as a settled answer when its durability and scale are unproven.
**sources:** Ding et al., comparative LCA of leather and synthetic-leather (poly­urethane) materials (2011); da Silva et al., cradle-to-gate LCA of bovine, synthetic and bio-based leather alternatives (2026); Hildebrandt et al., LCA of leather and leather substitutes (2020).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium-high (empirical) — Consensus-checked, genuinely two-sided and nuanced (three-way): da Silva 2026 (bovine highest, fossil-PU lowest cradle-to-gate, but impact/durability trade-off, excludes use/disposal); Ding 2011 (clean human-health/resource vs ecological split); Hildebrandt 2020 (substitute wins only with less-frequent replacement); D'Adamo 2024 (experts caution synthetics "may have greater drawbacks"); mycelium third pole unscaled. Confirm exact da Silva/Ding/Hildebrandt venues, years and pagination in the verification pass before live. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-certification-or-the-claim
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**vocabulary:** certification, certified, label, ecolabel, standard, GOTS, OEKO-TEX, Higg, greenwashing, claim, verified, third-party, trust, audit, transparency, proliferation
**felt as:** get it certified, then it's sustainable; we'll use a recognised ecolabel; certification proves it; just put the sustainability label on; which standard should we certify to; certified means trustworthy; the label says organic so it's fine; do these labels even mean anything.
**the_tension:** Reaching for a sustainability certification feels like the rigorous move — a third party verifies the claim, so it is not just marketing. The evidence is genuinely split. On one side, third-party certification *does* work: Behre et al. (2025) find certification labels raise the credibility of sustainability communication and purchase intention, and reviews of ecolabels (Kesidou et al., 2025) confirm rigorous third-party verification builds transparency and trust and sets benchmarks designers can align to. On the other, the same literature documents how certification can underwrite greenwashing: Nygaard (2023) shows certifications raise willingness-to-pay yet carry real risks of greenwashing, free-riding and "structural inertia" that blocks better technologies; the proliferation of competing, inconsistent schemes confuses consumers and erodes the very trust certification promises; and the Higg Materials Sustainability Index had its consumer-facing transparency programme suspended in 2022 after the Norwegian Consumer Authority found its data could mislead. So a certificate is neither proof nor alibi by default. The student's move is to ask what the certification on *this* garment actually verifies, who verifies it and how, and whether the label is doing real assurance work or supplying a green badge that ends the thinking.
**questions_it_invites:** What does this certification actually verify — the fibre, the factory, the whole supply chain — and who checks, and how? · Is the label doing real assurance work here, or is it a green badge that lets the harder questions stop?
**failure_modes:** treating any sustainability certificate as proof and ending the enquiry there; dismissing all certification as greenwashing when rigorous third-party schemes genuinely raise standards and trust; certifying to a narrow standard (one fibre, one stage) and presenting it as a whole-garment sustainability claim.
**sources:** Nygaard, "Is sustainable certification's ability to combat greenwashing trustworthy?" (*Frontiers in Sustainability* 4, 2023, DOI 10.3389/frsus.2023.1188069); Behre et al., "Signaling Sustainability in Online Fashion Consumption: The Role of Credibility induced by Certification Labels" (*Cleaner and Responsible Consumption*, 2025); Kesidou et al., review of eco-credentials in the textile and fashion industry (2025).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high (empirical) — Consensus-checked and two-sided: certification raises credibility/trust/willingness-to-pay and third-party verification works (Behre 2025; Nygaard 2023; Kesidou 2025) vs greenwashing risk, scheme proliferation/consumer confusion, structural inertia and the 2022 Higg suspension after the Norwegian Consumer Authority finding (Nygaard 2023; Adamkiewicz 2022; Badhwar 2024). Nygaard venue Exa-verified (*Frontiers in Sustainability* 4, 2023, DOI 10.3389/frsus.2023.1188069). Confirm Kesidou 2025 status (SSRN vs journal) and Behre 2025 pagination before live. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: access-or-ownership
**discipline:** sustainable-fashion
**vocabulary:** rental, rent, access, ownership, sharing, product-service, PSS, circular, subscription, library, reuse, transport, dry-cleaning, laundering, utilisation, displacement
**felt as:** rent it instead of buying; rental is the circular answer; sharing clothes is greener; design it as a service not a product; access over ownership; rental cuts waste; just make it a subscription; renting must be more sustainable than owning.
**the_tension:** Renting rather than owning — clothing as a service — feels like the obviously circular, lower-impact model: one garment worn by many. The lifecycle evidence refuses to settle it. Several LCAs do find rental reduces impact: Monticelli et al. (2022) and Gray et al. (2025) report circular business models cutting greenhouse-gas emissions by extending the uses per garment (Gray's range 14–26%). But the same studies and others find the gain can vanish or reverse: Levänen et al. (2021) find a SHARE/rental scenario produces the *highest* global-warming impact of five options once it increases customers' mobility; Zamani et al. (2017) show increased customer transport can "completely offset" the production savings; and Amasawa et al. find rented garments carry *greater* life-cycle emissions per piece once dry-cleaned. Johnson et al. (2021) conclude the savings depend entirely on consumer behaviour — how many extra wears the rental actually adds, whether it displaces a purchase, and how far people travel. So access-over-ownership is a real lever or a net loss depending on transport, cleaning and displacement. The student's move is to ask whether the rental model they are designing actually adds wears and displaces buying, or just adds delivery trips and dry-cleaning to a garment that would have been worn anyway.
**questions_it_invites:** Does renting this garment add real extra wears and replace a purchase, or just add transport and cleaning to a thing that would have been worn regardless? · Where does the impact of your access model actually sit — in the sharing, or in the logistics and laundering the sharing requires?
**failure_modes:** assuming rental is circular and therefore lower-impact while delivery, returns and dry-cleaning erase the benefit; designing a rental model that does not displace new purchases (people rent *and* buy); ignoring that the gain depends on utilisation — how many extra wears the model genuinely adds.
**sources:** Levänen et al., "Innovative recycling or extended use? Comparing the global warming potential of different ownership and end-of-life scenarios for textiles" (*Environmental Research Letters*, 2021); Johnson et al., "Product-Service Systems and Sustainability: Analysing the Environmental Impacts of Rental Clothing" (*Sustainability*, 2021); Zamani et al., "Life cycle assessment of clothing libraries: can collaborative consumption reduce the environmental impact of fast fashion?" (*Journal of Cleaner Production*, 2017).
