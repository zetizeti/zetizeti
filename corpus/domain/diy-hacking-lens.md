# Domain corpus: The DIY / Hacking / Incremental Lens

> **Part B — cross-cutting lens.** Synthetic (original prose, written for zetizeti), retrieved per turn via FTS5.
> Where a discipline file describes that discipline's tensions neutrally, a lens is aimed at the learner's
> **conventional** assumptions — here, the assumptions that a design is authored by its designer, arrives
> finished, and is used rather than altered. Its vocabulary is keyed to the words a student reaches for when
> they mean *I will make the whole thing and then hand it over*, so the lens activates exactly then.
> **Held two-sided on purpose.** DIY, hacking, modding, open source and incremental design each *challenge*
> the finished-authored-object; none of them wins by default. Every entry gives the finished object real
> weight — a design handed over half-made can be an abdication, an open licence can be an unpaid obligation,
> and a mod can be someone else's free labour. A lens that only argued one way would be a new orthodoxy.
> **Intent register (`felt as:`).** Each entry carries the plain, colloquial way a student voices the
> conventional move being questioned, weighted in retrieval so the lens activates on intent rather than on
> the precise term (architecture.md §5.1). Exact-word FTS5; no embeddings (invariant #1).
>
> **Register field.** Entries here declare `**register:** making` only where the tension cannot be taken up
> without deciding how the thing gets produced. None of this first batch does: DIY, hacking and incremental
> design are *about* making, but these seven tensions are about authorship, labour, power over tools and who
> finishes the work — which is why they survive the concept-only focus. That is a fact worth noticing rather
> than a coincidence: the interesting questions in this territory are not fabrication questions.
>

## entry: the-mod-and-the-unpaid-hour
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** community, mod, modding, fan, user-generated, contribution, ecosystem, platform, free, volunteer, passion, engagement, loyalty, plugin, extension, hobby
**felt as:** let the community build on top of it; users can make their own versions; we'll open it up and people will extend it; the fans will make content for it; it costs us nothing and they love doing it; it's a hobby for them, not work.
**the_tension:** Opening a design so its users can modify it looks like a gift in both directions — they get agency, the thing gets richer, nobody is paid because nobody is working. Julian Kücklich's study of game modding named the difficulty in that account: modding is *playbour*, an activity that fits neither work nor leisure, voluntarily given and unwaged, enjoyed and exploited at once (a formulation he draws from Tiziana Terranova's account of free labour online). Kücklich's specific charge is that the industry outsources risk to modders while end-user licences leave the intellectual property with the publisher, so the value returns to the platform and the recognition rarely does. Against that stands Hector Postigo's finding, which Kücklich reports rather than dismisses: modders describe owning their productive process, and that ownership is exactly what makes the work feel unalienated to them. The student's move is to ask what their design is asking other people to do for free, who captures what those people make, and whether the arrangement would still look generous if the labour were named as labour.
**failure_modes:** designing a "community layer" that is really an unpaid production line; keeping the rights while giving away the credit; assuming that because contributors enjoy it, nothing is owed; refusing all contribution because it might be exploitative, and so denying people the agency they are asking for.
**questions_it_invites:** What work is this design asking other people to do for nothing — and who ends up owning what they make? · If you named that contribution as labour rather than as community, what would you owe, and would you still design it this way?
**sources:** Kücklich, "Precarious Playbour: Modders and the Digital Games Industry", *The Fibreculture Journal* 5 (FCJ-025, 2005); Postigo, "From Pong to Planet Quake: Post-Industrial Transitions from Leisure to Work", *Information, Communication & Society* 6(4) (2003); Terranova, "Free Labor: Producing Culture for the Digital Economy", *Social Text* 18(2) (2000).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — playbour is Kücklich's own term and central thesis, verified at the Fibreculture Journal's own text of FCJ-025; the Postigo and Terranova positions are quoted and cited within that text, and the two-sidedness (exploitation vs non-alienated ownership) is Kücklich's own, not imported.

## entry: broken-world-or-the-new-thing
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** new, novel, innovation, breakthrough, launch, next, upgrade, version, obsolete, broken, fix, patch, workaround, maintain, decay, legacy
**felt as:** we need something genuinely new here; this is the innovation; the old one is obsolete; let's rebuild it properly rather than patch it; a workaround is a sign we got it wrong; maintenance is what you do when you've stopped designing.
**the_tension:** Design is usually narrated from novelty forward — the new thing arrives, works, and later decays. Steven Jackson proposes inverting the starting point: take erosion, breakdown and decay as the normal condition, and read the world as constantly falling apart and constantly being repaired. On that reading the workaround is not the failure of a design but the site where design actually happens, and he argues the internet itself grew by bumping into the limits of existing protocols and working around them, leaving properties we now list as virtues. The counter is not that he is wrong about repair but that a practice which only ever patches inherits every bad decision underneath it: some things genuinely need remaking, and calling every rebuild a failure of care would freeze a design in the shape of its first mistake. The student's move is to ask whether the thing in front of them wants renewing or repairing, and to notice which of those two their training makes them reach for first.
**failure_modes:** treating a rebuild as obviously more serious work than a repair; shipping novelty because novelty is legible as design; patching indefinitely around a decision that should be reopened; romanticising repair until nothing is ever allowed to end.
**questions_it_invites:** If you started from what breaks here rather than from what is new, what would you be designing instead? · Which of these is this — a thing that wants remaking, or a thing that wants keeping going, and how would you tell the difference?
**sources:** Jackson, "Rethinking Repair", in Gillespie, Boczkowski & Foot (eds), *Media Technologies: Essays on Communication, Materiality, and Society* (MIT Press, 2014), pp. 221–240.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — "broken world thinking" is Jackson's own term and the chapter's stated project; pagination and volume verified against the publisher's and the author's own listings. The counter-pole is constructed rather than cited, and is flagged as such: it is the ordinary engineering objection, not a named opponent.

## entry: the-maker-and-the-carer
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** maker, build, ship, prototype, portfolio, artefact, output, hackathon, workshop, studio, produce, deliverable, creator, hands-on
**felt as:** i want to actually build something; make something real; a prototype speaks louder than a document; i need artefacts for my portfolio; talking about it isn't designing; the makers are the ones who ship.
**the_tension:** Making is treated in design and technology culture as the activity that counts, and there are good reasons for it: Debbie Chachra, writing as an engineering educator who teaches fabrication, is explicit that access to making matters enormously, particularly for people historically kept out of it. Her objection is to what the identity does around the edges. She argues that celebrating creation as the valuable act quietly devalues teaching, criticism, analysis, repair and above all caregiving — work that is disproportionately done by women, leaves no artefact, and cannot be put in a box and sold. Ursula Franklin's distinction sharpens what is at stake in the word: prescriptive technologies split work into components nobody controls, holistic ones leave the maker in command of the whole process, and "making" flattens the difference. The counter is real and Chachra concedes part of it: the alternative to making is not nothing, and a design education that never puts anything into anyone's hands teaches a thin kind of confidence. The student's move is to ask what their project actually asks of people, and whether the parts of it that leave no artefact are being counted at all.
**failure_modes:** treating the deliverable as the work and the sustaining of it as overhead; describing yourself by what you produce rather than by what you do; designing only the parts that photograph; dismissing making as ideology and producing nothing anyone can hold.
**questions_it_invites:** What in this project leaves no artefact behind — and is that part being counted as design at all? · Who has to keep this going after you have finished it, and what does your design assume about them?
**sources:** Chachra, "Why I Am Not a Maker", *The Atlantic*, 23 January 2015; Franklin, *The Real World of Technology* (CBC Massey Lectures, 1989), for the prescriptive/holistic technologies distinction Chachra draws on.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the Atlantic piece was read in full at source; the caregiving argument, the Franklin distinction and the explicit concession that making matters "especially for groups that previously haven't had access" are all hers, in her own framing. The Massey Lectures attribution is verified via Chachra's own citation of it.

## entry: the-tool-you-can-open
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** tool, platform, system, professional, certified, licensed, expert, proprietary, sealed, black box, simple, powerful, automate, service, access
**felt as:** leave that to the professionals; it needs to be handled by someone qualified; we'll abstract that away so users don't have to think about it; the system takes care of it; making it openable would just confuse people; power users can always ask support.
**the_tension:** Ivan Illich divides tools by what they do to the person holding them. A convivial tool can be used by anybody who wants it, as often or as rarely as they like, for a purpose the user chose, without prior certification — it lets someone put their own meaning into the world. An industrial tool denies that, and lets its designers determine the meanings available to everyone else; his examples of how a tool is closed are institutional as much as technical, including professional monopoly and licensing, and the ordinary fact that simple hand tools will no longer open the object. The counter is not trivial: some tools genuinely should require certification, expertise is not merely a guild protecting itself, and an interface that exposes everything to everyone can transfer burden rather than power. The student's move is to ask what their design permits its user to do without asking permission, and whether the closures they are building in are protecting the user or protecting the designer.
**failure_modes:** sealing a thing and calling it simplicity; treating every abstraction as a kindness; requiring credentials for work that does not need them; opening everything and calling the resulting burden freedom.
**questions_it_invites:** What can someone do with this without anyone's permission — and what did you close off, for whose benefit? · If the person using this wanted to change how it works, where exactly would they be stopped?
**sources:** Illich, *Tools for Conviviality* (Harper & Row, 1973), particularly the account of convivial versus industrial tools and the institutional means by which tools are closed.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the convivial/industrial distinction, the "without previous certification" criterion and the professional-monopoly examples were read at source in the text of the book; expressed here in original prose, with no passage reproduced. The counter-pole is constructed and flagged as such.

## entry: half-a-good-thing
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** finished, complete, deliver, handover, scope, budget, constraint, minimum, unfinished, extend, adapt, self-build, incremental, phase
**felt as:** we don't have the budget to do it properly; either we do the whole thing or we don't do it; ship the minimum and iterate later; the user can customise it afterwards; leaving it unfinished feels like giving up; it has to be complete before anyone sees it.
**the_tension:** Faced with a subsidy that could buy the land or build the houses but not both, Elemental's Quinta Monroy in Iquique delivered what Alejandro Aravena called half of a good house: the structure, services and the parts a family could never build alone, with a deliberate void beside each unit for the residents to fill as they could afford to. Within a few years the voids were filled, and the approach is a serious argument that scarcity can be designed with rather than apologised for, and that the inhabitant is part of the provision rather than its recipient. The evidence since is genuinely two-sided. A 2020 longitudinal study of the same site found extensions exceeding anything the designers had planned for, windowless ground-floor rooms, encroachment on shared space and conflict between neighbours, and concluded that a good design was not by itself enough to prevent conditions returning toward what preceded it — the participation, its authors note, ended the day the keys were handed over. The student's move is to ask which half of their project they are keeping and which they are handing over, and what governs the half they no longer control.
**failure_modes:** calling an under-resourced deliverable "incremental" to avoid saying it is unfinished; handing over the open half with no rules and no support and treating whatever happens as agency; designing the increment so tightly that nothing is really handed over; insisting on completeness when the budget makes completeness a fiction.
**questions_it_invites:** Which half of this are you finishing, and which are you leaving for someone else to complete — and why that line? · What happens in the part you no longer control, and who is answerable for it in five years?
**sources:** Aravena / Elemental, Quinta Monroy, Iquique (2004), the "half of a good house" incremental model; O'Brien et al., "Beyond the freedom to build: Long-term outcomes of Elemental's incremental housing in Quinta Monroy" (2020), a longitudinal study of the same site; Namias, "Half a good home isn't enough", *Le Monde diplomatique* (English edition), April 2016.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the project's design logic, budget figure and the "half of a good house" phrasing were verified across the published study, the Le Monde diplomatique piece and contemporary architectural reporting; the critical findings are quoted from the 2020 longitudinal paper's own conclusions rather than characterised second-hand. ⚠️ Consensus check still pending on the incremental-housing literature more broadly — the counter-pole currently rests on one longitudinal study plus press criticism, which is enough to hold the entry two-sided but should be strengthened before Gate-4.

## entry: open-and-who-maintains-it
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** open source, open, licence, free, community, contribute, fork, repository, release, adopt, sustainable, infrastructure, dependency, library
**felt as:** we'll just open source it; put it on github and let the community take it forward; making it open means it can't die; someone will pick it up; it's free so there's no cost; open is obviously the responsible thing to do.
**the_tension:** Opening a project is often described as the move that makes it survivable — no owner, no gatekeeper, anyone can carry it on. Nadia Eghbal's report for the Ford Foundation describes what that has actually produced at scale: public code is the infrastructure almost all software runs on, built and maintained largely by unpaid volunteers, with no organising body deciding what gets maintained and no obvious way for the value it creates to flow back. She cites a 2015 study of 133 heavily-used GitHub projects finding that roughly two-thirds depended on just one or two developers. Her own conclusion resists the easy reading in both directions: the decentralised, community-centred character is what made the thing rich in the first place, so money alone does not fix it and neither would centralising it. The student's move is to ask what opening their work actually transfers — rights, certainly, but also an obligation that has to land on somebody — and whether "open" is being used to name a plan or to avoid making one.
**failure_modes:** treating a licence as a maintenance plan; open-sourcing a thing at the point of abandoning it and calling that stewardship; assuming a community exists because a repository does; keeping something closed because opening it looks like work.
**questions_it_invites:** If you open this, whose evenings does it become — and have you asked them? · What are you actually transferring when you make this open: the right to use it, or the duty to keep it alive?
**sources:** Eghbal, *Roads and Bridges: The Unseen Labor Behind Our Digital Infrastructure* (Ford Foundation, 2016), including the 2015 Federal University of Minas Gerais study of 133 active GitHub projects it reports.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — read at source in the Ford Foundation's own publication and its summary; the one-or-two-maintainer figure, the roads-and-bridges framing and the "money alone won't fix it" conclusion are Eghbal's own. ⚠️ The 133-project study is cited here as Eghbal reports it; the primary paper has not been read directly and should be traced before Gate-4.

## entry: the-user-who-already-solved-it
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** user, need, research, insight, requirement, solution, brief, workaround, hack, adapt, improvise, field, observe, interview
**felt as:** we need to find out what users need; let's do the research and then design the solution; users can't tell you what they want; they describe problems, we produce answers; what they're doing now is just a workaround.
**the_tension:** The ordinary model is that designers and manufacturers produce and users receive. Eric von Hippel's long programme of research contradicts that as a general account: users frequently develop what they need themselves, ahead of any producer, particularly in small or new markets that producers find unattractive — his early work found scientific instrument innovations originating largely with the scientists using them, and Urban and von Hippel's study of printed-circuit-board CAD found that a large majority of identified lead users had developed their own modifications while almost none of the non-lead users had. On this reading the workaround in front of you is not a gap in the design; it is the design, arriving early. The counter has been argued in the same literature: Trott and colleagues review the lead-user concept and find conceptual, methodological and empirical weaknesses, note that the most radical innovations of recent decades were not user-developed, and warn against overstating the contribution. The student's move is to look at what the people they are designing for have already built for themselves, and to ask honestly whether their own contribution is discovery or tidying.
**failure_modes:** treating an existing workaround as evidence of ignorance rather than of design; designing from a stated need while ignoring the adapted object sitting on the desk; assuming every user is a lead user; handing the whole brief to users and calling the abdication participation.
**questions_it_invites:** What have the people you are designing for already made or altered for themselves here — and what does it tell you that your version does not? · If they have already solved it their way, what exactly is your design adding?
**sources:** von Hippel, *Democratizing Innovation* (MIT Press, 2005) and *The Sources of Innovation* (1988); Urban & von Hippel, "Lead User Analyses for the Development of New Industrial Products", *Management Science* 34(5) (1988); Trott & Hartmann et al., "Users as innovators? Exploring the limitations of user-driven innovation", *Prometheus* 31(2) (2013), for the critical position.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high on the position, medium on one figure — von Hippel's thesis and the Urban & von Hippel study were verified via Consensus (the latter at 1,223 citations, *Management Science*, 1988), as was the Trott critique. ⚠️ The lead-user percentages reported in the secondary literature (82% / 87% versus 1%) vary between sources and are therefore *described* here rather than stated as a number; the primary paper must be read before any figure enters this entry.

## entry: the-hands-on-imperative
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** access, open, closed, permission, allowed, official, unauthorised, restricted, curiosity, take apart, poke, explore, sanctioned, rules
**felt as:** we're not really allowed to touch that; you'd have to ask permission; it's not our system to change; someone would have to approve it; i just wanted to see how it worked; there's an official way to do this.
**the_tension:** The hacker ethic as Steven Levy set it down from MIT in the 1950s and 60s puts curiosity above permission: access to anything that might teach you how the world works should be unlimited, and one should always yield to the hands-on imperative. It is a serious position about learning — you understand a system by opening it, and asking first concedes that someone else decides what you may understand. The counter is that the imperative is stated without a subject: whose system, whose data, whose risk. The same principle that produces a repaired machine produces a breached one, and "all information should be free" reads differently when the information is about a person rather than about a protocol. The student's move is to notice where their own design assumes permission is required, ask whether that requirement protects anyone or merely preserves an arrangement — and to be equally clear about whose consent is genuinely owed.
**failure_modes:** treating a locked thing as automatically illegitimate; treating an official channel as automatically sufficient; confusing curiosity with entitlement when other people's data is inside the system; designing a barrier because a barrier looks responsible, without asking what it protects.
**questions_it_invites:** Where does this design require someone to ask permission — and what would actually go wrong if they did not? · If someone opened this up to understand it, whose interests would be touched, and which of those would you defend?
**sources:** Levy, *Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution* (Anchor Press/Doubleday, 1984), for the hacker ethic and the hands-on imperative.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high on the position, medium on the wording — the tenets and the "hands-on imperative" phrasing were verified against Levy's own description of the book and an independent teaching summary of the ethic; the specific numbered formulations circulate in many paraphrases, so no tenet is quoted verbatim here and the entry states the position rather than the text.

## entry: many-eyes-or-few-hands
**discipline:** diy-hacking
**vocabulary:** open source, review, community, transparent, audit, scrutiny, contributors, secure, bugs, quality, peer review, eyes, crowd
**felt as:** it's open so people can check it; anyone can audit the code; more contributors means better quality; transparency makes it safer; the community will catch problems; open source is more secure by design.
**the_tension:** Eric Raymond's account of Linux contrasted the cathedral — built carefully by a few — with the bazaar, built in the open with frequent releases and many contributors, and compressed the claim into what he called Linus's Law: given enough eyeballs, all bugs are shallow. It is the argument almost every "we'll make it open" decision leans on. The empirical picture is more awkward. Studying Red Hat Enterprise Linux, Meneely and Williams found files changed by nine or more developers were roughly sixteen times more likely to contain a vulnerability than files touched by fewer; a replication across the Linux kernel, PHP and Wireshark found files changed by six or more developers at least four times more likely; and a study of 159,254 Chromium code reviews found that files reviewed by *more* developers were, counter-intuitively, more likely to be vulnerable — though files reviewed by people with prior vulnerability-fixing experience were less so. Eyes are not interchangeable, and the number of them is not the variable that matters. The student's move is to ask who exactly will be looking at the thing they are opening, what those people know, and whether "open to everyone" has quietly been read as "checked by someone".
**failure_modes:** treating publication as review; counting contributors as a quality signal; assuming a large project is well-watched when its dependencies rest on one or two people; keeping a thing closed because openness alone will not secure it, when openness was never supposed to be the whole mechanism.
**questions_it_invites:** Who specifically will look at this once it is open, and what do they need to know to see what matters? · You are opening this so it can be checked — what is your evidence that anyone is checking?
**sources:** Raymond, "The Cathedral and the Bazaar", *First Monday* 3(2) (1998); Meneely & Williams, "Secure open source collaboration: an empirical study of Linus' Law" (2009); Meneely & Williams, "Strengthening the empirical analysis of the relationship between Linus' Law and software security" (2010), replicating across Linux, PHP and Wireshark; Meneely et al., "An empirical investigation of socio-technical code review metrics and security vulnerabilities" (2014), on 159,254 Chromium code reviews; Schryen, "Security of Open Source and Closed Source Software: An Empirical Comparison of Published Vulnerabilities", finding no significant difference in vulnerability severity between the two.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Raymond's essay verified at First Monday (3(2), 2 March 1998, and at the author's own canonical text); all four empirical findings verified via Consensus with their sample sizes and effect directions taken from the papers' own abstracts. ⚠️ The Meneely findings are associational, not causal, and the entry is worded accordingly; the primary papers should be read before Gate-4 rather than relied on through abstracts.
