# Domain corpus: The Withholding Lens

> **Part B — cross-cutting lens.** Synthetic, retrieved per turn via FTS5. Aimed at the learner's **conventional** reflex to supply: to explain, to guide, to recommend, to prefill, to cover every case, to build the thing. Its vocabulary is deliberately keyed to those conventional words, so the lens activates exactly when a student reaches for them.
> **Withholding is not subtraction, and the distinction is the reason this file exists.** `subtract-or-add` in `slow-design.md` asks whether removing something already there would serve better, and rests on the additive-bias literature. This lens asks a different question: whether to *supply in the first place* — the answer, the instruction, the recommendation, the feature, the artefact. Nothing has to be taken away for a withholding decision to have been made, and nobody sees what was withheld. That invisibility is what makes it hard to examine and worth a lens.
> **Held two-sided on purpose, and harder here than elsewhere.** This tool withholds answers by construction, so a lens that argued for withholding would be the tool teaching its own constitution back to the student. Every entry below carries the counter at full strength, and one entry (`who-pays-for-what-you-left-out`) exists to run against the rest.
> **Intent register (`felt as:`).** Each entry carries a `felt as:` line — the plain, colloquial way a student voices the *conventional* move this lens questions — weighted in retrieval, so the lens activates on intent rather than the precise word (architecture.md §5.1). Exact-word FTS5; no embeddings.
> **Provenance (entries 1–5, 20 September 2026):** `origin: synthetic` · `generated: 2026-09-20` · **every entry carries `provenance: pending`** and stays so until the three-pass (architecture.md §3a) and Prayas's human sign-off. Verification sets the pace, not breadth (invariant #0).
> **Citations passed the three-tool chain** — Consensus to find, `find-research-papers` to verify, Crossref to settle. Kornell/Hays/Bjork, Loibl/Tillema/Rummel/van Gog and Pierce (2012) were settled against the raw Crossref record; Pierce & Paulos (2014) and Baumer & Silberman (2011) against the simplified record, both ACM proceedings whose print, online and issued dates coincide. ⚠️ Kapur & Bielaczyc (2012) and Kirschner, Sweller & Clark (2006) were taken from the reference list of the settled Loibl record rather than queried directly; treat their page ranges as unconfirmed until checked.

## entry: the-answer-withheld
**discipline:** withholding
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** explain, tell, show, answer, guide, teach, tutorial, onboarding, walkthrough, tooltip, instructions, help, clear, obvious, demonstrate
**felt as:** just explain it to them clearly; add a tutorial so they know what to do; show them how it works up front; make it obvious, don't make them think; walk them through it; add some help text; i'll just tell them the answer.
**the_tension:** Making something clear and telling someone the answer feel like the same act of generosity, and they are not. Before you explain this, what would the person have had to work out if you had not — and is that working-out part of what you are trying to give them, or an obstacle in front of it?
Kornell, Hays and Bjork showed that an *unsuccessful* attempt to retrieve an answer, before the answer is given, produces better later memory than being shown the question and answer together — and their materials were built so that the attempt would fail, which rules out the explanation that guessing right is what helps (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 2009). Pan and colleagues found the same advantage for pretesting over posttesting across five experiments, with and without feedback (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 2021). The gap the learner notices is doing work that the explanation cannot do for them. (The counter holds, and it is the strongest counter in this file: Kirschner, Sweller and Clark argue that minimal guidance during instruction does not work, that it ignores the limits of working memory, and that for a novice with no prior knowledge to attach the struggle to, withholding the explanation produces confusion rather than learning — *Educational Psychologist*, 2006. A person stuck with no way forward is not being taught, and "let them figure it out" is also what neglect sounds like.)
**failure_modes:** explaining first, every time, so that nothing is ever encountered as a question; withholding from somebody who has nothing to attach the struggle to, and calling their confusion productive.
**questions_it_invites:** What would this person have to work out if you did not explain it — and is that the thing you wanted them to have? · Do they have enough to make an attempt with, or would you be leaving them with nothing?
**sources:** Kornell, Hays & Bjork, "Unsuccessful retrieval attempts enhance subsequent learning" (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 35(4), 2009, 989–998); Pan, Sana, Schmitt & Bjork, "Pretesting versus posttesting" (*Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 2021); Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, "Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work" (*Educational Psychologist*, 41, 2006).
**framing_confidence:** medium-high — the retrieval and pretesting findings are replicated and robust, and the Kirschner counter is a live and well-argued position rather than a token balance. ⚠️ The Pan author list beyond the first two names was not settled against Crossref. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: struggle-before-telling
**discipline:** withholding
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** first, before, order, sequence, prepare, brief, introduce, background, context, setup, primer, groundwork, teach, workshop, session
**felt as:** i'll brief them first, then let them try; give them the background before the exercise; set it up properly so they're not lost; introduce the concept, then the activity; they need context before they can start; let's do a primer first.
**the_tension:** Putting the explanation before the attempt is the default order in almost every workshop, brief and lesson, and it is rarely argued for — it is simply what happens. If you moved the explanation to *after* the attempt, what would change about what the person brings to it, and are you willing to accept a worse first attempt in exchange?
Productive failure proposes that attempting a problem *before* instruction builds better conceptual understanding than instruction first, because the attempt activates prior knowledge and makes the learner aware of their own gaps (Kapur & Bielaczyc, *Journal of the Learning Sciences*, 2012). **The evidence is genuinely mixed and the lens must not pretend otherwise.** Loibl, Tillema, Rummel and van Gog found that merely delaying instruction did *not* increase conceptual understanding, and that instruction-first was better for procedural fluency (*Instructional Science*, 2020). Mazziotti, Rummel, Deiglmayr and Loibl found no support for it in fifth-graders (*npj Science of Learning*, 2019). Braas and colleagues failed to replicate the effect and concluded that activating prior knowledge matters more than experiencing failure first-hand (*Instructional Science*, 2025). So the defensible claim is narrow: the order is a real design decision with real consequences, and the case for withholding the explanation is weaker than its advocates state. (The counter holds: for a skill that must simply be performed correctly, telling first is better, and an attempt made with nothing to attempt *with* produces neither understanding nor fluency.)
**failure_modes:** treating "let them struggle first" as a principle rather than a bet, and never checking whether the struggle produced anything; front-loading so much context that nothing is left to discover.
**questions_it_invites:** What does this person already have that an attempt could draw on, before you give them more? · If the first attempt is worse, what are you buying with it — and can you tell whether you got it?
**sources:** Kapur & Bielaczyc, "Designing for productive failure" (*Journal of the Learning Sciences*, 21, 2012, 45–83); Loibl, Tillema, Rummel & van Gog, "The effect of contrasting cases during problem solving prior to and after instruction" (*Instructional Science*, 48(2), 2020, 115–136); Mazziotti, Rummel, Deiglmayr & Loibl, "Probing boundary conditions of productive failure" (*npj Science of Learning*, 4, 2019).
**framing_confidence:** medium — deliberately stated as contested, because it is. Three of the four studies retrieved report a null or a failed replication, and the entry is written so that a student cannot take it as licence to withhold instruction. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: the-implication-is-not-to-design
**discipline:** withholding
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** build, make, app, platform, tool, solution, system, solve, feature, launch, product, prototype, develop, create, intervention
**felt as:** so i'll build an app for it; we need a platform where people can; the solution is a tool that; let's prototype something that solves this; i want to make a system for it; what if there was an app.
**the_tension:** Research into a problem almost always ends in something being built, because that is what the format expects — the finding arrives already shaped as a brief. What did you learn here that argues *against* making anything, and would you be able to say so if the answer were that nothing should be built?
Baumer and Silberman argue that human-computer interaction should be able to conclude that a technology *should not be built* — that "implications for design" is treated as the only legitimate ending, so the option of not designing is rarely examined even when the research supports it (*Proceedings of CHI*, 2011). They propose asking whether a technological intervention addresses a problem better than a non-technological one, whether it replaces a practice people valued, and whether it is worth the harms it introduces. The lens asks the student to make not-building a live option rather than a failure of nerve. (The counter holds, and genuinely: refusing to build is cheap, carries no risk, and can be a way of avoiding the work while sounding principled. Somebody living with the problem may want the imperfect thing now, and "we decided not to build it" is no use to them. A critique is not a gift.)
**failure_modes:** arriving at a build because the format required an outcome, rather than because the evidence pointed there; treating refusal as automatically the more thoughtful position, when it costs nothing and helps nobody.
**questions_it_invites:** If the honest conclusion were that nothing should be built here, could you say that — and to whom? · Who is left holding the problem if you do not build it?
**sources:** Baumer & Silberman, "When the implication is not to design (technology)" (*Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, CHI '11, 2011); Pierce, "Undesigning technology: considering the negation of design by design" (*Proceedings of the SIGCHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, CHI '12, 2012, 957–966).
**framing_confidence:** high — both papers say what the entry says they say, settled against Crossref, and the counter about refusal being cheap is the standard and fair objection. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: limitation-as-the-feature
**discipline:** withholding
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** capable, powerful, flexible, unlimited, settings, options, configurable, modes, customise, advanced, full, complete, everything, versatile, all
**felt as:** make it powerful and flexible; give them full control; let them customise everything; add an advanced mode; it should handle every case; more capability is better; why limit what it can do.
**the_tension:** A limit is normally something you apologise for — what the thing cannot yet do, to be removed in the next version. But a limit can be the thing somebody comes for. What can this deliberately not do, and would anybody choose it *because* of that?
Pierce and Paulos build what they call *counterfunctional things* — objects that work against part of their own expected function, such as digital cameras with the review screen removed or made inaccessible — and argue that omission can be a defining feature of the experience of use rather than merely a decision taken during design (*Proceedings of DIS*, 2014). The claim is not that less is better but that a limitation can create a use that its absence could not. The lens asks the student to name what their thing refuses, and whether that refusal is a shortfall or the offer. (The counter holds: an imposed limit that the person did not choose is just a worse product, and "it's minimal on purpose" is the oldest cover for unfinished work. Constraints that read as elegant to a designer often read as broken to somebody trying to get something done.)
**failure_modes:** dressing up a missing feature as a deliberate constraint; a limitation that only the designer enjoys, because only the designer chose it.
**questions_it_invites:** What does this deliberately not do, and would somebody choose it for that? · Is this constraint something the person opts into, or something you imposed on them?
**sources:** Pierce & Paulos, "Counterfunctional things: exploring possibilities in designing digital limitations" (*Proceedings of the 2014 Conference on Designing Interactive Systems*, DIS '14, 2014); Pierce, "Undesigning technology: considering the negation of design by design" (CHI '12, 2012, 957–966).
**framing_confidence:** high — design-research position papers reporting their own artefacts, not empirical claims about people, and the entry does not attribute an effect to them. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: who-pays-for-what-you-left-out
**discipline:** withholding
**provenance:** pending
**vocabulary:** simple, lean, restrained, minimal, less, essential, focused, stripped, pared, deliberate, intentional, opinionated, constraint, discipline, scope
**felt as:** we're keeping it deliberately simple; it's opinionated on purpose; we stripped it back to the essentials; that's out of scope by design; less is more here; it's a constraint, not a limitation; we're being disciplined about it.
**the_tension:** Every decision to withhold moves work somewhere else — onto the person using the thing, onto whoever supports them, onto somebody who now has to find another way. Naming a restraint as deliberate does not make that transfer disappear. Who does the work you decided not to do, and did they agree to it?
This entry runs against the other four in this file on purpose. Withholding is cheap for the person withholding and is paid for by somebody who is usually not in the conversation: the learner who needed the explanation and did not get it, the user who needed the feature and now maintains a workaround, the person who is told the tool is "opinionated" when what they mean is that it will not do the thing they came for. Restraint has a specific appeal to designers because it reads as taste and costs nothing to claim. (The counter holds, and this entry would be dishonest without it: supplying everything is also a transfer — of attention, of choice, of the effort of working out which of forty options applies. A thing that refuses nothing asks the person to do the refusing.)
**failure_modes:** calling something a principled constraint when it is an unbudgeted cost passed to the user; deciding on somebody's behalf that the work you left undone is work worth their doing.
**questions_it_invites:** Who does the work you decided not to do here? · Was the person who pays for this restraint ever asked, and would they have agreed?
**sources:** Baumer & Silberman, "When the implication is not to design (technology)" (CHI '11, 2011); Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, "Why minimal guidance during instruction does not work" (*Educational Psychologist*, 41, 2006).
**framing_confidence:** high as a framing, and it makes no empirical claim of its own — it names a cost transfer and asks who bears it. Framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.
