# Domain corpus: The Manufacturing Lens — design and the making of things

> **Part B — cross-cutting lens (SEED).** Synthetic (original prose, written for zetizeti), retrieved per turn via FTS5. Aimed at the manufacturing-centred reflexes of Indian product design — the factory's constraints, the romance of the cheap "jugaad" fix, the distance between a clever solution and a finished, dignified thing. Vocabulary is keyed to those words so the lens activates when a student reaches for them.
> **On the term *jugaad* (deliberate position).** This lens does **not** hold "jugaad" as a neutral, celebrated pole. At Prayas's explicit instruction — and on the scholarly authority of Anil K. Gupta (Honey Bee Network / SRISTI) and of Kudva & Kamath's "Against Jugaad" — the lens centres **grassroots innovation** as the dignified frame and treats "jugaad" as a flattening term to be interrogated. The corpus's two-sided rule is preserved on the *design choice* the student faces, not on the term. See `entry: grassroots-innovation-not-jugaad`, `framing_confidence`.
> **The term is revealed, never used innocently.** "Jugaad" stays *visible* — it is the word students actually reach for, so it is kept in `vocabulary` and `felt as` so they find the entry that questions it; erasing it would only hide the critique from the people who most need it. But it must never appear as a neutral descriptor: **wherever the term surfaces in entry prose, its critique surfaces with it.** The `felt as` lines are the student's *unreflective, celebratory* register on purpose — that is the innocent use the entry exists to name and challenge, not the lens's own voice.

## entry: grassroots-innovation-not-jugaad
**discipline:** manufacturing
**vocabulary:** manufacture, manufacturing, factory, jugaad, frugal, grassroots, innovation, hack, fix, workaround, improvise, make-do, local, indigenous, scale, cheap, scrap, ingenuity, shortcut, credit, appropriate
**felt as:** it's a clever jugaad; indians are so resourceful; just hack it together with whatever's around; it's a cheap fix that works; the villager already solved this; let's scale that hack up; we can productise it; people improvise here; it's frugal and ingenious; good enough for them.
**the_tension:** In Anil K. Gupta's account of *grassroots innovation* — built through the Honey Bee Network and SRISTI (*Grassroots Innovation: Minds on the Margin Are Not Marginal Minds*, 2016) — the person at the margin who solves a real problem with local knowledge is an innovator whose work carries authorship, dignity and a claim to reward, not a quaint hack to be admired and taken. The hard tension a designer meets is what happens *next*. Running that local solution through the manufacturing discipline — testing, standardising, improving, tooling it for scale — is how it reaches many people, is made safe and durable, and, where the reciprocity Gupta argues for is honoured, can bring the maker reach and reward they could not reach alone; but it is also, often, how mainstream innovation strips the innovator's authorship, sanitises away the fit-to-context that made the thing work, and turns a person's knowledge into someone else's product. (The Honey Bee Network exists precisely because that is a common outcome.) So the choice is real and costly: respect the solution as-theirs and in-context — which may mean it does not scale, is never reworked for the mainstream, and stays inconvenient to its channels — or put it through the engineering and factory discipline that scales and safeguards it, but can appropriate it and credit the engineer over the innovator. The popular celebration of *jugaad* (Radjou and colleagues, 2012) hides this choice altogether: it romances the cheap quick-fix while erasing both the innovator's authorship and the question of whether the fix is safe or finished — which is why Gupta resists the label, why Kudva and Kamath argue *against* jugaad as "neither quality design nor frugal innovation", and why others name the unsafe, corrupt, sometimes unlawful edges of the "responsible jugaad" paradox. The student's move is to ask whose innovation this is, what running it through the factory would give it *and* take from it, and whether "jugaad" is being used to honour a person's knowledge or to take it cheaply.
**failure_modes:** admiring a "jugaad" fix while taking the idea — scaling or publishing it with no credit, reward or reciprocity to the innovator (the Honey Bee ethic violated); reworking a local solution until the contextual fit that made it work is sanitised away; dismissing an improvised solution as "just jugaad" and, with it, the real knowledge of the person who made it; or the reverse — leaving a genuinely useful solution unsafe, unscaled and unshared in the name of authenticity, so it helps no one beyond its maker; or refusing, on the maker's behalf, the scale, safety or reward they might actually have wanted.
**questions_it_invites:** Whose innovation is this — and if you ran it through the factory, what would that give it and the maker, and what would it take from them? · If instead you keep it as-theirs and unscaled, who does it not reach — and are you honouring the maker's knowledge, or romanticising a shortcut on their behalf?
**sources:** Gupta, *Grassroots Innovation: Minds on the Margin Are Not Marginal Minds* (Random House India, 2016); Kudva & Kamath, "Against Jugaad: Making a Case for Design as Innovation," *Ekistics and The New Habitat* 80(2), 2021, pp. 47–57; Smith, Fressoli, Abrol, Arond & Ely, *Grassroots Innovation Movements* (Routledge, 2017). The celebratory framing this entry resists: Radjou, Prabhu & Ahuja, *Jugaad Innovation* (Jossey-Bass, 2012). The critical paradox: Ananthram & Chan, "The paradox of responsible Jugaad innovation," *Journal of Open Innovation* 11(4), 2025, art. 100672.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium-high — the grassroots-innovation framing (Gupta; minds-on-the-margin; Honey Bee reciprocity) and the *appropriation* tension are faithful to Gupta's stated position and the grassroots-innovation-movements literature (Smith et al.). **Deliberate, user-instructed exception to the no-verdict rule:** this entry carries a verdict *on the term "jugaad"* — it does not hold "jugaad" as a neutral pole — at Prayas's explicit instruction and on the scholarly authority of Gupta and of Kudva & Kamath. The two-sided rule is preserved on the *design choice* the student faces (respect-as-theirs vs run-through-the-discipline-that-appropriates); the verdict is on the discourse, not on the student's decision. Pass 3 should read this exception as intended, not as drift. ## entry: design-intent-vs-the-factory
**discipline:** manufacturing
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** manufacture, manufacturing, factory, DFM, DFMA, tooling, mould, MOQ, tolerance, cost, production, assembly, material, process, scale, mass, fabricate, machine, makeable, affordable
**felt as:** can the factory even make this; the tooling costs too much; the moq is huge; redesign it cheaper to make; the factory says no; it won't hold tolerance; just simplify it for production; design for the machine; that process can't do it; the mould won't allow it.
**the_tension:** Indian product design is, by habit and economics, manufacturing-centred: the factory's constraints — tooling cost, minimum order quantities, available processes, tolerances, assembly time — often arrive before the design is settled, and frequently decide it. Boothroyd, Dewhurst and Knight's *design for manufacture and assembly* makes the disciplined case for letting them: a product designed without regard to how it will be made is one designed to be unmakeable or unaffordable, so part count, process and assembly cost should shape the design from the first sketch. The counter, which Mears and Summers name *Manufacturing for Design*, is that a manufacturing process is not a fixed wall to design against — it can be made to evolve to reach a design's ambition rather than only clip it; sometimes the design should pull the process forward. They frame this not as replacing design-for-manufacture but as extending it — a blend in which the design is allowed to *question* the manufacturing constraints, not only obey them. Between them sits the working designer's daily question: when the factory says "no", is the design genuinely unmakeable, or merely unfamiliar to the process that happens to be on hand — and is the right move to bend the design to the machine, or to push the machine toward the design?
**failure_modes:** designing in ignorance of how a thing is made, so it arrives unmakeable or far too costly; surrendering every interesting decision to "the factory can't" until design shrinks to tweaking what the machine already does; or pushing a process to chase a design ambition whose cost or scale never justified the reach.
**questions_it_invites:** When the factory says "no", is this genuinely unmakeable, or unfamiliar to this process? · Are you bending the design to fit the machine, or asking the machine to reach the design — and what does each cost, here?
**sources:** Boothroyd, Dewhurst & Knight, *Product Design for Manufacture and Assembly* (CRC Press, 3rd ed., 2010); Mears & Summers, "Manufacturing for Design: A sustaining approach to drive manufacturing process evolution, then innovation," *Procedia Manufacturing* 48 (2020), pp. 1136–1142.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — DFM/DFMA is Boothroyd, Dewhurst & Knight's central, standard contribution. The "Manufacturing for Design" counter is now **confirmed against Mears & Summers' own abstract** (Gate 2 closed, 24 May 2026, via the NSF open-access copy): they argue product-design creativity should be a "motive force" to rethink manufacturing processes — explicitly as an **extension/complement to DFM (a blended "MFD|DFM approach"), not a replacement** — which the entry now states. Citations Pass-4 verified. Three-pass: Pass 3 clean PASS; Pass 2 flagged a mild Q1 tilt ("just / happen to have"), fixed.

## entry: mass-or-batch
**discipline:** manufacturing
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** mass, scale, production, line, volume, uniform, identical, batch, run, units, mould, tooling, economies, standard, bespoke, small-batch, fabricate, maker
**felt as:** just mass-produce it; scale the line; we need volume to bring the cost down; make them all identical; one big production run; economies of scale; can't afford small batches; bespoke is too expensive; make thousands.
**the_tension:** The reflex of manufacturing is volume: tool up once, run thousands of identical units, let economies of scale drive the unit cost down — and for a genuinely universal need that logic is sound. Piore and Sabel argued that mass production is only one historically contingent path, and that *flexible specialisation* — skilled, small-batch, regionally clustered production that can change what it makes — is a real alternative, trading some unit-cost for variety, responsiveness and the retention of craft skill (*The Second Industrial Divide*, 1984); the digital-fabrication writers (Gershenfeld's *Fab*, 2005; Anderson's *Makers*, 2012) extend the claim, arguing desktop and small-run tools now let small producers make varied things affordably. The student's question is which their thing actually wants: one identical artefact made a million times, or many close variants made well in small runs — and what committing to a high-volume tool forecloses if the design, the market or the context turns out to vary. (The counter holds, and genuinely: for a truly standard need, mass production is what makes a thing affordable and available at all, and small-batch romance can price the very people you meant to serve out of reach; scale is not the enemy.)
**questions_it_invites:** Does this want to be one identical thing made at volume, or many close variants made in small runs — and which does the need actually call for? · What does committing to a high-volume tool foreclose if the design or context later varies — and is that a risk worth taking here?
**failure_modes:** tooling up for mass volume a thing whose value lay in variation or fit, so it ships uniform and wrong for most; clinging to small-batch craft for a genuinely standard need until it is too costly to reach the people it was for.
**sources:** Piore & Sabel, *The Second Industrial Divide: Possibilities for Prosperity* (Basic Books, 1984); Gershenfeld, *Fab: The Coming Revolution on Your Desktop* (Basic Books, 2005); Anderson, *Makers: The New Industrial Revolution* (Crown Business, 2012).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — flexible specialisation (Piore & Sabel) and the digital-fabrication "new industrial revolution" (Gershenfeld, Anderson) are well-known positions, citations Perplexity-verified. Position-in-named-works (not a contested empirical claim): citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: standardise-or-fit-the-place
**discipline:** manufacturing
**vocabulary:** standard, universal, one-size, global, scale, intermediate, appropriate, local, context, low-tech, high-tech, suitable, frugal, real need, fit, place, advanced
**felt as:** make one standard version for everyone; use the latest high-tech process; scale it globally; one design fits all markets; the advanced solution is obviously better; just import the standard kit; why make it local; the modern way is best.
**the_tension:** The instinct is to reach for the most advanced, standardised process and a single universal design — newer and bigger reading as better. Schumacher's *intermediate technology* argues the opposite for many contexts: that the right technology is the one fitting the scale, skills, materials and economy of the place it serves — neither the primitive nor the most advanced, but the *appropriate* — and that imported high-tech standard solutions often fail precisely because they ignore that fit (*Small Is Beautiful*, 1973). Papanek presses the designer's version: too much design serves an imagined universal consumer rather than the real, situated needs of actual people, especially outside the rich world (*Design for the Real World*, 1971). The student's move is to ask what this place, this user, this economy can actually make, maintain and afford — and whether the standard, advanced answer fits it or merely flatters an idea of progress. (The counter holds, and genuinely: standardisation and advanced process bring real gains — interoperability, safety, quality, the steep cost-falls of a global supply chain — and "appropriate technology" can curdle into condescension, keeping people in low-tech solutions a richer market would never accept; sometimes the standard modern answer is simply the better one.)
**questions_it_invites:** What can this place and this user actually make, maintain and afford — and does the standard advanced answer fit that, or your idea of progress? · Is choosing the lower-tech "appropriate" option serving the user, or deciding on their behalf what they should settle for?
**failure_modes:** imposing a standardised, advanced solution the context cannot maintain, afford or repair, so it fails after the designer leaves; romanticising low-tech "appropriateness" until you withhold from people the quality or capability they actually wanted.
**sources:** Schumacher, *Small Is Beautiful: A Study of Economics as if People Mattered* (Blond & Briggs, 1973); Papanek, *Design for the Real World: Human Ecology and Social Change* (1971).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — intermediate/appropriate technology (Schumacher) and real-needs design (Papanek) are well-known positions, citations Perplexity-verified. Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off. (Schumacher also anchors entrepreneurship's scale-vs-craft on the *business-scale* axis; here the axis is manufacturing/material appropriateness — distinct framing.)

## entry: the-material-and-its-afterlife
**discipline:** manufacturing
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** material, cheap, glue, weld, bond, solid, sealed, integrated, composite, disposable, recycle, disassemble, repair, afterlife, waste, end-of-life, circular
**felt as:** just pick the cheapest material; glue it shut; weld it solid; make it one sealed piece; we'll worry about recycling later; nobody repairs these; bond it permanently; it's not our problem after they buy it.
**the_tension:** The makeable, cheap, clean-looking choice is often the sealed one — bond it, glue it, mould it as a single integrated piece — and for cost and strength that can be exactly right. McDonough and Braungart argue that this is where much waste is designed in: a product whose materials cannot be separated, repaired or returned to use is a product designed for the landfill, and *cradle-to-cradle* asks that materials be chosen and joined so they can have an afterlife — disassembled, repaired, recycled or safely returned (*Cradle to Cradle*, 2002); Stahel's product-life work makes the parallel economic case for designing loops rather than lines (*The Product-Life Factor*, 1982). The student's question is what happens to this thing when it breaks or its life ends — whether the way it is made lets it be repaired or recovered, or quietly commits it to waste — and whether that afterlife is the designer's responsibility here. (The counter holds, and genuinely: sealed, bonded, integrated construction is sometimes cheaper, stronger, safer and lighter — design-for-disassembly can add cost, bulk and failure points for a recovery that, for some products in some places, never actually happens; the afterlife only counts if the system to use it exists.)
**questions_it_invites:** When this breaks or its life ends, can the way it's made let it be repaired or recovered — or does its construction commit it to waste? · Is designing for that afterlife the right call here, or are you adding cost for a recovery system that doesn't exist for this product?
**failure_modes:** sealing a thing shut for cost or looks so it cannot be repaired or recovered, designing in the waste; engineering for a disassembly and recycling the surrounding system will never actually perform, paying in cost and bulk for an afterlife that never comes.
**sources:** McDonough & Braungart, *Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things* (North Point Press, 2002); Stahel, "The Product-Life Factor" (1982) and *The Performance Economy* (Palgrave Macmillan, 2010).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — cradle-to-cradle (McDonough & Braungart) and product-life/circular economy (Stahel) are well-known positions, citations Perplexity-verified. The empirics on whether life-extension and design-for-repair reduce waste are independently Consensus-backed under slow-design's the-new-model-or-the-lasting-thing [NM-Ba, NM-Pr, NM-Ha]. Position-in-named-works otherwise: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: prototype-or-product
**discipline:** manufacturing
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** prototype, works, mockup, proof, concept, mvp, demo, ship, good enough, one-off, repeatable, reliable, safe, production, scale, robust, finished
**felt as:** it works as a prototype, ship it; the demo runs fine; it's good enough; proof of concept is done; just productise it; we made one and it worked; scale it up; why isn't this ready.
**the_tension:** A working prototype is a powerful thing — it proves the idea — and the temptation is to treat "it works once, here, in my hands" as "it is a product". Ulrich and Eppinger's account of product development names the long discipline that lies between: the move from a one-off that works for its maker to a thing that can be made repeatedly, holds up across many users and conditions, meets safety and reliability standards, and can be produced at cost is a distinct body of work, not a formality (*Product Design and Development*). The student's question is what separates this prototype from a product — what fails on the hundredth unit, in another's hands, in the rain, at scale — and whether that gap is being respected or wished away. (The counter holds, and genuinely: over-engineering for a robustness the thing will never meet is its own waste, and endless production-hardening can kill a product that needed to ship and learn; sometimes "good enough to put in front of people" is the disciplined call, and the prototype *is* close enough.)
**questions_it_invites:** What separates this prototype from a product — what would fail on the hundredth unit, in someone else's hands, at scale? · Is the gap between "it works here" and "it's a product" being respected, or wished away — and is closing it worth the cost for this thing?
**failure_modes:** shipping a prototype as a product, so what worked once fails in the field, unsafe or unrepeatable; over-hardening for production a thing that needed to ship early and learn, until the effort outruns what the product warranted.
**sources:** Ulrich & Eppinger, *Product Design and Development* (McGraw-Hill, 1st ed. 1995; later editions).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Ulrich & Eppinger's product-development process (concept → manufacturable, production-ready design) is the standard textbook account, Perplexity-verified. Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off. (Relates to grassroots-innovation-not-jugaad's "finished, dignified thing" but is the general prototype→production discipline, not the appropriation question.)

## entry: mechanise-or-keep-the-hand
**discipline:** manufacturing
**register:** making
**vocabulary:** machine, mechanise, automate, factory, industrialise, labour, jobs, hand, handmade, craft, artisan, village, decentralise, charkha, khadi, livelihood, displace, scale
**felt as:** mechanise it to cut labour costs; replace the handwork with a machine; industrialise the process; the factory is more efficient; handmade is too slow; automate the artisans out; scale it up in a plant; machines are progress.
**the_tension:** When handwork is slow or costly, mechanising it looks like obvious progress — the machine is faster, cheaper, more uniform. Gandhi's argument in *Hind Swaraj* (1909) was not against machines as such, but against machinery that centralises production, concentrates capital and displaces the labour of the many — he set against it a vision of decentralised village production, hand-spinning and the charkha as a way to keep livelihood, dignity and self-reliance in people's own hands. That position met its enduring counter in the Nehruvian case that large-scale industry was what could lift a poor nation out of poverty at speed — a tension that still runs through Indian making. The student's move is to ask what mechanising this would give and to whom: the cost, scale and consistency a machine brings, against the livelihoods, skills and decentralised making it may displace — and whether the people whose work it replaces are made better off, or merely cheaper to do without. (The counter holds, and genuinely: mechanisation has lifted enormous numbers out of grinding, precarious manual labour, and insisting on the hand can romanticise drudgery or trap artisans in poverty a machine would relieve; keeping the handwork is not automatically the humane choice.)
**questions_it_invites:** What would mechanising this give, and to whom — and who does the machine displace, made better off or merely done without? · Is keeping the handwork honouring a livelihood and a skill, or romanticising drudgery the maker would gladly be freed from?
**failure_modes:** mechanising to cut cost while displacing the artisans whose livelihood and knowledge the work carried, and calling it progress; insisting on handwork out of romance, trapping makers in slow, precarious labour a machine would have relieved.
**sources:** Gandhi, *Hind Swaraj or Indian Home Rule* (1909); and the Gandhi–Nehru debate on decentralised craft production versus centralised heavy industry in Indian development.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium-high — Gandhi's *Hind Swaraj* critique of centralising machinery (carefully: anti-mass-industrial, *not* anti-technology — he allowed socially useful machines) and the charkha/khadi philosophy, plus the enduring Gandhi–Nehru industrialisation debate, are well-documented positions (Perplexity-verified). Position-in-named-works: citation-verified, framing PENDING three-pass + Prayas's sign-off. (Distinct from slow-design's automate-or-by-hand, which is the individual designer's craft-skill; this is the political economy of decentralised vs centralised production.)
