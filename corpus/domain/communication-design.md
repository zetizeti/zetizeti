## entry: clarity-vs-decoration
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** clear, clarity, chart, graph, data, decorate, ornament, infographic, visual, legible, declutter, chartjunk, simple, striking, pop
**felt as:** make the chart look nicer; add visuals to liven it up; it's too plain; nobody reads a boring graph; make it pop; should i simplify or make it striking; it needs more colour and icons.
**the_tension:** Edward Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 1983) argues that good information graphics maximise the *data-ink* — the marks that carry actual information — and erase the rest, treating decoration, heavy gridlines and "chartjunk" as noise that competes with meaning. On this reading clarity is an ethics: the graphic's job is to show the data truthfully and efficiently, and ornament usually distracts or lies. The counter is real: an austere, maximally-efficient graphic can be cold, unmemorable, or simply unread, and in much of communication design — campaigns, editorial, the everyday glance — mood, memorability and engagement are part of the message, not noise. The student's move is to ask what this artefact is *for* — to inform precisely, or to move and be remembered — and whether the decoration they are reaching for serves the meaning or buries it.
**failure_modes:** drowning the data in decoration until the message is harder to read, not easier; stripping a piece so bare it is accurate and ignored; treating "make it pop" as a goal rather than asking what the reader needs to see.
**questions_it_invites:** Which marks here carry the data, and which carry mood or memorability — and which does this artefact need more of? · Is this made to be understood precisely, or to be felt and remembered — and is your decoration serving that?
**sources:** Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (Graphics Press, 1983).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the data-ink / chartjunk argument is the central, well-known thesis of the cited work. Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers; both poles empirically supported — decluttering aids comprehension, yet embellished charts are recalled significantly better — see verification-consensus-backing.md).

## entry: legibility-vs-readability
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** legible, legibility, readable, readability, font, typeface, serif, sans-serif, sans serif, type, reading, size, spacing, body text, set, leading
**felt as:** which font is easiest to read; is this too small to read; should i use a serif or a sans serif; it's hard to read in a big block; people won't get through this much text; what's the most readable font; it looks fine but does it read.
**the_tension:** Miles Tinker (*Legibility of Print*, 1963) treated legibility as something measurable — the speed and accuracy with which letters and words can be discriminated — and a long experimental tradition has chased the "most legible" type. But Sofie Beier (*Reading Letters: Designing for Legibility*, 2012) and others hold a line that students routinely collapse: *legibility* is whether a letter can be told apart at all, while *readability* is whether a long passage can be read with ease and stamina — and the two do not always pull the same way. A face can be highly legible letter-by-letter yet tiring to read at length; a "less legible" face can carry a book comfortably. The evidence sharpens rather than settles this: font choice measurably changes reading speed and the best font differs from reader to reader, yet serif and sans-serif faces are largely interchangeable for speed once size is equalised, and comprehension is mostly unaffected by the choice. So the student's question is not "which font is the most legible?" but "legible or readable, for whom, at what length — and is the thing I am optimising the thing this reader actually needs?"
**failure_modes:** chasing maximum letter-legibility for a long passage that needed reading stamina instead; assuming one "most readable" font exists for everyone; equalising nothing — comparing faces at different sizes and crediting the difference to the typeface.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this set to be told apart letter-by-letter, or to be read for pages — and which are you optimising? · Legible or readable for whom, and how would you know?
**sources:** Tinker, *Legibility of Print* (Iowa State University Press, 1963); Beier, *Reading Letters: Designing for Legibility* (BIS Publishers, 2012); Lupton, *Thinking with Type* (Princeton Architectural Press, 2004).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the legibility/readability distinction is foundational and correctly attributed (citations Perplexity-verified). Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers): font choice significantly affects reading *speed* and individual fit, yet serif vs sans is largely interchangeable when x-height is equalised and comprehension is typically unaffected — the tension is preserved and deepened (the "rules" of legibility are weaker and more individual than dogma claims).

## entry: the-crystal-goblet
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** invisible, transparent, neutral, crystal, goblet, expression, expressive, style, voice, restraint, self-effacing, decoration, classical, experimental
**felt as:** should the design just get out of the way; am i overdesigning this; it should be neutral and let the content speak; i want it to have a strong style; is my voice getting in the way of the message; should typography be felt or not.
**the_tension:** Beatrice Warde's "The Crystal Goblet" (a 1930 address, collected 1955) argues that typography should efface itself so the reader receives the content directly — noticing the meaning, not the means; on this view the designer's craft is proven precisely by going unremarked. (Her own figure for this is the crystal goblet; the question stays clean of it — the image belongs behind the curtain.) The counter-tradition is just as serious. David Carson's work (*The End of Print*, with Lewis Blackwell, 1995) and the wider postmodern turn (Rick Poynor, *No More Rules*, 2003) insist that the form is part of the meaning — that type has a voice, that expression and even difficulty can be the message rather than noise over it. The student's move is to ask what this particular piece asks of its form: to step back so the words arrive untouched, or to speak — and whether the expressiveness they are reaching for serves the meaning or merely performs the designer.
**failure_modes:** treating self-effacement as the only virtue and producing work that is correct and mute; treating expression as automatically deep, so the form shouts over a message that needed to simply arrive; confusing "I can feel the design" with "the design is good".
**questions_it_invites:** Does this piece need its form to disappear, or to speak — and which are you giving it? · When you make it expressive, is the expression carrying the meaning or covering it?
**sources:** Warde, "The Crystal Goblet, or Printing Should Be Invisible" (1930; collected in *The Crystal Goblet: Sixteen Essays on Typography*, Sylvan Press, 1955); Carson & Blackwell, *The End of Print* (Laurence King, 1995); Poynor, *No More Rules: Graphic Design and Postmodernism* (Yale University Press, 2003).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the transparency/expression debate and its two named poles (Warde vs the postmodern turn) are well-established and correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified). A theory-of-design tension, not an empirical claim.

## entry: the-typeface-has-a-voice
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** typeface, font, voice, tone, personality, connotation, feel, character, mood, appropriate, formal, playful, friendly, serious, choose, pick
**felt as:** this font feels too formal; it needs a friendlier font; the typeface gives the wrong vibe; does this font suit a wedding / a bank / a punk gig; why does this look cheap; the words are right but it feels off; what does this font say about us.
**the_tension:** A typeface is not a neutral container. Empirical work (A. Dawn Shaikh, Barbara Chaparro and colleagues, from 2006; popularised by Sarah Hyndman, *Why Fonts Matter*, 2016) finds that readers attribute fairly consistent "personalities" to typefaces and read a text partly through the tone its setting implies — a typeface incongruent with the words slows and colours interpretation. This is power: the designer can set a register before a single word is read. But it is also a trap. The "voice" is not universal — it weakens in some languages and writing systems, varies with reader and context, and in some studies experienced designers' intuition serves about as well as the data. The student's question, then, is not "what is the right font?" but "what is this typeface saying underneath the words, to *these* readers — and is that a register I chose, or one that leaked in?"
**failure_modes:** choosing type purely on personal taste while a clashing tone quietly undercuts the message; treating font-personality charts as universal law across cultures and scripts; reaching for a "characterful" face whose voice fights the content.
**questions_it_invites:** Before a word is read, what is this typeface already saying — and did you choose that, or inherit it? · Whose ear are you tuning this voice for, and would it sound the same to a different reader?
**sources:** Shaikh, Chaparro & Fox, "Perception of Fonts: Perceived Personality Traits and Uses" (2006); Hyndman, *Why Fonts Matter* (Virgin Books, 2016).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — citations verified real and correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified). Consensus-checked (20 peer-reviewed papers): readers reliably attribute personality to typefaces and congruence affects processing and appropriateness judgements; counter preserved — the effect is weaker in a genderless language, varies by context, and intuition is sometimes sufficient.

## entry: type-as-image-vs-text
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** type, typography, image, letterform, read, see, shape, visual, experimental, abstract, word, picture, material, surface, illegible
**felt as:** when does a letter stop being readable; i'm treating the type like a picture; is this still meant to be read; the words have become a shape; it looks amazing but you can't read it; should this be read or just seen.
**the_tension:** Johanna Drucker (*The Visible Word*, 1994) studies the moment in early-twentieth-century avant-garde work when typography stopped behaving purely as transparent notation and became visual, material — a thing to be *seen* as much as read. This opens a real spectrum: at one end the letter is a sign that disappears into meaning; at the other it is an image, an object, a texture on the surface. Much of the strongest graphic work lives in the tension — type that is read *and* seen. The risk is sliding to either pole without noticing: text so purely functional it forfeits the visual life of the page, or composition so image-like that the reading it promised never happens. The student's move is to locate where on that spectrum a given piece is actually working, and whether that position is a decision or an accident.
**failure_modes:** pushing type toward image until the thing that needed reading cannot be read; treating every letter as mere notation and ignoring the visual life it carries; not noticing which mode a piece is in, so it half-promises reading and half-delivers picture.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this meant to be read, seen, or both — and where exactly on that line is it sitting? · At what point does your letterform stop being a word and become an image, and did you intend to cross it?
**sources:** Drucker, *The Visible Word: Experimental Typography and Modern Art, 1909–1923* (University of Chicago Press, 1994).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Drucker's study of type's visual/material dimension is correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified). A theory-of-design tension, not an empirical claim.

## entry: the-rule-and-the-recantation
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** rules, typography, new typography, modernist, asymmetric, sans-serif, classical, symmetrical, dogma, grid, convention, manifesto, break the rules
**felt as:** am i allowed to break this rule; my tutor says always do it this way; should i follow the typographic rules or not; this feels too rigid; when is a rule worth keeping; who decided these rules anyway.
**the_tension:** Jan Tschichold wrote *Die neue Typographie* (1928) as a near-manifesto for a modern typography — asymmetric, sans-serif, rule-governed, sweeping away the ornamental past. Then, after the war, he renounced much of it, returning to classical, symmetrical book typography and going so far as to call the earlier dogmatism authoritarian. The same brilliant practitioner authored both the rules and their repudiation. This is the cleanest case design offers of a hard question: are typographic rules discovered truths, or historical conventions dressed as truths — and what is the difference between mastering a discipline's rules and being mastered by them? The student caught between "always do it this way" and "break all the rules" is usually missing the real move: understanding *why* a rule exists well enough to know when it is load-bearing and when it is merely inherited.
**failure_modes:** following typographic rules as dogma without asking what they are for; breaking rules for novelty before understanding what they were holding up; mistaking a historical convention for a timeless law (or the reverse).
**questions_it_invites:** What is this rule actually holding up — and what happens to the work if you remove it? · Are you keeping this convention because it serves the piece, or because someone told you to?
**sources:** Tschichold, *Die neue Typographie* (Berlin, 1928; English trans. Ruari McLean, *The New Typography*, University of California Press, 1995).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the New Typography and Tschichold's later repudiation are well-documented and correctly attributed (Perplexity- and citation-verified). The "authoritarian" charge is grounded in Tschichold's *own* post-war statements (he likened the New Typography's intolerance to the authoritarian temperament of the period); the prose says he "renounced much of it" — a partial return to classical book typography, not a wholesale disowning — so the strong language and that partial scope agree. A theory/history tension.

## entry: latin-centric-type
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** script, latin, non-latin, devanagari, arabic, multilingual, multiscript, writing system, type design, font, language, localise, bilingual
**felt as:** the font doesn't have my language; the hindi looks like an afterthought next to the english; why do non-english scripts look worse; the type rules don't fit my script; how do i set two scripts together; the original was designed for english.
**the_tension:** Fiona Ross, who has spent a career designing non-Latin type and studying its history, shows how much of mainstream type-making — its tools, proportions, the very idea of what a "well-designed" letter is — was shaped by and for the Latin alphabet, then exported to other scripts as if neutral (*Zurich Type Design*, 2020; *Non-Latin Scripts: From Metal to Digital Type*, 2012). The result a student meets daily: a typeface where the English is considered and the Devanagari or Arabic is a thin afterthought, a script bent to fit conventions that were never its own. But the tension does not run all one way. Some typographic principles — clear differentiation between letters, rhythm, modulation of contrast — do carry across scripts and are not merely Latin habits in disguise; and "treat each script on its own terms" can itself harden into a purism that refuses any shared craft, or that makes setting two scripts harmoniously together impossible. The student's move is to ask which of the "universal" rules they were taught are one writing system's conventions wearing a neutral mask, and which genuinely cross scripts — and whether "on its own terms" is sharpening the work or becoming a new dogma of its own.
**failure_modes:** treating Latin typographic conventions as universal and bending other scripts to fit them; setting a second script as a sized-down clone of the Latin rather than on its own terms; over-correcting into a purism that denies any shared cross-script craft, or that cannot make two scripts sit together; assuming "neutral" type has no cultural default built in.
**questions_it_invites:** Which of these "universal" rules are one writing system's conventions in disguise, and which genuinely carry across scripts? · When you set this script "on its own terms," is that sharpening the work — or hardening into a rule of its own?
**sources:** Ross, "Non-Latin type design — some considerations," in *Zurich Type Design*, ed. Rudolf Barmettler (Triest Verlag, 2020); Ross, Shaw & Luna, *Non-Latin Scripts: From Metal to Digital Type* (St Bride Library, 2012).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Ross is a recognised authority on non-Latin type and the Latin-centric legacy of type technology. **Citations corrected at Pass 4** (live lookup): the 2020 chapter sits in *Zurich Type Design* (ed. Barmettler, Triest Verlag), not a book of its own title, and the 2012 book is Ross, Shaw & **Luna** (St Bride Library) — an earlier draft, following a mistaken Perplexity answer, gave the wrong container title and named Berry (who wrote only the afterword) as third author. Two-sidedness strengthened after Pass 2/3 flagged a one-directional framing. A theory/history tension; kept distinct from `pluriversal-and-decolonial-design` in `critical-counterculture-design.md` by being specifically about scripts and writing systems.

## entry: the-grid-order-vs-intuition
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** grid, layout, column, module, structure, align, system, baseline, margin, compose, order, intuition, freeform, break the grid
**felt as:** should i use a grid or just place things where they feel right; the grid feels restrictive; my layout looks messy without structure; everything's aligned but it's boring; when can i break the grid; do i even need a grid for this.
**the_tension:** Josef Müller-Brockmann (*Grid Systems in Graphic Design*, 1981) presents the grid almost as an ethic — a rational, modular structure that brings order, clarity and a kind of impersonal objectivity to the page, subordinating the designer's whim to a system the reader can trust. The grid genuinely does this: it coordinates complexity, speeds reading, makes large bodies of material navigable. But the same virtue is its limit. A grid can produce work that is orderly and lifeless, and some of the most alive composition comes from intuition, tension, the deliberately broken module. The student oscillating between "I must use a grid" and "the grid is killing it" is usually skipping the real question: what is the grid *for* in this piece — coordination of genuine complexity, or a security blanket — and does breaking it here release energy or just create noise?
**failure_modes:** imposing a grid where there is nothing complex to coordinate, producing dead order; abandoning structure and calling the resulting mess "intuition"; breaking the grid for effect without first establishing the order the break depends on.
**questions_it_invites:** What is the grid coordinating here — real complexity, or your nerves? · When you break it, does the break release something, or is it just untidy?
**sources:** Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (Niggli, 1981).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the grid-as-system argument is the central thesis of the cited work and correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified; 1981 first edition under that title). A theory-of-design tension.

## entry: gestalt-grouping-vs-disruption
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** gestalt, grouping, proximity, similarity, perception, eye, organise, cluster, whole, pattern, figure, ground, disrupt, expectation
**felt as:** things that go together don't look like they go together; the eye doesn't know where to look; it reads as one blob; how do i make these feel like a group; i want to break the pattern on purpose; why does this look related when it isn't.
**the_tension:** Gestalt psychology (Max Wertheimer's 1923 work on the laws of perceptual organisation; later brought into visual art by Rudolf Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception*, 1954) describes how the eye automatically groups what is near, similar, enclosed or continuous — we perceive organised wholes, not loose marks. For the designer this is a quiet power: proximity and similarity *will* group elements whether you intend it or not, so layout is partly the management of perceptions the viewer cannot help having. But two moves live here, not one. You can work *with* grouping to make relationships instantly legible — or *against* it, disrupting an expected pattern to create emphasis, surprise, friction. The student's question is whether a given grouping in their work is one they designed or one the eye imposed by accident, and whether a disruption earns its break from the pattern or merely fights the reader's perception.
**failure_modes:** placing unrelated things close together so the eye groups them wrongly; relying on grouping you never checked, so the accidental reading wins; disrupting a pattern before establishing it, so there is nothing for the break to play against.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this grouping one you intended, or one the eye made for you? · When you break the pattern, what does the break buy — emphasis, or confusion?
**sources:** Wertheimer, "Untersuchungen zur Lehre von der Gestalt II" / "Laws of Organization in Perceptual Forms" (*Psychologische Forschung*, 1923); Arnheim, *Art and Visual Perception: A Psychology of the Creative Eye* (University of California Press, 1954).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Gestalt grouping is a well-established body of perceptual psychology, correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified). The grouping principles themselves are not in dispute; the *tension* (work with grouping vs disrupt it) is a design-craft tension framed for questioning.

## entry: emptiness-as-content
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** white space, whitespace, empty, emptiness, negative space, blank, sparse, ma, minimal, breathing room, fill, density, void, restraint
**felt as:** there's too much empty space; should i fill this gap; it looks unfinished and bare; the client wants me to use the whole page; is white space wasteful; it just feels empty.
**the_tension:** Kenya Hara (*Designing Design*, 2007) treats emptiness not as the absence of content but as a kind of content — drawing on the Japanese sense of *ma*, the charged interval, the void that holds potential and invites the viewer to bring meaning to it. On this view white space is active: it gives weight, directs attention, lets a thing breathe and be received. The everyday pressure runs the other way — empty space reads to many clients and beginners as unfinished, wasteful, money not spent, so the impulse is to fill it. The student is rarely choosing between "good design" and "bad design" here; they are choosing between two readings of emptiness — as a void to be eliminated, or as a presence doing work. The move is to ask what the empty space in their piece is actually *doing*, and whether filling it would add meaning or only add stuff.
**failure_modes:** filling space to look "finished" and burying the thing that mattered; leaving emptiness that is merely empty, not charged, and calling it restraint; treating white space as decoration rather than as something with a job.
**questions_it_invites:** What is this empty space doing — holding attention, or just unfilled? · If you filled it, would you be adding meaning, or only adding stuff?
**sources:** Hara, *Designing Design* (Lars Müller Publishers, 2007).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Hara's treatment of emptiness / *ma* as active content is correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified). A theory-of-design tension.

## entry: less-is-more-vs-less-is-a-bore
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** minimal, minimalism, less, simple, reduce, strip, clean, maximal, rich, complex, ornament, bore, plain, busy
**felt as:** should i strip this back or add more; minimalism feels safe but boring; is it too plain; the client wants it richer; everyone's design looks the same and minimal; when is simple just empty.
**the_tension:** Two slogans frame a long argument. "Less is more," associated with Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, makes reduction a virtue — strip to the essential and the result gains clarity, calm, force. "Less is a bore," Robert Venturi's rejoinder (linked to *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture*, 1966), answers that the world is messy and plural, that complexity, contradiction and even ornament carry meaning a purified design throws away. Graphic design inherited both as live options: the disciplined minimal system, and the rich, layered, vernacular-embracing maximal. The student treating minimalism as automatically tasteful (or maximalism as automatically rich) has skipped the question. Reduction can clarify or it can impoverish; complexity can mean or it can clutter. The move is to ask what this particular message gains and loses under each, rather than defaulting to the safer-looking pole.
**failure_modes:** reducing until the work is clean and says nothing; piling on richness until meaning drowns in busyness; choosing minimalism because it photographs well and reads as "tasteful," not because the message needs it.
**questions_it_invites:** What does this message gain — and lose — if you strip it back? · Is your minimalism essential, or just the safe-looking choice?
**sources:** Venturi, *Complexity and Contradiction in Architecture* (Museum of Modern Art, 1966); the aphorism "less is more" is associated with Ludwig Mies van der Rohe.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium-high — the minimalism/complexity debate and its two aphorisms are correctly attributed, with the documented nuance that "less is a bore" circulates as a tag for Venturi's position rather than as a literal phrase printed in the 1966 book (Perplexity-verified). A theory-of-design tension.

## entry: denotation-and-connotation
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** image, meaning, denotation, connotation, sign, semiotics, message, read, photograph, advertisement, symbol, literal, association, culture
**felt as:** what does this image actually say; the picture means more than what it shows; people read it differently than i meant; how do i control what an image implies; it's just a photo of a product but it feels luxurious; the image carries a hidden message.
**the_tension:** Roland Barthes, in "Rhetoric of the Image" (1964; English in *Image-Music-Text*, 1977), takes apart an advertisement to show that an image carries at least two layers of meaning at once: *denotation*, what it literally depicts, and *connotation*, the freight of cultural association it triggers — Italianness, freshness, luxury — which feels natural but is learned, and ideological. For the designer this is the whole game and its hazard. You compose denotation (you choose what is shown), but connotation is completed in the viewer, out of a culture you do not fully control. The student who believes an image "just shows the product" has missed the second layer entirely; the student who thinks they can dictate connotation has overestimated their control. The move is to ask what a given image is connoting beneath what it depicts, to whom, and how much of that the designer actually authored.
**failure_modes:** treating an image as a neutral depiction and ignoring what it connotes; assuming the intended connotation is the one the viewer will receive; loading an image with associations that read as manipulative once noticed.
**questions_it_invites:** Beneath what this image literally shows, what is it connoting — and to whom? · How much of that second meaning did you author, and how much did the culture?
**sources:** Barthes, "Rhetoric of the Image," in *Image-Music-Text* (trans. Stephen Heath, Fontana, 1977; originally "Rhétorique de l'image," *Communications* 4, 1964).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the denotation/connotation analysis is the central argument of the cited essay and correctly attributed (Perplexity-verified). A theory-of-meaning tension, not an empirical claim.

## entry: the-photograph-evidence-or-construction
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** photograph, photo, image, real, evidence, document, truth, objective, construct, frame, manipulate, authentic, capture, staged
**felt as:** a photo doesn't lie; is this image honest; it really happened so it must be true; the photo feels staged; am i manipulating reality by framing it this way; can i trust this picture; it's documentary so it's objective.
**the_tension:** A photograph carries an unusual authority — it seems to certify that *this was really there*. Susan Sontag (*On Photography*, 1977) and Roland Barthes (*Camera Lucida*, 1980) both sit on the knife-edge of this: the photograph does bear a trace of the real, and yet every photograph is also a construction — framed, timed, selected, lit, captioned — an interpretation wearing the costume of evidence. For a communication designer who reaches for photography precisely because it reads as true, this is the live problem. The image's persuasive power comes from its claimed objectivity, but that objectivity is partly an effect the designer is producing. The student's move is to ask what a given photograph is being asked to certify, how much of its "truth" is the trace and how much the framing, and whether using that authority here is honest or a borrowed costume.
**failure_modes:** trusting a photograph as objective evidence because it is a photograph; using documentary authority to lend false credibility to a constructed claim; ignoring how framing, selection and caption author the "truth" the image seems to merely record.
**questions_it_invites:** What is this photograph being asked to prove, and how much of its truth is the trace versus the framing? · Are you using the image's air of objectivity honestly, or borrowing it?
**sources:** Sontag, *On Photography* (Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 1977); Barthes, *Camera Lucida: Reflections on Photography* (trans. Richard Howard, Hill and Wang, 1981; originally *La Chambre claire*, Seuil, 1980).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the evidence/construction tension in photography is correctly attributed to the cited works (Perplexity-verified). A theory tension, not an empirical claim.

## entry: the-designer-as-author
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** author, authorship, designer, content, form, voice, neutral, service, commission, originate, brief, message, mine, client
**felt as:** am i just making someone else's message look good; is this my work or the client's; should designers have something to say; i only styled it, i didn't write it; can a designer be an author; whose voice is this.
**the_tension:** Michael Rock's essay "The Designer as Author" (*Eye*, 1996) presses a question designers tend to avoid: is the designer a neutral form-giver who makes someone else's content legible and attractive, or an author — someone with content, a position, a voice of their own? The romance of authorship is attractive; it promises the designer is more than a stylist for hire. But Rock complicates the very idea, noting how slippery "author" becomes when the designer's medium is other people's messages. For a student this is identity-shaping: it decides whether you understand your work as service (make the brief sing) or as authorship (have something to say through the work), and the two carry different responsibilities and different freedoms. ## entry: graphical-integrity-the-lie-factor
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** chart, graph, data, axis, scale, truncate, distort, mislead, exaggerate, honest, integrity, proportion, statistics, accurate
**felt as:** should the axis start at zero; this chart makes the difference look bigger; is it cheating to crop the scale; i want the trend to look dramatic; the data's boring, can i make it pop; is this graph honest.
**the_tension:** Edward Tufte (*The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, 1983) names *graphical integrity* as an ethic and offers the "lie factor" — the ratio between the size of the effect shown in a graphic and the size of the effect in the data. A truncated axis, an area scaled to a length, a distorted proportion: these make a difference *look* bigger than it is, and Tufte argues the honest graphic keeps that ratio close to one. The student designing a chart to "make the trend pop" is usually one decision away from a lie. But the tension is real, not a simple rule: emphasis is a legitimate part of communication, the evidence shows distortions such as axis-truncation persistently mislead even sophisticated viewers, *and yet* context often matters more than the axis, and some "distorted" framings genuinely serve a task. The move is to ask whether a given choice is clarifying a real signal for the reader or manufacturing one — and to be honest about which.
**failure_modes:** truncating or rescaling to manufacture a difference that is not in the data; assuming any emphasis is dishonest and refusing all design judgement; trusting that a "clean" chart is automatically truthful when the framing still misleads.
**questions_it_invites:** Is this choice making a real signal easier to see, or making a small effect look like a big one? · If a careful reader noticed what you did to this scale, would they feel informed or tricked?
**sources:** Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (Graphics Press, 1983).

## entry: transmission-or-meaning-made
**discipline:** communication-design
**vocabulary:** message, communicate, sender, receiver, transmit, signal, noise, encode, decode, audience, meaning, interpret, channel, clear
**felt as:** i sent the message clearly so why don't they get it; the audience read it completely differently; communication is just getting the message across, right; how do i make sure they receive what i meant; they decoded it wrong; the meaning is in what i sent.
**the_tension:** Two models of communication sit side by side, and a designer often works inside one without noticing the other. Claude Shannon and Warren Weaver's *The Mathematical Theory of Communication* (1949) gives the *transmission* picture: a sender encodes a message, sends it down a channel against noise, a receiver decodes it — communication as accurate delivery. It is powerful and precise, and it shaped how we talk about "getting the message across." Stuart Hall's "Encoding/Decoding" (1980) describes a different motion: the receiver is not a passive endpoint but *makes* meaning out of their own context and codes, and may take a message in the preferred way, negotiate it, or oppose it. The two are not really rivals at the same task — one models the fidelity of a signal, the other the production of meaning — but a designer who only ever thinks in one will be caught out by the other. The student's move is to ask which model a given problem actually belongs to: is the trouble that the signal isn't getting through cleanly, or that the audience is constructing a meaning you did not intend — and what does each diagnosis ask you to do differently?
**failure_modes:** assuming a clearly-sent message arrives intact, so a misreading must be the audience's fault; ignoring the codes and context the audience decodes through; treating every reading as equally valid and so abandoning all responsibility for clarity; confusing a signal problem (it didn't get through) with a meaning problem (it got through and was read otherwise).
**questions_it_invites:** Is the difficulty here that the signal isn't arriving cleanly, or that the audience is making a meaning you didn't intend? · Which of the two models does this problem belong to — and what does that ask you to change?
**sources:** Shannon & Weaver, *The Mathematical Theory of Communication* (University of Illinois Press, 1949); Hall, "Encoding/Decoding," in *Culture, Media, Language* (Hutchinson, 1980; based on the 1973 CCCS stencilled paper "Encoding and Decoding in the Television Discourse").
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both models are correctly attributed (Perplexity- and citation-verified, incl. Hall's 1973/1980 dating). **Re-balanced after Pass 2/3** flagged a verdict-smuggling tilt ("the weaker one") and a prescriptive close — the two models now stand as answering *different* questions, with the resolution left to the learner. The student's move is to ask, for a given colour, whether it has been judged in the context it will actually live in — and also where a fixed, named value is exactly what the work requires.
