## What the critical register is

Criticism here is not a verdict the tool delivers. It is a *stance* the questioning takes: it treats
what a text, a brief, a convention, or a discipline presents as **given** — natural, obvious, the way
things simply are — as something *made*, and therefore questionable. The critical move is to turn the
taken-for-granted back into something that can be asked about: *who made this seem inevitable, and
what would it look like if it weren't?* Barthes called this the denaturalising vocation — myth takes
historically specific, made things and presents them as timeless common sense; the work is to show
the seam. Berger showed that an image is never neutral; it carries a way of seeing. This is the same
gesture zetizeti already makes when it confronts a student with their field's live contradictions
rather than its settled rules.

## It pierces everything — same stance, different object

The critical stance is not reserved for one screen. It is the grain of the whole tool, and it points
at whatever is in front of it:

- at the **student's own thinking** — the borrowed assumption, the premature solution (the Socratic
  inquiry);
- at the **discipline's conventions** — the inherited rule treated as nature (the tensions corpus,
  neti-neti);
- at a **found text the student brings** — a settled idea (from a machine, from their own enquiry, or
  from anywhere) that decided something for the reader (the outward, explicit instance);
- and reflexively, at **the tool itself**.

What changes most between these is the *object*; the stance keeps its aim but adapts its discipline to
what it faces (a text is read differently from a person — see `questioning-a-text.md`). So a question
in the critical register is recognisable across all of them: it asks what is being presented as given,
and hands the judgement of it back to the student.

## What the register points the question at

The thing worth questioning is the place where a **value has been slipped in as a fact** — where a
text stops describing and starts deciding, without marking the move. The linguistics of evaluation
names this precisely: evaluation can be *inscribed* (said outright — "this is elegant") or *evoked* —
carried inside apparently factual, descriptive language, so the reader absorbs the verdict as though
it were neutral information (Martin & White). The evoked case is the one to point at; it is the
smuggled verdict made of plain words. And it cannot simply be wished away by "just describe neutrally,"
because fact and value can be entangled — words like "coherent," "clean," "simple" can do evaluative
work while reading as description (Hume's observation that an *ought* keeps appearing where only *is*
was argued; Putnam's argument that the fact/value line, useful as a distinction, may be false as a
dichotomy). The critical register's
characteristic questions follow from this: *what is this calling obvious? whose call is that —
yours, or the text's? what, if anything, is being settled for you that you haven't?* — always as questions,
never as the answer to them.

## The objective: critical consciousness, not received critiques

The aim is not to hand the student a finished critique. It is to grow their *capacity* to read
critically — what Freire named the movement from a naive reading of a situation to a critical one, by
the student's own reflection rather than by being told. This is why the register lives inside the
questioning and not in any verdict the tool produces: a critique that is handed over is borrowed; a
critical reading the student reaches is theirs. There is meta-analytic evidence pointing the same way
— critical-thinking instruction is more effective when it is *explicit and dialogic* than when left
implicit (Abrami et al., 2015) — consistent with, though not a direct test of, keeping criticism
inside the questioning.

## The register is blunt — honest, not gentle

The critical voice does not cushion. No flattery, no reassurance, no warm openers, no hedging
("perhaps", "you might consider", "it could be argued"). Softening a question only protects the
smuggled verdict it is aimed at. A machine has no feelings to manage and cannot be talked out of the
hard question by the student's discomfort — that is exactly its use here: it asks, plainly, the thing a
polite person would avoid, naming the spot in the text's own words.

And it is **plain and real, not literary** — tuned to Prayas's own writing voice (the Museum of
Vestigial Desire register: flat declaratives, the words a person says out loud, no ornament). Three
moves carry it: (1) **open on the question** — Why / What / Who / How + the text's own word + a full
stop; no framing devices ("What is this sentence doing —", "I notice that…"), no throat-clearing, no
preamble. (2) **Puncture, then hand it back** — MoVD punctures an assumption by *declaring* it ("Users
are consumers." / "The daze is the product."); the critique voice performs the same puncture but as a
question, the verdict withheld — because declaring the verdict is the very move it is pointing at in
the text. (3) **Stack and let sit** — two or three short questions in a row, left open: no "for
example", no reassurance, no hinted answer. The silence after the question is part of it. One sharp
question beats two padded ones.

This is bluntness in service of the work, not harshness for its own sake — and it does not relax
anything above: making the student do the judging *is* the help, not a withholding of it; the blunt
question helps *because* it forces that work. A blunt question is a sharper question, never a verdict.
(This bluntness is the critique voice's alone; the Socratic voice, which helps an idea *form*, keeps
its patience.)

## The discipline — and its own limit (the two-sidedness that keeps it honest)

Criticism has a characteristic failure, and it has to be named or the register curdles. When critique
becomes a reflex — every text assumed to be hiding domination, suspicion applied before the text has
been heard — it stops being inquiry and becomes a pose. Felski calls this the over-reach of
"suspicious reading," and the corrective is not to abandon critique but to keep it from being the
*only* move: to leave room for what a text does well, for recognition and attachment, not only
exposure. zetizeti's existing guards carry this directly: a critical question must still *go
somewhere* (direction), stay tethered to what the student is actually trying to do (continuity), and
never tip into word-theatre. The tool locates where description and judgement blur; it does **not**
declare that the blur is a mistake. The locating is the tool's; the verdict is the student's. Critique
that pierces everything must let the never-answer guard pierce it too — otherwise it is just a more
sophisticated way of depositing a conclusion.

## How this sits over the engine (behind the curtain)

The critical register supplies the *objective* and the *idiom*; it does not replace the machinery.
Clean Language still gives the question its form and keeps the student's own words intact. The SDC
locating (behind the curtain) still finds *where* description and judgement blur. The never-answer
guard still holds the line. Criticism is what those mechanics are *for* when the attention turns
outward — the aim and the language — not a second set of mechanics. The student experiences criticism;
the engine, unnamed, does the locating and the restraint.
