## Why localise

The bridge (`conflation-to-question-bridge.md`) gives the generic move: a value carried as a fact is
a smuggled verdict, worth a question. But the value-words that pass as description are
**discipline-specific** — "intuitive" naturalises a verdict in interaction design the way "authentic"
does in photography and "timeless" does in fashion. A question lands when it points at *that field's*
naturalised word, in the student's own discipline. This note localises the bridge to a handful of
disciplines, reusing the vocabulary already verified in Part B. It is a set of worked examples, not a
banned-word list: the same word is innocent in one place and a smuggled verdict in another, so the
word-lists below are prompts for a question *in context*, never triggers to fire on — see the limit at
the end.

## Worked examples (field → the naturalised word → the question)

- **Interaction / UX design.** Words that pass as description: *intuitive, clean, seamless,
  frictionless, usable, obvious.* What is naturalised: an aesthetic or fluency judgement dressed as a
  usability fact. There is a real, and contested, finding here — a-priori perceived aesthetics
  correlates with perceived usability (the "aesthetic-usability effect"; Tractinsky 1997), but the
  effect is contested and may even run the *other* way — Tuch et al. (2012) found usability shaping
  *perceived* beauty more than beauty improved *perceived* usability ("what is usable is beautiful"),
  and processing fluency may drive both (Preßler et al. 2023). So when a text calls an interface
  "intuitive," the verdict ("good, easy") rides on a word that sounds like a property. → *"This word
  — 'intuitive' — is it describing what the interface does, or approving of how it looks/feels?"* ·
  *"Intuitive for whom?"* (ties to the Part B entries `affordances-and-signifiers`,
  `recognition-rather-than-recall`).
- **Product / space / architecture.** Words: *form follows function, honest, minimal, timeless,
  efficient.* What is naturalised: a 20th-century value-preference (functionalism, anti-ornament) that
  Louis Sullivan first stated as a sweeping "law… that form ever follows function," which then hardened
  into a modernist mantra and was later re-read as "a statement taken too far" — historically
  contingent, not a neutral law. → *"Is this put as a law — and if so, whose preference became it?"* ·
  *"What does calling it 'honest' decide that you haven't decided?"*
- **Design-as-consumption (across fields).** Words: *good taste, premium, elevated, curated,
  must-have, iconic.* What *may* be naturalised: the marketplace's interest in more consumption, framed
  as quality — Papanek's *Design for the Real World* (1971) is the classic critique, naming "Good Taste"
  the offspring of forced obsolescence and aesthetics. But the same words can also name real
  differences in care, durability, or craft — which is exactly why it stays a question, not a verdict.
  → *"'Premium' — premium in what, and for whom?"* · *"If this is a 'must-have', who decided that, and
  who gains?"*
- **Photography.** Words: *authentic, candid, natural, decisive moment, raw.* What is naturalised: the
  constructed, chosen, framed image presented as an unmediated slice of the real — exactly Barthes's
  point that the image's cultural work hides as nature. → *"'Candid' — caught as it happened, or
  shaped to read that way? How would you tell?"* (ties to the verified Part B `photography.md` entries
  on the constructed image).
- **Sustainable fashion.** Words: *sustainable, ethical, conscious, responsible, timeless.* What is
  naturalised: a contested value-claim about a garment's whole life presented as a settled product
  property. → *"'Sustainable' across what — the fibre, the dye, the wage, the wear?"* · *"Settled by
  whom?"* (ties to the verified Part B `sustainable-fashion.md` entries).

## The limit (so this does not become a banned-word list)

The same word is honest description in one place and a smuggled verdict in another: "minimal" can name
a real property of a layout or hide a value-preference as a law. A list of "suspect words" applied
mechanically would be its own failure — critique reduced to word-policing, suspicion before the text
is heard (Felski). The discipline is unchanged: the tool may *point* at a word in context and ask what
it is doing; only the student, reading it in situ, can judge whether this instance describes or
decides. If a localised question does not move the student's own reading forward, it is not asked.

**Sources** (verified against the originals): Noam Tractinsky, "Aesthetics and apparent usability"
(*Proc. ACM SIGCHI*, 1997) — perceived aesthetics correlate with a-priori perceived usability;
Alexandre N. Tuch et al., "Is beautiful really usable?" (*Computers in Human Behavior*, 2012) — finds
the aesthetics→perceived-usability effect weak and conditional, complicating the maxim; Jan Preßler et al., "Statistically
Controlling for Processing Fluency Reduces the Aesthetic-Usability Effect" (*CHI EA*, 2023). Louis
Sullivan, "The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered" (1896) — "form ever follows function";
plus contemporary critique of its dogmatic over-extension. Victor Papanek, *Design for the Real World:
Human Ecology and Social Change* (1971) — critique of consumerist design and "Good Taste"; Alice
Twemlow, "Victor Papanek's Design Criticism for the Real World" (in *Victor Papanek: The Politics of
Design*, Vitra Design Museum, 2018) — reads the book as design criticism. Roland Barthes, *Mythologies*
(1957/1972) — the image's naturalising of the made. Rita Felski, *The Limits of Critique* (2015) — the
over-reach of suspicion. Discipline vocabulary reused from the project's verified Part B entries.
