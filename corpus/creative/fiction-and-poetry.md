## entry: does-the-story-make-them-kinder
**discipline:** fiction-and-poetry
**vocabulary:** story, novel, short story, fiction, reader, empathy, character, inner life, feel for, literary, genre, understand, minds, moral, message
**felt as:** i want readers to understand people like her; this story should make people more empathetic; i want them to feel what it's like; fiction can change how people see others; is genre fiction lesser; i want it to have a message; the reader should come out kinder.
**the_tension:** Kidd and Castano (2013) reported, across five experiments, that reading a short passage of literary fiction improved performance on tests of understanding other minds more than reading non-fiction, popular fiction or nothing. The claim travelled widely. A replication by three independent groups with 792 participants (Panero et al., 2016) found no advantage for literary fiction; what did predict performance was how much fiction a person had read over their life, which leaves open whether reading builds the capacity or people with it are drawn to reading. The original authors then argued that the replication's methods, not the hypothesis, explained the failure (Kidd & Castano, 2017), and the replicators replied that even the reanalysis did not reproduce the original pattern (Panero et al., 2017), so the question stands unsettled. Whatever the evidence settles, a writer faces a separate question: whether a story written to make its reader better begins to instruct. The counter is that a great deal of fiction has been written to change its readers, in protest novels and in the didactic traditions of many cultures, and some of it lasts. The writer's move is to ask what they want the reader to do, and what the reader is left to work out.
**failure_modes:** shaping a character to teach the reader something until the character stops being a person; assuming a story's goodness lies in its effect on the reader; dismissing genre fiction on the strength of a contested finding.
**questions_it_invites:** What do you want the reader to do with this character? · Where in the story does the reader have to work something out for themselves?
**sources:** Kidd & Castano, "Reading Literary Fiction Improves Theory of Mind," *Science* 342(6156), 377–380 (2013), doi:10.1126/science.1239918 · Panero, Weisberg, Black, Goldstein, Barnes, Brownell & Winner, "Does reading a single passage of literary fiction really improve theory of mind? An attempt at replication," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 111(5), e46–e54 (2016), doi:10.1037/pspa0000064 (correction: doi:10.1037/pspa0000067) · Kidd & Castano, "Panero et al. (2016): Failure to replicate methods caused the failure to replicate results," *JPSP* 112(3), e1–e4 (2017), doi:10.1037/pspa0000072 · Panero, Weisberg, Black, Goldstein, Barnes, Brownell & Winner, "No support for the claim that literary fiction uniquely and immediately improves theory of mind: A reply to Kidd and Castano's commentary on Panero et al. (2016)," *JPSP* 112(3), e5–e8 (2017), doi:10.1037/pspa0000079.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the original claim, the replication's null and its lifetime-reading finding are as stated in the abstracts; the methods dispute is now cited from its two abstracts (gate 2 had marked it NOT COVERED).

## entry: making-it-strange
**discipline:** fiction-and-poetry
**vocabulary:** poem, line, image, metaphor, style, strange, unusual, sound, rhythm, word, plain, clear, voice, striking, language
**felt as:** should i just say it plainly; is this line too weird; i want the language to do something; my writing feels flat; people won't get the metaphor; i'm trying to make it striking; maybe simpler is better.
**the_tension:** A long tradition, from Coleridge through the Russian Formalists, holds that literary language works by making the familiar strange: unusual sound, image or syntax slows the reader and renews perception. Miall and Kuiken (1994) tested this with readers of a short story and found that foregrounded passages, those with marked stylistic features, were read more slowly and rated more striking and more feeling-laden, regardless of the reader's literary training. But in a text-manipulation study with 37 readers, analysed mainly qualitatively, Kuzmičová and colleagues (2017) gave readers either Katherine Mansfield's "The Fly" or a version with its foregrounding reduced, and the plainer version drew significantly more explicitly empathic responses. In that one story, reducing the strangeness seems to have brought readers closer to the people in it, while Miall and Kuiken's readers felt more, not less, at the foregrounded passages; the two findings sit uneasily together, and with the contested claim, discussed in does-the-story-make-them-kinder, that literary fiction builds understanding of minds; the two studies also measured different things, feeling ratings in one and explicitly empathic responses in the other. The writer's move is to ask what this line's strangeness is for, and what it costs the reader's closeness to what the line is about.
**failure_modes:** flattening every line for clarity until nothing stops the reader; reaching for strangeness as decoration, so the reader notices the writer instead of the thing; assuming that what strikes a reader will also bring them closer to the people in it.
**questions_it_invites:** What do you want the reader to notice in this line — the words, or what the words are about? · If you said this plainly, what would change?
**sources:** Miall & Kuiken, "Foregrounding, defamiliarization, and affect: Response to literary stories," *Poetics* 22(5), 389–407 (1994), doi:10.1016/0304-422x(94)00011-5 · Kuzmičová, Mangen, Støle & Begnum, "Literature and readers' empathy: A qualitative text manipulation study," *Language and Literature* 26(2), 137–152 (2017), doi:10.1177/0963947017704729.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both findings as stated in the abstracts. Both studies use short stories; the entry extends the question to the line of a poem, which is the entry's reading, not the studies'.

## entry: lost-in-the-story
**discipline:** fiction-and-poetry
**vocabulary:** absorbed, immersed, lost, gripped, page-turner, world, believe, realistic, convincing, story, reader, pull, escape, vivid, false note
**felt as:** i want readers to get lost in it; it has to feel real; will they believe this happened; i want them to forget they're reading; is it gripping enough; does it ring false; the world has to be immersive.
**the_tension:** Green and Brock (2000) described *transportation*: being carried into a story through attention, imagery and feeling. Across four experiments, readers who were more transported came to hold beliefs more in line with the story and judged its protagonists more favourably, and in one experiment they also found fewer false notes in it; whether the story was labelled fact or fiction made little difference. That more absorbed readers found fewer false notes could mean absorption lowers the reader's guard, or that readers who notice fewer false notes become more absorbed; the study does not separate the two, and for a writer either reading cuts both ways. The counter is that some writing deliberately breaks absorption, to make the reader notice and think, and a reader kept at a distance is not a failure of craft. The writer's move is to ask whether they want this reader inside the story or standing beside it, and at which points.
**failure_modes:** counting on immersion to hide the story's weak points; breaking the spell by accident through a false detail; assuming a reader who stays outside the story has been lost.
**questions_it_invites:** Where in your story do you want the reader completely inside it — and is there anywhere you want them to step back? · What is the one detail most likely to ring false to someone who knows this world?
**sources:** Green & Brock, "The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 79(5), 701–721 (2000), doi:10.1037/0022-3514.79.5.701.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the four experiments and the false-notes and fact/fiction findings are as described in the article's summary (Consensus record); citation settled in Crossref.

## entry: the-sound-of-it-being-true
**discipline:** fiction-and-poetry
**vocabulary:** rhyme, rhythm, sound, line, verse, lyric, catchy, memorable, ring, music, couplet, meter, slogan, saying, true
**felt as:** should it rhyme; rhyme feels old-fashioned; the line sounds right but i don't know if it means anything; it's catchy; free verse or rhyme; i want it to stick in their head; it rings true.
**the_tension:** McGlone and Tofighbakhsh (2000) showed people unfamiliar sayings in their original rhyming form ("What sobriety conceals, alcohol reveals") or reworded without the rhyme ("What sobriety conceals, alcohol unmasks"). The rhyming versions were judged more accurate as descriptions of human behaviour, though the advantage shrank when people were warned to separate a saying's sound from its sense. Sound, on this evidence, can be taken for truth. For a poet or lyricist that is both power and hazard: a line can feel right because it sounds right, to a reader, and perhaps to the writer too, though the study tested only readers. The counter is that sound is part of what a poem means, not a disguise laid over it. The writer's move is to ask whether a line would still say what it says if it lost its music, and whether it should.
**failure_modes:** keeping a line for its sound without asking what it does in the poem; refusing rhyme and rhythm on principle and losing what sound can carry; trusting the feeling that a line is true because it rhymes.
**questions_it_invites:** If this line lost its rhyme, would it still say what you mean? · Which line do you like most for its sound — and what is it doing in the poem?
**sources:** McGlone & Tofighbakhsh, "Birds of a Feather Flock Conjointly (?): Rhyme as Reason in Aphorisms," *Psychological Science* 11(5), 424–428 (2000), doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00282.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — finding and the attenuation under caution as stated in the abstract. Aphorisms, not poems; the extension to poetry is the entry's.

## entry: writing-from-what-hurts
**discipline:** fiction-and-poetry
**vocabulary:** pain, grief, trauma, personal, my life, memoir, confessional, healing, therapy, catharsis, autobiographical, raw, wound, honest, depression
**felt as:** writing this helps me get through it; it's about my own life; i need to write about what happened; it's too raw; writing is my therapy; i'm afraid of what it'll bring up; confessional poetry.
**the_tension:** Research on expressive writing found that writing about difficult experiences could benefit health, and many writers describe their work as healing. Kaufman and Sexton (2006) point out the puzzle: professional poets show poorer health outcomes than other writers and the population at large. Among their possible explanations is that the benefit of writing seems to come through forming a narrative, which poetry often does not do; another is that poets may have been worse off to begin with, and worse still without writing. Part of that general claim has been tested directly: for depressive symptoms in physically healthy adults under stress, a meta-analysis of 39 randomised trials (Reinhold, Bürkner & Holling, 2018) found no significant long-term effect of brief expressive writing, though effects were larger with more sessions and a more specific topic. None of this says what anyone should write. The writer's move is to ask what they want this piece to do for the work, and whether that is the same as what they want it to do for them.
**failure_modes:** expecting a piece to heal and judging it by whether it did; deciding without noticing which of one's own material is off-limits; deciding in advance whether the material should stay raw or be shaped.
**questions_it_invites:** What do you want this piece to do — for you, and for whoever reads it? · What shape does this material seem to want: a story, a moment, something else?
**sources:** Kaufman & Sexton, "Why Doesn't the Writing Cure Help Poets?", *Review of General Psychology* 10(3), 268–282 (2006), doi:10.1037/1089-2680.10.3.268 · Reinhold, Bürkner & Holling, "Effects of expressive writing on depressive symptoms—A meta-analysis," *Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice* 25(1), e12224 (2018; online 23 Jan 2018), doi:10.1111/cpsp.12224.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in the abstracts; Kaufman & Sexton verified unretracted. ⚠️ A sensitive subject: the entry gives no advice about mental health and the questions concern the piece and what the writer wants from it, never their health.
