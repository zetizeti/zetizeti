## entry: surprise-and-the-expected
**discipline:** music-and-song
**vocabulary:** song, melody, chord, chorus, hook, tune, surprise, predictable, expected, catchy, change, verse, music, listener, progression
**felt as:** the chorus is too predictable; i want a twist in the melody; it sounds like every other song; is it catchy enough; the chord change feels wrong; i don't want it to be boring; people like what they know.
**the_tension:** Pleasure in music seems to depend on expectation. Gold and colleagues (2019) used a model of auditory expectation to measure how predictable and how uncertain music was and found that listeners liked music of intermediate complexity best, neither fully predictable nor fully random. Cheung and colleagues (2019) analysed some 80,000 chords from US Billboard pop songs and found two kinds of pleasure: a surprising chord was enjoyed most when the listener had been fairly sure what was coming, and an expected chord was enjoyed most when the listener had been unsure. Surprise, on this view, depends on what the song has led the listener to expect. The counter is that Cheung and colleagues modelled Western tonal harmony specifically, and music from other traditions organises expectation differently; a song can also be loved for its familiarity alone. The songwriter's move is to ask what their song has taught the listener to expect at this point, and whether the change they want lands against that.
**failure_modes:** adding twists everywhere until nothing can surprise because nothing was expected; keeping everything predictable for fear of losing the listener; borrowing a surprise from another song without building the expectation it depended on.
**questions_it_invites:** At this point in the song, what has the listener been led to expect? · Where is your song most certain of itself, and what would one change there do?
**sources:** Gold, Pearce, Mas-Herrero, Dagher & Zatorre, "Predictability and Uncertainty in the Pleasure of Music: A Reward for Learning?", *The Journal of Neuroscience* 39(47), 9397–9409 (2019; online 21 Oct 2019), doi:10.1523/jneurosci.0428-19.2019 · Cheung, Harrison, Meyer, Pearce, Haynes & Koelsch, "Uncertainty and Surprise Jointly Predict Musical Pleasure and Amygdala, Hippocampus, and Auditory Cortex Activity," *Current Biology* 29(23), 4084–4092.e4 (2019), doi:10.1016/j.cub.2019.09.067.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both findings as stated in the abstracts; the Western-tonal limit is stated by Cheung et al. only (gate 2 caught an earlier claim of it for both).

## entry: the-groove
**discipline:** music-and-song
**vocabulary:** groove, beat, rhythm, drums, syncopation, dance, move, swing, pocket, offbeat, funk, loop, feel, tight, complex
**felt as:** it doesn't make you want to move; the beat is too straight; i keep making the rhythm more complicated; does it groove; people should want to dance; the drums feel stiff; it's too busy.
**the_tension:** Witek and colleagues (2014) played listeners funk drum breaks with different amounts of syncopation and found an inverted U: medium syncopation made people want to move most and gave the most pleasure, more than either straight or very syncopated patterns, especially for people who enjoy dancing. The idea that groove lives at a middle level of complexity is now widely repeated. But Senn and colleagues (2024), using forty idiomatic popular-music drum patterns whose perceived complexity had been measured in an earlier study, rather than counting syncopation, found no effect of complexity on 179 listeners' urge to move. That does not test syncopation itself, but it questions whether the perceived complexity of a drum pattern is the lever. Their reading, drawn from a psychological model of groove rather than measured, is that simple patterns can make people dance because they are clear, complex ones because they are interesting. The songwriter's move is to ask what makes their rhythm move a body, and whether adding or removing complexity is really the lever.
**failure_modes:** adding rhythmic detail until the pulse is lost; keeping everything straight to stay safe; treating a "medium complexity" rule as a recipe.
**questions_it_invites:** What in this rhythm makes a body want to move — and what gets in its way? · If you took one layer out of the beat, what would happen to the feel?
**sources:** Witek, Clarke, Wallentin, Kringelbach & Vuust, "Syncopation, Body-Movement and Pleasure in Groove Music," *PLoS ONE* 9(4), e94446 (2014), doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0094446 · Senn, Hoesl, Bechtold, Kilchenmann, Jerjen & Witek, "Null effect of perceived drum pattern complexity on the experience of groove," *PLOS ONE* 19(11), e0311877 (2024), doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0311877.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in the abstracts. Western popular drum patterns; contested, written as contested.

## entry: hearing-it-again
**discipline:** music-and-song
**vocabulary:** repeat, repetition, chorus, loop, familiar, again, replay, earworm, grow on you, first listen, hook, refrain, same, return, listen
**felt as:** the chorus comes back too many times; it grows on you; people only like what they've heard before; is it too repetitive; i've listened to it so often i can't hear it anymore; it should hit on the first listen.
**the_tension:** Repetition runs through music, inside songs and in how people listen to them. Johnston (2016) played unfamiliar pieces to students repeatedly over five weeks; their liking rose, with no point of diminishing returns, and the author reports a possible transfer to other pieces in the same style. Madsen, Margulis, Simchy-Gross and Parra (2019) found a different pattern in how closely listeners' brain responses moved together, read as a sign of engagement: it fell across repeated hearings of music in a familiar style, while music in an unfamiliar style held it, especially for listeners with musical training. Taken together, the two suggest that liking and engagement may respond to repetition differently, depending on the style; they used different music and different measures, so this is a reading across them, not a finding of either. The songwriter's move is to ask what each return of a part is for, and whether the listener meets it as the same thing or as something changed by what came between.
**failure_modes:** repeating a hook until it stops meaning anything; refusing all repetition and giving the listener nothing to hold; judging a song after hearing it fifty times while making it, when a listener meets it fresh.
**questions_it_invites:** When this part comes back, what has changed for the listener since they last heard it? · How would someone hearing your song for the first time meet this chorus?
**sources:** Johnston, "The effect of repetition on preference ratings for select unfamiliar musical examples: Does preference transfer?", *Psychology of Music* 44(3), 514–526 (2016; online 31 Mar 2015), doi:10.1177/0305735615577248 · Madsen, Margulis, Simchy-Gross & Parra, "Music synchronizes brainwaves across listeners with strong effects of repetition, familiarity and training," *Scientific Reports* 9 (2019), doi:10.1038/s41598-019-40254-w.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in the abstracts; the engagement reading of inter-subject correlation is the Madsen authors' own, flagged by them as needing validation.
