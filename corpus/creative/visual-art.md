## entry: what-the-title-does
**discipline:** visual-art
**vocabulary:** title, untitled, name, caption, label, painting, abstract, meaning, viewer, read, describe, explain, wall text, series, image
**felt as:** what should i call it; i'd rather leave it untitled; the title gives too much away; people won't get it without a title; should the name explain it; the title is part of the work; i don't want to tell them what to see.
**the_tension:** Leder, Carbon and Ripsas (2006) showed people paintings with titles that either described them or offered an interpretation. With time to look, interpretive titles helped viewers understand abstract paintings, but did not make them like the paintings more; with only a second, a plain descriptive title helped more. Gerger and Leder (2015) found that both a title that fits the image and *no title at all* produced higher liking than a mismatched title, especially for abstract art, and read the untitled condition as asking more effort of the viewer. A title, on this evidence, can change what viewers understand, a mismatched one can lower how much they like the work, and Gerger and Leder read the untitled work not as a refusal to communicate but as a different demand on the viewer. Liking and understanding are not all a title can be for: some artists use a title that pulls against the image on purpose. The maker's move is to ask what they want the viewer to do in the first seconds of looking, and whether the title helps or does that work for them.
**failure_modes:** treating a title as a label when it changes what viewers understand; calling everything "Untitled" without deciding to; choosing a title that pulls against the image without meaning to.
**questions_it_invites:** What do you want a viewer to do in the first few seconds in front of this? · If the title were the only thing someone read about this work, what would it tell them to look for?
**sources:** Leder, Carbon & Ripsas, "Entitling art: Influence of title information on understanding and appreciation of paintings," *Acta Psychologica* 121(2), 176–198 (2006), doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2005.08.005 · Gerger & Leder, "Titles change the esthetic appreciations of paintings," *Frontiers in Human Neuroscience* 9, 464 (2015), doi:10.3389/fnhum.2015.00464.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in the abstracts.

## entry: where-it-will-be-seen
**discipline:** visual-art
**vocabulary:** gallery, museum, screen, online, instagram, phone, exhibition, wall, scale, size, in person, digital, reproduction, show, viewer
**felt as:** most people will only see it on their phone; does it work on instagram; it needs to be seen in person; the scale matters; it looks different on a screen; should i make it for the gallery or for online; people scroll past.
**the_tension:** Brieber, Nadal, Leder and Rosenberg (2014) sent two groups through the same exhibition, one in the museum and one viewing it in a laboratory. In the museum people liked the works more, found them more interesting and looked at them longer, and ambiguity drew longer looking in the museum and shorter looking in the laboratory. Darda and colleagues (2025), comparing museums with a screen, found the emotional and overall aesthetic impact broadly similar, but people gained more understanding from the works seen in the museum. Where a work is met can change how long people stay with it and what they take from it; the two studies compared different settings and measures, so they do not settle whether it changes how much people like the work. The counter is that work made for screens has its own ways of holding attention, and long looking is not the only kind worth making for. The maker's move is to ask where most people will actually meet this work, and whether it is made for that place or for another.
**failure_modes:** making for a wall the work will rarely hang on; flattening a work to read instantly on a phone without deciding to; assuming ambiguity will hold attention anywhere.
**questions_it_invites:** Where will most people actually see this — and for how long? · How long do you expect someone to look at this, and what happens in that time?
**sources:** Brieber, Nadal, Leder & Rosenberg, "Art in Time and Space: Context Modulates the Relation between Art Experience and Viewing Time," *PLoS ONE* 9(6), e99019 (2014), doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0099019 · Darda, Estrada Gonzalez, Christensen, Bobrow, Krimm, Nasim, Cardillo, Perthes & Chatterjee, "A comparison of art engagement in museums and through digital media," *Scientific Reports* 15, 8972 (2025), doi:10.1038/s41598-025-93630-0.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in the abstracts.

## entry: the-original-and-the-copy
**discipline:** visual-art
**vocabulary:** original, copy, print, edition, reproduction, hand-made, digital, authentic, fake, unique, by hand, touch, artist, generated, made
**felt as:** does it matter if it's a print; people want the original; is digital art less real; i didn't make it by hand; it's just a copy of what i did; should it be a one-off or an edition; what makes it mine.
**the_tension:** Why is an original valued more than a perfect copy? Newman and Bloom (2012), across five experiments using hypothetical scenarios, traced it to two lay beliefs: that the original is a unique creative act, a performance, and that it carries the maker's physical contact. Wolz and Carbon (2014) showed people the same paintings labelled either as originals or as copies; the label alone lowered judgements of quality, and even of the artist's talent. Value, on this evidence, sits partly in the story of how a thing was made and touched, not only in what it looks like. The counter is that prints, editions, photographs and digital work have their own histories of value, and these studies describe what people believe about originals, not whether they are right to value them. The maker's move is to ask what their audience will believe about how this was made, and whether the work depends on that belief.
**failure_modes:** hiding how a work was made because the method seems to lower its worth; leaning on the story of how it was made without deciding whether the work depends on it; assuming viewers judge only what they see.
**questions_it_invites:** What will a viewer believe about how this was made — and does the work need them to believe it? · If someone saw a perfect copy of this, what would they be missing?
**sources:** Newman & Bloom, "Art and authenticity: The importance of originals in judgments of value," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 141(3), 558–569 (2012), doi:10.1037/a0026035 · Wolz & Carbon, "What's Wrong with an Art Fake? Cognitive and Emotional Variables Influenced by Authenticity Status of Artworks," *Leonardo* 47(5), 467–473 (2014), doi:10.1162/leon_a_00869.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in the Consensus records; Newman & Bloom hypothetical scenarios, which the entry does not overstate.
