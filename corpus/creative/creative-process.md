> **Brainstorm corpus — cross-cutting lens.** Synthetic (original prose, written for zetizeti), retrieved per turn via FTS5 on the brainstorm surface only. ## entry: alone-or-together
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** brainstorm, group, team, together, alone, session, writing group, collaborate, ideas, bounce, partner, workshop, meeting, solo
**felt as:** i need people to bounce ideas off; we should brainstorm it together; i think better on my own; the group session went nowhere; everyone talked over each other; i wait for my turn and forget what i had; a group of writers would fix this.
**the_tension:** Group brainstorming was promoted on the claim that people produce more and better ideas together. Experiments say otherwise for idea *quantity*: Diehl and Stroebe (1987) found that people working alone and pooling their lists produced more ideas than the same number working as a group, and traced most of the loss to *production blocking*: in a group only one person speaks at a time, and ideas are lost while others hold the floor. The counter is that the studies measure listed ideas in short laboratory sessions, not the development of a shared project, and exchanging ideas can also stimulate new ones once the blocking is removed (Stroebe, Nijstad & Rietzschel, 2010). Hearing another person's idea is not the same as sitting in a meeting. The person's move is to ask what they want other people for — more ideas, or something else, such as pressure, a response, company — and when in the process that help belongs.
**failure_modes:** assuming a group session will produce more than time alone, and losing one's own thread while waiting to speak; refusing all company and never meeting a response that would change the idea; treating every kind of help from other people as the same thing.
**questions_it_invites:** What do you want other people for at this stage — more ideas, or something else? · What do other people's ideas do to yours while you are with them?
**sources:** Diehl & Stroebe, "Productivity loss in brainstorming groups: Toward the solution of a riddle," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 53(3), 497–509 (1987), doi:10.1037/0022-3514.53.3.497 · Stroebe, Nijstad & Rietzschel, "Beyond Productivity Loss in Brainstorming Groups," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 43, 157–203 (2010), doi:10.1016/s0065-2601(10)43004-x.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the nominal-group advantage and production blocking as its main cause are the 1987 paper's stated results (four experiments); the stimulation point is from the 2010 chapter's abstract. Laboratory sessions, which the entry says.

## entry: generating-and-choosing
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** choose, pick, select, best idea, shortlist, original, safe, feasible, options, list, many ideas, decide, keep, discard, wild
**felt as:** i have loads of ideas but which one; i always pick the safe one; the wild idea scares me; more ideas will help me decide; i can't tell which is best; i just go with what's doable; i have too many options.
**the_tension:** A common belief is that the way to a good idea is to have many and then pick. Rietzschel, Nijstad and Stroebe (2006) tested both halves. People who generated alone produced more ideas, and more original ones, than interacting groups; but when it came to choosing, neither did better than the other, and the choices were not significantly better than chance. Summing up this line of work, Stroebe, Nijstad and Rietzschel (2010) report that people prefer ideas that are feasible to those that are original. A larger and more original pool, on this evidence, did not by itself lead to better chosen ideas (past-the-first-ideas is about how good the pool of ideas gets, not about how well people choose from it). The counter is that choosing is a skill that can be practised and given criteria, and that a feasible idea is often the right one for the time and means available. The person's move is to ask what they are actually choosing by, and what that rule lets in and keeps out.
**failure_modes:** generating more and more ideas to put off a choice that has its own difficulty; always choosing the doable idea without noticing it is a rule; choosing the wildest idea whatever the time and means available; treating the first list as the decision.
**questions_it_invites:** When you choose between your ideas, what are you actually choosing by? · Which idea on your list are you most likely to drop, and why that one?
**sources:** Rietzschel, Nijstad & Stroebe, "Productivity is not enough: A comparison of interactive and nominal brainstorming groups on idea generation and selection," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 42(2), 244–251 (2006), doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2005.04.005 · Stroebe, Nijstad & Rietzschel, "Beyond Productivity Loss in Brainstorming Groups," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 43, 157–203 (2010), doi:10.1016/s0065-2601(10)43004-x.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — selection not significantly better than chance is in the 2006 abstract; the feasible-over-original preference is in the 2010 chapter's abstract (gate 2 caught the earlier misattribution to 2006). Lab tasks, not a creative work.

## entry: setting-it-aside
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** stuck, break, walk, sleep on it, step away, incubate, rest, distraction, come back, block, return, pause, shower, time off
**felt as:** i'm stuck and i keep staring at it; maybe i should leave it for a bit; it came to me in the shower; if i stop i'll lose momentum; i can't take a break now; i'll push through; i always get ideas when i'm doing something else.
**the_tension:** Many creators report ideas arriving after they stopped trying. Sio and Ormerod's meta-analysis (2009) found a real positive incubation effect: setting a problem aside before returning to it helped, and it helped open-ended tasks with many possible answers more than puzzles with one answer. Two details matter. The effect was larger after a longer period of working on the problem first, so incubation rewarded preparation rather than replacing it; and an undemanding activity during the break helped more than a demanding one (a later study of story writing found no difference between kinds of break; see drifting-or-directed). The counter is that the studies use short laboratory problems, and stepping away from the hardest part of a work can also leave that part untouched. The person's move is to ask whether they have worked on the thing long enough for a break to have something to work on, and what kind of break they are taking.
**failure_modes:** pushing on at the desk long after the work has stopped moving; stepping away before any real work has been done; filling the break with something so demanding that nothing else can surface.
**questions_it_invites:** How long have you worked on this before you got stuck — and what did you try? · What were you doing the last time an idea for this came to you?
**sources:** Sio & Ormerod, "Does incubation enhance problem solving? A meta-analytic review," *Psychological Bulletin* 135(1), 94–120 (2009), doi:10.1037/a0014212.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — positive effect, larger for divergent tasks, larger after longer preparation, low-demand breaks helping more are all stated in the abstract.

## entry: limits-help-or-hurt
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** constraint, limit, rule, budget, deadline, form, freedom, blank page, anything, restriction, brief, one location, word count, format, choice
**felt as:** i could do anything and that's the problem; the blank page; the budget is killing it; i hate having rules; give me a restriction; there are too many options; if i had more money it would be better; the brief is too tight.
**the_tension:** A common view is that creativity needs freedom and that limits get in its way. Acar, Tarakci and van Knippenberg (2019) reviewed work across management and organisational fields and found the evidence fragmented and conflicting: some constraints help, some hurt, through different routes. A later meta-analysis of 111 studies (Damadzic et al., 2022) found an overall positive relationship between constraints and creative performance, with large differences between studies; the authors conclude that how the studies were designed and measured explains those differences better than the type of constraint does. Writing about film and rock music, Murray Smith (2024) argues for a "sweet zone" between a disabling surplus of options and a stifling scarcity of them. The counter is real too: a deadline or a budget imposed from outside can simply flatten the work, and a chosen limit is not the same experience as an imposed one. The person's move is to ask which limits they are working within, and what each one is doing to the work.
**failure_modes:** waiting for more freedom, money or time before beginning; treating every limit as an enemy; collecting arbitrary rules as a game until they stand in for the work.
**questions_it_invites:** Which limits on this did you choose, and which were handed to you? · Which limit on this work is doing the most to shape it, for better or worse?
**sources:** Acar, Tarakci & van Knippenberg, "Creativity and Innovation Under Constraints: A Cross-Disciplinary Integrative Review," *Journal of Management* 45(1), 96–121 (2019; online 29 Oct 2018), doi:10.1177/0149206318805832 · Damadzic, Winchester, Medeiros & Griffith, "[Re]thinking outside the box: A meta-analysis of constraints and creative performance," *Journal of Organizational Behavior* 43(8), 1330–1357 (2022), doi:10.1002/job.2655 · Smith, "Artistic Freedom Realised," *European Journal of Analytic Philosophy* 20(2), 259–282 (2024), doi:10.31820/ejap.20.2.1.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — each finding as stated in its abstract. Contested literature, written as contested.

## entry: what-you-have-already-seen
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** reference, example, influence, inspiration, copy, original, seen, watched, mood board, moodboard, borrow, like, similar, derivative, references
**felt as:** i want it to be like that film; i keep watching references; it's starting to look like something else; i don't want to copy; i looked at loads of examples first; everything's been done; my idea is too close to what i saw.
**the_tension:** Smith, Ward and Schumacher (1993) asked people to invent new examples of a category after showing some of them examples first. Their inventions took on features of the examples, and telling them to make something very different did not reduce it: recent experience shaped what they made without their meaning it to. But a meta-analysis of 43 studies (Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan, 2015) complicates the warning. Examples did narrow the range of ideas, but the ideas produced were *more* novel, and the gains in novelty and quality were largest when fewer and less common examples were shown. Neither line comes from people making films or songs: one uses a short invention task, the other pools studies of design work. The person's move is to ask which references are in the work without their choosing, and whether they are drawing on many familiar ones or a few strange ones.
**failure_modes:** soaking in references until the work becomes an average of them; refusing to look at anything for fear of influence and reinventing the obvious; believing an instruction to "be different" is enough to escape what one has just seen.
**questions_it_invites:** Which of the things you have watched or read are already inside this idea? · Where did the references for this come from, and how many are there?
**sources:** Smith, Ward & Schumacher, "Constraining effects of examples in a creative generation task," *Memory & Cognition* 21(6), 837–845 (1993), doi:10.3758/bf03202751 · Sio, Kotovsky & Cagan, "Fixation or inspiration? A meta-analytic review of the role of examples on design processes," *Design Studies* 39, 70–99 (2015), doi:10.1016/j.destud.2015.04.004.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both findings as stated in the abstracts. The meta-analysis is of design studies; the entry says one line uses an invention task and the other pools design studies.

## entry: your-own-idea-in-your-own-eyes
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** my idea, judge, best, sure, confident, doubt, second, favourite, rank, worth, good enough, know, forecast, feedback, opinion
**felt as:** i know this is the one; i can't tell if it's any good; i love this idea too much; everyone else's ideas look better than mine; my second idea might be better; i'm too close to it; i need someone else to tell me.
**the_tension:** Justin Berg (2016) studied circus professionals predicting how audiences would receive new acts, and checked the predictions against more than 13,000 audience members. The creators were better than managers at forecasting the success of *other* people's new acts, but not of their own. In a later set of experiments (Berg, 2019), people ranked their rough initial ideas before developing them, and tended to under-rank the one with the most potential: the idea they thought second best often turned out best. Thinking about the ideas more abstractly helped them see it. The counter is that the person closest to an idea also holds knowledge nobody else has, and an outside opinion carries its own biases. The person's move is to ask what they can see in their own idea that nobody else can, and what being close to it might stop them seeing.
**failure_modes:** settling the ranking of rough ideas before any of them has been developed; handing the judgement wholly to someone else because one's own seems unreliable; mistaking attachment to an idea for evidence about it.
**questions_it_invites:** If someone else had brought you this idea, what would you say about it? · How did you rank your ideas — and how far had you developed them when you did?
**sources:** Berg, "Balancing on the Creative Highwire," *Administrative Science Quarterly* 61(3), 433–468 (2016; online 8 July 2016), doi:10.1177/0001839216642211 · Berg, "When silver is gold: Forecasting the potential creativity of initial ideas," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 154, 96–117 (2019), doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2019.08.004.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both findings as stated in the abstracts (Berg 2016: a field study of 339 circus professionals and a lab experiment). The title is given as Crossref records it.

## entry: past-the-first-ideas
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** first idea, obvious, cliché, more ideas, keep going, list, later, fresh, original, dry up, run out, time, first thought, push, again
**felt as:** my first idea is always the obvious one; i ran out of ideas after five minutes; everything i come up with is a cliché; should i go with my gut; the first thought is the best thought; i've stopped coming up with anything.
**the_tension:** One of the oldest findings in creativity research is the *serial order effect*: on an open idea task, later ideas tend to be better than earlier ones. Beaty and Silvia (2012) time-stamped every idea people gave in a ten-minute task and saw it clearly: the number of ideas was highest in the first minute and then fell sharply, while their rated creativity rose steeply over time and flattened slightly by the end. Because the effect shrank in people of higher fluid intelligence, the authors read it as strategy at work, the deliberate retrieval and reworking of what one knows, rather than ideas simply spreading to more distant associations. The counter is the long tradition of trusting the first impulse, "first thought, best thought", and the evidence comes from listing uses for objects, not from making a story or a song. The person's move is to ask how far past their first ideas they went before they stopped.
**failure_modes:** taking the first idea because it came easily; stopping as soon as ideas slow down, without seeing what comes after; generating for its own sake and never returning to the early impulse that mattered.
**questions_it_invites:** When did you stop adding ideas, and what made you stop then? · What came to you after your first ideas?
**sources:** Beaty & Silvia, "Why do ideas get more creative across time? An executive interpretation of the serial order effect in divergent thinking tasks," *Psychology of Aesthetics, Creativity, and the Arts* 6(4), 309–319 (2012), doi:10.1037/a0029171.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the time course as stated in the abstract (n = 133). Laboratory uses task, which the entry says.

## entry: when-it-feels-hard
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** give up, quit, hard, struggle, effort, persist, keep at it, slog, blocked, frustrated, nothing coming, stop, push through, difficult, tired
**felt as:** it's not flowing so maybe it's not meant to be; i've been at it for ages and nothing's coming; real ideas should come easily; i should give up on this one; it feels like a slog; if it were good it wouldn't be this hard.
**the_tension:** Lucas and Nordgren (2015), across seven studies, found, across a range of creative tasks, that people consistently underestimate how much more they will produce if they keep going on a creative task. The authors' explanation is the feeling itself: creative thinking feels difficult, and that difficulty is read as a sign that the well has run dry, when it often has not. When creative thinking felt difficult, people were also less willing to invest in an opportunity to persist. The counter is that not every struggle is productive, and knowing when to stop or set a thing aside is also a skill (see the entry on setting it aside). The person's move is to ask whether the difficulty they feel is evidence that the work has stopped, or only how working on it feels.
**failure_modes:** reading difficulty as a verdict on the idea; quitting just before the work turns; pressing on long after the work has stopped moving.
**questions_it_invites:** What tells you this has stopped moving? · The last time something felt this hard, what came after it?
**sources:** Lucas & Nordgren, "People underestimate the value of persistence for creative performance," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 109(2), 232–243 (2015), doi:10.1037/pspa0000030 · ⚠️ Correction to Lucas and Nordgren (2015), *JPSP* 110(4), 634 (2016), doi:10.1037/pspa0000051: misaligned headers and incorrect means and standard deviations in Table 2 from a production error. The notice does not address the conclusions.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — finding and its disfluency explanation as stated in the abstract; the correction is recorded in Crossref (`update-to`, 14 April 2016).

## entry: paid-for-it
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** commission, money, paid, prize, grant, client, fee, competition, deadline, reward, brief, for hire, contest, award, job
**felt as:** it's a commission so it has to please them; i'm only doing it for the prize; money kills it; i work better with a fee; it doesn't feel like mine when someone's paying; i have to deliver something by friday.
**the_tension:** The belief that rewards kill creativity has a long history. Byron and Khazanchi's meta-analysis (2012) of 60 studies found it depends on what the reward is for. Rewards given for being creative went with higher creative performance, more so when people also got positive, contingent, task-focused feedback about their work and had more choice and were less controlled by whoever set the reward. Rewards given simply for finishing, or for meeting a performance standard, had a slight negative effect. On this evidence, what a reward is given for, and how much latitude it leaves, matter to how creative the work is judged; whether paid work feels like one's own is a different question the studies do not measure. The counter is that a commission carries pressures, such as a client's taste, that the studies (experimental and non-experimental, with children and adults) do not reproduce. The person's move is to ask what exactly they are being rewarded for here, and where the choices are still theirs.
**failure_modes:** letting the terms of a commission decide what the work is about without noticing; delivering the safest version of what was asked for; refusing all paid work on the belief that money spoils it.
**questions_it_invites:** What exactly are you being paid or rewarded for — finishing, pleasing someone, or making something new? · Within this commission, which decisions are still yours?
**sources:** Byron & Khazanchi, "Rewards and creative performance: A meta-analytic test of theoretically derived hypotheses," *Psychological Bulletin* 138(4), 809–830 (2012), doi:10.1037/a0027652.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the contingency, feedback and choice moderators as stated in the abstract (60 studies, 69 samples).

## entry: drifting-or-directed
**discipline:** creative-process
**vocabulary:** daydream, mind wander, drift, chores, walk, doodle, thinking about it, on purpose, focus, wander, background, distracted, idle, routine, washing up
**felt as:** i get ideas when i'm doing the dishes; should i keep thinking about it on my break; i can't stop thinking about the script; my mind drifts when i walk; i try to force it and nothing comes; i only think of things when i'm not trying.
**the_tension:** Baird and colleagues (2012) gave people an idea task, then a break spent on a demanding task, an undemanding one, rest, or no break, then the task again. Only the undemanding task, the kind that lets the mind wander, substantially improved performance on the problems they had already seen; and the improvement went with more mind wandering, not with more deliberate thinking about the problems. McDaniel, Habibi and Kaplan (2025) took this to creative writing: people wrote two short stories with a break between, and while the kind of break made no difference, more mind wandering during it predicted greater improvement, measured by the semantic distance of the stories, among those who wrote to the same prompt after the break. The counter is that some ideas are found by thinking hard on purpose, and an idle mind can drift anywhere. Unlike Baird's study and the meta-analysis in setting-it-aside, the kind of break made no difference here, and in both studies the link to mind wandering is a correlation. The person's move is to ask whether the work ever gets time in the background, or only time when they are working on it directly.
**failure_modes:** trying to think the idea out by force during every spare moment; filling every gap with something absorbing so the mind never drifts; waiting for ideas to arrive without having worked on the thing first.
**questions_it_invites:** What were you doing the last time your mind drifted to this work? · Are you giving this idea any time when you are not trying to have it?
**sources:** Baird, Smallwood, Mrazek, Kam, Franklin & Schooler, "Inspired by Distraction," *Psychological Science* 23(10), 1117–1122 (2012; online 31 Aug 2012), doi:10.1177/0956797612446024 · McDaniel, Habibi & Kaplan, "Mind wandering during creative incubation predicts increases in creative performance in a writing task," *Scientific Reports* 15, 24629 (2025), doi:10.1038/s41598-025-09736-y.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in the abstracts; Baird et al. verified unretracted. McDaniel et al. is preregistered and correlational on the mind-wandering finding.
