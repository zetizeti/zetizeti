## entry: blueprint-or-text
**discipline:** screenwriting
**vocabulary:** script, screenplay, draft, page, scene, read, reader, blueprint, format, words, director, film, shoot, writing, literary
**felt as:** it's only a script, the film is the real thing; nobody reads scripts; i just need it to be shootable; should the script be good to read; the director will change it anyway; how much do i describe; is this writing or instructions.
**the_tension:** The common picture of a screenplay is a blueprint: a technical plan for a film, used up once the film exists. Kathryn Millard (2010) questions the metaphor, arguing that formatting conventions inherited from an earlier era can restrict what a screen idea becomes, and that images and sound may take a more significant part in screenwriting in a digital era. Ted Nannicelli (2011) argues against the view that a screenplay cannot be a work of art: like a play script it is a verbal object, a potential literary work, though he stops short of making the full positive case. Rosamund Davies (2019) offers a third picture, the screenplay as a *boundary object*, a shared thing around which people with different skills and interests can work together on one film. The counter to treating the script as literature is practical: a screenplay is also read by people who must build, cost and perform it, and prose written to be admired can get in their way. The writer's move is to ask who this page is written for, and what it has to do for each of them.
**failure_modes:** writing flat instructions because "it's only a blueprint", so nothing on the page carries feeling or voice; writing beautiful prose that nobody on set can act on; assuming one kind of reader when the script will pass through many hands.
**questions_it_invites:** Who is this page written for — a reader, a director, an actor, a producer — and what does each of them need from it? · If nobody ever shot this script, what would still be on the page?
**sources:** Millard, "After the typewriter: the screenplay in a digital era," *Journal of Screenwriting* 1(1), 11–25 (2010), doi:10.1386/josc.1.1.11/1 · Nannicelli, "Why Can't Screenplays Be Artworks?", *The Journal of Aesthetics and Art Criticism* 69(4), 405–414 (2011), doi:10.1111/j.1540-6245.2011.01484.x · Davies, "The screenplay as boundary object," *Journal of Screenwriting* 10(2), 149–164 (2019), doi:10.1386/josc.10.2.149_1.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** medium-high — Millard and Davies as stated in their abstracts; Nannicelli's position read through Exa highlights of the article's body, not the full text. Framing pending Prayas's sign-off.

## entry: structure-first-or-found
**discipline:** screenwriting
**vocabulary:** structure, act, three-act, plot point, beat, outline, midpoint, second act, formula, manual, plan, shape, sequence, turning point, story
**felt as:** where should the act break go; my second act sags; should i outline first or just write; do i have to follow the three acts; what page should the turning point be on; it doesn't fit the structure; the beat sheet says something should happen here.
**the_tension:** Since Syd Field's *Screenplay* (1979), the three-act structure with its plot points has become the dominant way of planning and analysing screenplays, and it is taught as if it were the shape of story itself. Matthias Brütsch (2015) tested how well the model actually works by comparing many published three-act analyses of the same films, and found surprisingly little agreement on where the acts divide: the core ideas are vague enough that structure becomes a matter of interpretation rather than measurement. If analysts cannot agree where the acts of finished films divide, the model may describe finished films less consistently than its users assume, and a writer pressing a draft into it may be following a shape the films themselves do not clearly have. The counter is that a shared vocabulary of beginnings, turns and ends is useful when a draft is lost, and that many writers find a plan frees them to write rather than trapping them. The writer's move is to ask whether the structure they are reaching for is coming out of this story, or being pressed onto it.
**failure_modes:** forcing a turn onto page 25 because a manual says so, whatever the story needs; refusing all planning and losing the draft in its own middle; mistaking a model built to describe finished films for a method that writes new ones.
**questions_it_invites:** What is this story doing at the point where the structure says something should happen? · If you took the act breaks away, what would change in how this story moves?
**sources:** Brütsch, "The three-act structure: Myth or magical formula?", *Journal of Screenwriting* 6(3), 301–326 (2015), doi:10.1386/josc.6.3.301_1.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the low-consensus finding and the vagueness argument are the article's stated result (abstract; verified unretracted). The counter is the common practitioner position, not attributed to a source.

## entry: who-the-audience-travels-with
**discipline:** screenwriting
**vocabulary:** character, protagonist, sympathy, likeable, viewer, audience, point of view, follow, root for, villain, hero, inside, perspective, alignment, care
**felt as:** does my main character need to be likeable; why would anyone care about her; i want them to root for him; the audience has to be on her side; whose story is this; we're with him the whole time; he's awful but i want them to stay with him.
**the_tension:** Writers are often told a protagonist must be likeable. Murray Smith's *Engaging Characters* replaces the everyday idea of "identifying" with a character by three levels of engagement. *Recognition* is perceiving a character as a character at all. *Alignment* concerns what the film lets us know: whose movements it stays with, and whose thoughts and feelings it opens to us. *Allegiance* concerns how the film asks us to judge a character, morally above all, and so where our sympathies fall. Because alignment and allegiance are different levels, a film can keep us with a character, following and knowing them, while inviting us to judge them; the second edition's afterword takes up such complex engagement with anti-heroic and villainous characters. The counter is that audiences do disengage from characters they cannot bear, and alignment alone does not guarantee that anyone keeps watching. The writer's move is to ask whether they want the audience *with* this character, *for* this character, or both, and whether those are the same decision here.
**failure_modes:** softening a character into blandness so nobody could object to them; assuming that showing everything from a character's side will make the audience approve of them; never deciding whose access the film gives and whose it withholds.
**questions_it_invites:** Do you want the audience to be with this character, or on their side — and are those the same thing here? · What does the audience get to know about this person that nobody else in the story knows?
**sources:** Smith, *Engaging Characters: Fiction, Emotion, and the Cinema* (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1995; 2nd edn, Oxford University Press, 2022, doi:10.1093/oso/9780198871071.001.0001), chapters "Engaging Characters" (doi:10.1093/oso/9780198871071.003.0004), "The Threshold of Legibility" (recognition, doi:10.1093/oso/9780198871071.003.0005), "Screens and Filters" (alignment, doi:10.1093/oso/9780198871071.003.0006), "Soot and Whitewash" (allegiance, doi:10.1093/oso/9780198871071.003.0007) and the Afterword (doi:10.1093/oso/9780198871071.003.0009).
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — every definition is taken from the 2022 edition's chapter abstracts (Crossref); gate 2 had marked the earlier, knowledge-written version NOT COVERED.

## entry: closed-or-open-ending
**discipline:** screenwriting
**vocabulary:** ending, end, climax, resolution, closure, open, ambiguous, final scene, last shot, answer, question, satisfying, finale, cliffhanger, unresolved
**felt as:** i don't know how it ends; should i tie everything up; i want to leave it open; is an ambiguous ending a cop-out; the ending feels too neat; people will hate it if nothing is resolved; what's the last image.
**the_tension:** For Noël Carroll (2007), closure is the sense that a story is finished, and it arrives once every question the story has made prominent has been answered. On this account closure is not simply the last scene; it is the feeling that the story's own questions have been settled, and an open ending is one that stops without giving that feeling. Krakowiak (2024), surveying 840 viewers about series finales, found that the closure viewers perceived went with more enjoyment and appreciation and less distress. The counter is long-standing in film and literature: an open ending can keep a story alive in the viewer after it stops, and a neat resolution can feel false to the material, especially when the story has been about something that does not resolve. The finale study is correlational and about long series, not single films. The writer's move is to ask which questions their story has actually raised, and which of those it owes an answer to.
**failure_modes:** leaving things open before deciding whether openness is what the story wants; tying up every thread until the ending answers questions nobody was asking; never listing what the story has promised the audience.
**questions_it_invites:** Which questions has your story made the audience ask — and which of those does it owe an answer? · What would be lost if the last scene answered everything?
**sources:** Carroll, "Narrative closure," *Philosophical Studies* 135(1), 1–15 (2007; online 30 May 2007), doi:10.1007/s11098-007-9097-9 · Krakowiak, "How It Ends: Exploring How Narrative Closure and Character Outcomes in Series Finales Relate to Viewer Responses and Coping Behaviors," *Mass Communication and Society* 27(5), 1028–1055 (2024; online 21 May 2024), doi:10.1080/15205436.2024.2344515.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — Carroll's definition and Krakowiak's survey finding are as stated in their abstracts; the survey is correlational, and the entry says so.

## entry: telling-them-the-ending
**discipline:** screenwriting
**vocabulary:** twist, reveal, spoiler, suspense, surprise, ending, mystery, secret, give away, plot, know, audience, guess, hook, payoff
**felt as:** the whole film depends on the twist; if they guess it it's ruined; should i reveal it early; i don't want to give it away; what if people already know how it ends; the suspense is everything; is it still good the second time.
**the_tension:** Writers guard their reveals on the belief that knowing the ending spoils a story. Leavitt and Christenfeld (2011) tested it in three experiments, each with stories from a different genre; in their follow-up (2013) they summarise the finding as spoilers seeming to *increase* enjoyment, and trace the benefit to a spoiled story being easier to process. The finding has not held cleanly: Levine, Betzner and Autry (2016), closely repeating the method with other stories, found that a spoiler given before the story reduced enjoyment, while one given midway made no difference. Beside that disagreement sits a question the studies do not settle but a writer can ask: whether a story's pull comes from not knowing *what* happens or from watching *how* it happens. The writer's move is to ask which of the two their script is built on, and what it would still give someone who knew the ending.
**failure_modes:** resting the whole script on one reveal, so it has nothing left once the reveal is known; withholding information the audience needs simply to keep a secret; giving away early what the story needs the audience not to know yet.
**questions_it_invites:** If someone knew how your story ends, what would they still want to watch it for? · Is the pull of this scene in what happens, or in how it happens?
**sources:** Leavitt & Christenfeld, "Story Spoilers Don't Spoil Stories," *Psychological Science* 22(9), 1152–1154 (2011; online 12 Aug 2011), doi:10.1177/0956797611417007 · Leavitt & Christenfeld, "The fluency of spoilers," *Scientific Study of Literature* 3(1), 93–104 (2013), doi:10.1075/ssol.3.1.09lea · Levine, Betzner & Autry, "The Effect of Spoilers on the Enjoyment of Short Stories," *Discourse Processes* 53(7), 513–531 (2016; online 22 Jan 2016), doi:10.1080/0163853x.2016.1141350.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — the design (three experiments, three genres) is from the 2011 report's opening, its result as the authors summarise it in the 2013 abstract (gate 2 marked the earlier genre list and result NOT COVERED); Levine et al. as in its abstract. Short stories, not films; contested, written as contested.

## entry: the-episode-ending
**discipline:** screenwriting
**vocabulary:** episode, series, season, web series, cliffhanger, finale, next episode, installment, serial, binge, hook, end of episode, pilot, arc, resolve
**felt as:** how do i end the episode; do i need a cliffhanger; will they come back for the next one; i want them to binge it; should each episode stand alone; ending on a cliffhanger feels cheap; the pilot has to hook them.
**the_tension:** Series writers are told to end an episode on a cliffhanger so that viewers come back. Schibler, Hahn and Green (2024) compared written stories ending on a cliffhanger with stories ending in a win for the hero or for the villain. Readers of the cliffhanger wanted the next instalment more than readers of a hero's win (in both studies) and of a villain's win (in the second), but they did not enjoy the story more, nor feel more suspense. On this evidence a cliffhanger does a specific job, pulling people forward, without raising enjoyment or suspense (closed-or-open-ending reports a survey in which perceived closure went with enjoyment; the two used different designs). The counter is that the study used short written stories rather than episodes, and an episode that resolves something can bring viewers back for the characters rather than the plot. The writer's move is to ask what this episode's ending is for: to finish something, or to open the next one, and whether it can do both.
**failure_modes:** ending every episode on a new danger until viewers stop believing it; resolving everything so neatly there is no reason to return; using the cliffhanger to cover an episode that did not hold on its own.
**questions_it_invites:** What does this episode finish, and what does it leave open? · If there were no next episode, would this ending still work?
**sources:** Schibler, Hahn & Green, "Investigating Responses to Narrative Cliffhangers Using Affective Disposition Theory," *Media Psychology* 27(1), 1–25 (2024; online 2 June 2023), doi:10.1080/15213269.2023.2219456.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — two experiments (N = 202, 273) as stated in the abstract; written narratives, which the entry says.

## entry: faithful-to-what
**discipline:** screenwriting
**vocabulary:** adaptation, adapt, novel, book, source, faithful, fidelity, original, change, based on, true to, rights, remake, reader, version
**felt as:** i'm adapting a novel; fans of the book will hate it if i change things; how faithful do i have to be; the book doesn't work as a film; can i cut that character; it's based on a true story; i want to make it my own.
**the_tension:** Adaptations are usually judged by how faithful they are to the book. Anne Furlong (2012) argues that this expectation misunderstands what an adaptation is: viewers hope to see their own reading of the book on screen and are disappointed, but an adaptation performs the filmmaker's interpretation, not the reader's, so some disappointment is built in. James Harold (2018), against the field's habit of dismissing fidelity altogether, separates two senses of it, fidelity to the story and fidelity to the themes, and argues that thematic fidelity is an aesthetic merit in an adaptation while story fidelity typically is not: keeping a book's events on screen is seldom an artistic accomplishment, whereas keeping its themes alive in a new medium is. Furlong asks whose reading an adaptation serves; Harold asks which kind of faithfulness counts. Together they leave the writer with *fidelity to what, and to whose reading*. The counter is that for many readers the events themselves are what they love, and an adaptation of a true story owes something to the real people in it that neither paper addresses. The writer's move is to ask what they are keeping faith with, and what they are willing to let change.
**failure_modes:** reproducing every scene of the book and losing what made it matter; changing everything and calling it interpretation when nothing of the source is left; trying to satisfy every reader's private version of the book.
**questions_it_invites:** What, in the book, are you keeping faith with — the story, a character, a feeling, an idea? · Which part of the book would you cut first, and what would the film gain?
**sources:** Furlong, "'It's not quite what I had in mind': Adaptation, faithfulness, and interpretation," *Journal of Literary Semantics* 41(2), 175–191 (2012), doi:10.1515/jls-2012-0011 · Harold, "The Value of Fidelity in Adaptation," *The British Journal of Aesthetics* 58(1), 89–100 (2018), doi:10.1093/aesthj/ayx041.
**provenance:** pending
**framing_confidence:** high — both as stated in their abstracts. Gate 2 caught an earlier version that gave Harold the position of Paisley Livingston, which Harold summarises before arguing his own.
